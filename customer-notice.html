GVR-02 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Risk communication · Governance

The warning changed. Did customers notice?

Recall updates in the news · June 2025

In June 2025, Anker expanded an earlier power-bank recall and asked people who had checked before to check again. Hong Kong reporting described affected local model and serial-number details. The communication problem is wider than batteries: when the scope changes, how do you reach people who remember the old message?

Your classroom decision

You lead customer communications for a fictional Hong Kong travel-cup brand. At 9am, three customers report that lids on model M10 leaked during normal use. All three cups are from batch A. Hot liquid could cause injury, although no injuries have been reported to your team. The cause is not yet confirmed.

The safety team has stopped all M10 sales and dispatches, started technical checks and contacted the relevant authority. It recommends an immediate stop-use warning for at least batch A; it cannot yet rule out batch B. You must send a customer notice now, not wait for the investigation. Management has authorized refunds for customers covered by the precautionary notice. The customer-service desk can identify batches and arrange those refunds. The model and batch code are printed on the cup base. Your next public update is due at 4pm today, even if checks remain incomplete.

Information available at 9am · fictional M10 records
BatchSoldLeak reports received
A2,0003
B2,0000

These are reports received so far, not results from testing every cup. No reports from batch B does not establish that B is safe. The company has direct contact details for some, but not all, buyers.

Your decision

Draft a short notice now. Is your precautionary stop-use message limited to batch A or extended to all M10 cups? State the uncertainty, what customers should do and when they will hear more.

Round 2 · One fact changes

At 11am, a technical check confirms a lid-fitting fault in a tested batch-A cup. Supplier records now show that batches A and B used lids from the same suspect production lot. The safety team extends the stop-use and refund advice to both batches. It has not established that every cup is faulty; no injuries have been reported to your team.

If your first notice focused on batch A, some batch-B owners may believe it did not apply to them. If you already warned all M10 owners, explain what the new evidence confirms. Sales remain stopped and the 4pm update commitment remains. All figures and technical findings in this cup scenario are fictional.

Your decision

Write the update’s first three sentences. Make the changed scope unmistakable, explain what changed, and tell any customers who thought the warning did not apply what to do now.

Debrief · Facts, uncertainty and action

Separate facts, uncertainty and action. At 9am, you know what customers reported and which batches they identified. You do not yet know the cause or full extent. A responsible notice can state that uncertainty while giving a concrete precaution. It should not say “only batch A is affected,” “batch B is safe” or “there is no risk” merely because B has no reports.

Choose scope deliberately, not to protect a headline. A model-wide precaution reaches potentially affected customers sooner but causes more disruption and refunds if B is later cleared. A narrower initial warning needs a clear explanation that B remains under investigation and a route for uncertain owners to obtain help. With a potential injury risk and unresolved scope, the broader precaution is easier to defend here; silence while waiting for certainty is not the alternative. Real decisions must follow competent safety assessment and applicable obligations.

An update must correct the earlier audience boundary. Lead with “The warning now includes batch B,” not a vague “further information.” Explain the shared suspect lid lot without claiming all units have failed. Repeat the model/batch identifier, stop-use action, refund route and next update time. If the first warning already covered all M10 cups, say that the wider scope is now supported by the new evidence, rather than pretending the action changed.

Put the revised notice wherever the original appeared, timestamp it and directly re-contact reachable customers. Ask retailers to display the same version; keep the earlier notice visibly marked as superseded rather than quietly changing it. Use the customer-service desk to help owners who cannot identify a batch. Communication is not the whole response: technical investigation, remedy delivery and appropriate escalation must continue.

Example update · Compare after drafting

Illustrative classroom update · 11am · after an initial batch-A notice

Our stop-use and refund notice for M10 travel cups now includes both batches A and B. A check found a lid-fitting fault in a batch-A cup, and supplier records show both batches used lids from the same suspect production lot; we have not established that every cup is faulty. Please stop using these cups, including if you previously thought your batch-B cup was outside the warning, and contact our customer-service desk for a refund or help identifying your batch.

Model and batch codes are on the cup base. We apologize for the uncertainty and disruption. Sales remain stopped, and we will update customers at 4pm today even if the investigation is continuing.

Back to the news · Scope matters

The real Anker notices distinguish models and serial numbers and explicitly ask people to recheck an expanded scope. They motivate the case’s update problem; they are not evidence about travel-cup lids. The earlier US notice and later regional notices have different scopes. They should not be combined into a single universal product list.

The lesson is to make changes and customer actions easy to find, not to invent a technical remedy. For an actual product issue, use the current official notice for that product and jurisdiction. The fictional notice is a classroom drafting exercise, not a real recall, technical safety instruction or legal compliance checklist.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–4: ask pairs to draft the first notice and underline its scope, uncertainty and action. Compare a broad precaution with a narrower notice that does not falsely clear B. Minutes 4–8: reveal the shared lid lot and ask for three new opening sentences. Minutes 8–12: ask a classmate who imagined owning batch B to explain what they would do after reading the update.

Assess clarity and evidence discipline, not reassuring tone alone. Reject invented injury counts, guarantees of safety and vague “contact us if concerned” wording that omits the required action. Ask how the team will reach people who will not revisit the original page. If students already warned both batches, reward that reasoning and require an honest update rather than manufacturing a reversal.

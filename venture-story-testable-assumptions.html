ENT-30 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Assumption dependencies · Research sequencing · Decision relevance

One venture story. Several testable assumptions?

A venture model is a set of linked claims

Discovery-driven planning and assumption mapping make the claims behind a venture explicit before treating projections as facts. A useful research sequence considers what a finding would change, what other claims depend on it and what evidence costs. The fictional map below extends these ideas into a small sequencing decision; it is not an estimated financial value-of-information model.

Your classroom decision

Two Hong Kong founders propose renting reusable board-game event kits to community organisers. Their model assumes customers collect and return kits at a partner venue during evening sessions. They have enough sample kits to investigate a small twenty-rental-per-month pilot, but have not validated paid demand, a continuing operating budget or venue access. No customer has been charged or promised service.

The founders have HK$2,000 solely for the next research sequence. Further development and an actual pilot need a later decision. A spends the budget investigating their least certain demand claim: whether the wider district could eventually support large monthly volume. B first checks the less uncertain delivery dependency: whether the proposed venue can actually host collection and returns under the model’s conditions. Friendly informal conversations make the founders optimistic about access, but no responsible venue operator has confirmed the arrangements.

Both founders want evidence, not a prettier pitch. A could reduce discomfort about scale before they develop more. B could expose a basic delivery problem before they investigate demand for an offer they cannot provide. The task is to choose the order of learning within the fixed budget, not design a randomised experiment, raise finance, sign a venue agreement or approve launch.

Current venture story: venue access enables evening collection and returns; that delivery promise supports customer demand and the cost model; those inputs support later scale projections. Informal enthusiasm is not confirmed operational access. The team ranks district-scale demand as highly uncertain and venue access as moderately uncertain; these are subjective labels, not probabilities.

Your decision

Would you spend first on A or B? Explain why uncertainty alone may not determine which assumption should be investigated first.

Round 2 · More information

The founders turn the story into a dependency map. F is permission and practical capacity to use this venue for the specified evening collection/return window, storage and responsibility arrangements. Without F, the advertised convenience and assumed venue-based handling cost are unsupported; demand evidence for that offer would need reinterpretation after redesign. D is whether enough organisers would make credible reservations at the stated terms to justify investigating a twenty-rental monthly pilot. G is whether district-scale demand might eventually support 150 or 300 monthly rentals. Either G outcome leaves the immediate next action unchanged: still verify F and D before further development.

The evidence-cost table gives F a HK$500 verification exercise with the responsible venue operator, including a walkthrough and recorded confirmation of scope and constraints. This is not signing a lease or proving durable access. D costs HK$1,500 for structured reservation research on the defined offer, with no payment or service commitment; stated interest alone is insufficient. G costs HK$2,000 for an exploratory district-demand study. The estimates include the research team’s time and incidental expenses. A finding may remain inconclusive, and there are no probabilities or expected-profit estimates.

F and D can therefore be investigated sequentially within HK$2,000. If F is unsupported or inconclusive, pause D and use no further research budget until the offer or evidence plan is revised; HK$1,500 remains unspent. If F is supported for the intended pilot scope, spend the remaining HK$1,500 on D. A positive F does not establish customer demand, and a positive D still does not establish profitability or long-term venue rights. The founders must assess operating economics and any real agreements separately before committing to a pilot.

Answer the first decision: B, not merely the biggest unknown

Choose B provisionally because an upstream delivery assumption can determine whether downstream demand questions describe a deliverable offer. High uncertainty is not the same as high immediate decision value. A might be appropriate if its result could change the present action, but an eventual scale range is not automatically relevant to the next small step. Check that B will generate useful evidence rather than another friendly conversation.

Answer the revealed decision: F, then D if warranted

Investigate F for HK$500. If the venue operator cannot support the defined access, or evidence remains inconclusive, pause demand research for that offer and redesign or clarify first. Do not label an inconclusive check a rejection of the entire venture. If F is supported, investigate D with the remaining HK$1,500. Defer G because both stated scale outcomes leave the immediate F-and-D gate unchanged. This is a defensible order under the supplied constraints, not a universal rule to test delivery before demand.

Trace what depends on what

Venue access affects the collection promise and handling-cost assumptions; those shape what customers are being asked to reserve and the relevance of a later scale forecast. A failed F does not mean there is no demand for any kit-rental service. It means demand for a redesigned offer must be reassessed. Likewise, passing F validates neither D nor the wider business model. Maintain separate evidence records for each claim rather than giving the whole story a single “validated” label.

Account for evidence and expenditure

F plus D costs HK$500 + HK$1,500 = HK$2,000. If F is unsupported or inconclusive, only HK$500 is spent and HK$1,500 remains available; it is not guaranteed economic benefit or expected savings. G alone exhausts the budget without resolving the immediate dependency. Research quality, scope and possible ambiguity matter alongside price. No expected value can be calculated without probabilities, payoffs and the reliability of each investigation.

Instructor note

Require learners to state what they would do differently after each result. This distinguishes decision-relevant learning from reducing anxiety about the forecast. Reservation research is evidence of intent, not paid conversion or a launch obligation. An operational confirmation is not a signed long-term right. Practitioner frameworks and a conceptual scholarly article support disciplined mapping, not a statistically optimal sequence or this fictional budget. The focus is dependencies and order, not experimental identification or financing milestones.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All founders, kits, venue, costs, volume ranges and evidence activities are fictional. HK$500+HK$1,500=HK$2,000; after F alone remaining HK$1,500. G costs HK$2,000, leaving zero for F/D. G’s 150 versus 300 monthly rentals concern later scale, not current paid demand or forecasts; either leaves the immediate action unchanged. Twenty monthly rentals defines the proposed pilot scope, not confirmed demand or profitability. Uncertainty rankings are qualitative and do not supply outcome probabilities. No expected monetary information value is calculated. F supports only the specified practical access scope and is not an executed legal right; D obtains no payment and creates no customer promise. Both still require later economics and agreement review before launch. Three Strategyzer pieces share authorship/framework and are not independent empirical evidence.

  1. McGrath: Business Models: A Discovery Driven Approach, 2010. Original scholarly article articulates assumptions, bounded checkpoints and learning before committing further resources; conceptual guidance rather than a validated test-order algorithm.
  2. Bland: How Assumptions Mapping Can Focus Teams, 2020. Original practitioner explanation maps evidence and importance across desirability, feasibility, viability and adaptability.
  3. Osterwalder: Start With the Most Critical Hypotheses, 2017. Original method guidance puts the hypothesis before the experiment and links learning back to the business model.
  4. Osterwalder: How Strong Is Your Innovation Evidence?, 2017. Original practitioner guidance distinguishes speed and strength of evidence; stated views differ from behaviour. No assurance of reliable sales forecasting is adopted.
  5. Blank: No Plan Survives First Contact With Customers, 2010. Author’s original account distinguishes a testable business model from treating projections as facts. Illustrative anecdote, not controlled evidence.

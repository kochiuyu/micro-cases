PPL-38 · Hong Kong and Europe · 15–20 minutes · Time-zone coordination · Meeting burden · Asynchronous collaboration

Same meeting. Different burden.

The invitation does not show the whole burden

Equal invitations can conceal unequal coordination costs. A distributed team must distinguish work that needs a live exchange from work that can proceed in writing, then account for preparation as well as meeting time. The offices, schedules and counts below are fictional, not evidence that one time-zone policy suits every team.

Your classroom decision

You lead fictional Harbour Studio’s six-person digital-exhibition team: three adult colleagues in Hong Kong and three in its central European office. For this six-week winter exercise, Hong Kong is UTC+8 and the European office UTC+1 throughout. There are no clock changes or holidays. The team is scheduling its next six weekly project reviews.

All work must stay within each person’s agreed paid schedule, rest requirements and local 08:00–18:00 outer boundary. Each colleague has voluntarily confirmed that either supplied slot can be arranged within those protections without extra weekly hours. Everyone prefers 09:00–17:00. The wider boundary is not a ten-hour shift or permission for unpaid availability.

Each proposed live review lasts thirty minutes and involves all six roles. Slot E starts at 09:00 in Europe and 16:00 in Hong Kong. Slot H starts at 10:00 in Europe and 17:00 in Hong Kong. F fixes all six reviews at E; R alternates three E and three H reviews, published in advance.

All six people attended each of the previous six E reviews. The manager takes that as evidence that a fixed slot works equally well for both offices. No one has yet checked preparation time or which upcoming agendas need live discussion. Both F and R are operationally possible; attendance is not a performance rating.

Accessible written updates and comments can be completed within each person’s paid schedule. Nobody must monitor messages outside it. For any unavailable live contributor, collect their input beforehand and allow a response before a decision closes. If essential exchange cannot be achieved, defer that item; do not treat silence as consent or require someone to break a protected boundary.

Round 2 · More information

Each live review requires every participant to spend thirty minutes immediately beforehand checking a fresh comparison file. The file becomes available only then under this fictional workflow. E therefore requires European preparation at 08:30–09:00; H requires Hong Kong attendance at 17:00–17:30. Each is thirty minutes outside that office’s preferred band, but within the agreed paid protections.

For the earlier six E reviews, all three European colleagues did that early preparation each time. Hong Kong preparation and attendance stayed within 09:00–17:00. Both offices report that these outside-preference periods are inconvenient, although feasible. No individual is asked to disclose private reasons. Count the stated minutes as a scheduling indicator, not a measure of personal distress.

The upcoming six agendas comprise four genuine joint-design decisions needing rapid questions between all six roles, plus two routine status updates. The team confirms the status updates can be completed in a shared document during preferred paid hours. Their full writing and review effort is funded; this change is not claimed to eliminate work.

For the four live decisions, two E and two H slots fit everyone’s confirmed availability. Publish them now, retain the written contribution route and replace the two status calls with asynchronous updates. Their response windows include a full paid working day for both offices before closure. No project deadline, mandatory check or employee protection is waived.

Your decision

Compare outside-preference person-hours for six fixed E reviews, six evenly rotated reviews, and the revised four-review rotation. Does rotation reduce the burden total by itself? Recommend a schedule and explain why equal counted minutes do not prove equal personal impact.

Debrief · Attendance is not comfort

Thirty-six attendances across six meetings show that six people appeared each time. They do not establish equal convenience, adequate preparation or a preferred schedule. The previous record omits European preparation before 09:00. Count necessary work surrounding the invitation, and ask about feasibility and preferences without turning personal explanations into personnel data.

The two offices are seven hours apart under the supplied winter offsets. E preparation is 08:30–09:00 in Europe and 15:30–16:00 in Hong Kong. H preparation is 09:30–10:00 in Europe and 16:30–17:00 in Hong Kong. Only E’s European preparation and H’s Hong Kong meeting extend outside the preferred band.

Debrief · Count people as well as minutes

For six E reviews, the European burden is 6 × 3 people × 0.5 hour = 9 person-hours; Hong Kong has zero. Each European colleague has three hours outside preference over the six reviews. These are paid hours already scheduled, not overtime, wage loss or nine extra hours per person.

With three E and three H reviews, Europe has 3 × 3 × 0.5 = 4.5 person-hours and Hong Kong also has 4.5. The total remains nine. Rotation redistributes the stated timing burden; it does not remove it. A changing slot also needs a clear calendar so people can plan.

Debrief · Remove avoidable live demand

Replacing two status calls leaves four necessary live reviews. With two E and two H, each office has 2 × 3 × 0.5 = 3 outside-preference person-hours, six in total. Compared with six rotated reviews, the counted burden falls by three person-hours. The asynchronous work remains paid work and is not assigned zero effort.

Recommend the revised four-review rotation plus two documented updates. Live discussion is justified by the stated joint-design need, not by habit. Written status updates can preserve information without requiring everyone at the same instant. A response deadline must actually accommodate both offices’ working days, not merely give everyone the same number of clock hours.

Debrief · Fairness is not identical arithmetic

Three person-hours per office is a useful distribution check, not proof of equal welfare. Individual circumstances, energy and task demands can differ. Retain private availability checks, voluntary agreed adjustments and a route to raise concerns without penalty. Never trade away rest or an individual’s protected boundary to make office totals look symmetrical.

Keep the agenda and decision record accessible. Invite written questions before the live exchange and capture reasons and unresolved issues afterward. If a person cannot attend, use the agreed input-and-response route; if that cannot satisfy an essential interaction, reschedule or defer the decision rather than pretending a recording equals participation.

Debrief · Review the design, not the people

After the cycle, review actual preparation, attendance and timing alongside whether decisions were informed and updates were understood. Ask whether the advance schedule and contribution route worked. Do not rank employees by early attendance, late replies or willingness to absorb inconvenience. Do not infer a causal productivity improvement from one six-week trial.

Recheck offsets, holidays, local protections and individual availability before reusing the calendar in another season. The fixed offsets here deliberately remove daylight-saving arithmetic. A global policy cannot assume that the same UTC slot produces the same local time throughout the year.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–5: compare fixed and rotating slots. Minutes 5–10: reveal preparation and classify the agendas. Minutes 10–15: calculate person-hours and propose the revised schedule. Ask students to distinguish reducing unnecessary live work from redistributing unavoidable inconvenience.

PPL-06 addresses physical overlap for a prototype; PPL-27 addresses roster notice and usable shift flexibility. Here the mechanism is repeated cross-time-zone burden and choosing live versus written coordination. All work remains paid and within protected hours; the exercise is not permission to change real employment terms or diagnose colleagues.

MKT-37 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Testimonial typicality · Overall advertising impression · Comprehension testing

A true success story. A typical result?

Literal truth is not the whole message

An authentic success story can still invite an unsupported inference about what most buyers should expect. Official advertising guidance distinguishes the truth of a testimonial from the impression of the advertisement as a whole. The tutoring service, learner outcomes and parent interviews below are fictional; no real child’s result or identity is used.

Your classroom decision

You manage a proposed campaign for fictional Hong Kong tutoring service ClearSteps. It offers a twelve-week mathematics course. The campaign targets parents considering the course, not children directly. Fees, teaching hours and admission rules are unchanged. Neither draft promises a guaranteed result, and neither has been published or cleared for release.

The service has complete start-and-end results for all 100 learners who enrolled in its latest course, including those who stopped attending. Everyone took comparable tests on the same five-grade scale under the same assessment protocol. These are observed grade changes, not estimates of the course’s causal effect or forecasts for a future intake.

A leads with one learner’s four-grade improvement and a vivid success-story layout. In smaller but readable text below, it says this was exceptional: two of the 100 learners improved four grades. It also states that outcomes vary and that the records do not establish what caused the changes. The story is accurate within the fictional records.

B leads with the complete distribution below, identifying the course period, all-enrollee denominator and assessment method. It presents the same warning against causal interpretation or guaranteed future results at the main display. Both drafts use only anonymous classroom material, with no real endorsement, image or personal record. All required permissions would be checked before any real campaign.

Each option costs HK$8,000 for production, revisions and comprehension checks before release. Only one principal asset is funded. The manager thinks A is easier to remember; the adviser prefers B’s population context. Neither supplies a conversion forecast. A misleading overall impression is a release blocker, not a permissible trade for more enquiries.

Round 2 · More information

A fictional pre-release study recruits forty adult parents considering tutoring and randomly assigns twenty to view A and twenty to view B on the same phone layout. Each sees only one draft. A neutral interviewer asks what change most enrolled learners experienced and what the advertisement establishes about the course causing those changes.

Fourteen of A’s twenty viewers say most learners improved four grades. Two of B’s twenty viewers give that answer. When asked to explain, several A viewers refer to the dominant success story rather than the smaller exception statement. The interviewer does not correct them until the comprehension responses have been recorded.

No other response counts are supplied, including answers about causation. The study is small and recruited for diagnosis, not a representative estimate of all parents. It compares the complete layouts, so it cannot isolate a pure effect of font size, storytelling or qualification wording. No enrolment or willingness-to-pay behaviour was measured.

The production budget includes one redesign and another comprehension check with fresh participants. A redesigned asset may place the full cohort distribution and limits prominently before any individual story, or omit the exceptional story. Both still require factual, permission and appropriate local compliance review; an encouraging small test cannot certify legal compliance.

Your decision

Would you keep the selected draft unchanged, revise it or reject it? Calculate the exceptional outcome’s share of the cohort and the two observed misunderstanding proportions. Specify a redesign and two neutral comprehension checks without claiming that B is proven effective or fully understood.

Debrief · A genuine example is not a typical outcome

The four-grade improvement occurred for 2/100 = 2% of the observed cohort. Seventy learners improved by at least one grade, but only two improved by four. The most frequent category was a one-grade improvement, at 40%. The median change is also one grade because the 50th and 51st observations fall in that category.

The grade categories are ordered, but equal educational distances between grades are not supplied. Do not turn them into an average learning gain or an effect size. Complete records avoid an omitted-dropout denominator here; they still do not create a comparison group, remove other influences or predict outcomes for new learners.

Debrief · Read the whole advertisement

A accurately identifies an exceptional result, but its tested presentation leaves a substantial comprehension problem. The story’s truth does not repair a misleading typicality inference. Nor does the absence of the word “guaranteed” prevent viewers from forming an unsupported expectation about what usually happens.

I would not release A unchanged. Develop the distribution-led B design, retaining its cohort, period, assessment description and limitations, then check it again. An individual story could remain secondary if it does not overwhelm that context. Do not invent a “typical learner” testimonial from the most frequent category.

Debrief · Interpret the test narrowly

The specified misunderstanding appears in 14/20 = 70% of A’s test group and 2/20 = 10% of B’s: a 60-percentage-point observed difference. Those are proportions in this small diagnostic exercise, not market-wide rates, a conversion increase or proof of statistical significance.

Eighteen B viewers did not give the particular four-grade answer. That does not show all eighteen understood the entire distribution, its denominator or the lack of causal evidence. Other answers were not reported. Random assignment supports comparing these layouts within the exercise, but does not separate their multiple design differences.

Debrief · Redesign for an accurate inference

Put the full distribution, all 100 enrolled learners and the specific twelve-week course beside the main message in readable type. State plainly that the records describe what happened, not how much the course caused or what a new learner will achieve. Keep any exceptional story subordinate to that information.

Use the complete data, including no improvement and decline. Do not replace the four-grade headline with “70% success” unless success is precisely defined and the overall claim is supportable. A retrospective improvement count is not a course success guarantee, and a parent may reasonably care about outcomes other than grade movement.

Debrief · Test understanding before attention

Ask fresh viewers, “What does this tell you about how results differed across the enrolled group?” Then ask, “What can you conclude about why those results changed?” Record their explanations before revealing answers. Ask them to locate the denominator and time period without pointing directly to the relevant text.

Check the actual mobile layout, not only a desktop proof. Revisit misunderstood wording and retain necessary limitations rather than shrinking them to preserve impact. Attention and enquiries can be evaluated after truthful communication is established; neither supplies a reason to publish a message that invites an unsupported expectation.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–4: choose the lead message and inspect the cohort. Minutes 4–8: reveal the comprehension responses. Minutes 8–13: calculate 2%, 70%, 10% and the observed gap. Minutes 13–18: redesign the main message and separate authenticity, typicality, causation and future prediction.

Treat both layouts as unapproved classroom drafts, not advertisements students should publish. No real learner, parent, course or testimonial is represented. Overseas guidance supplies conceptual comparisons, not Hong Kong legal clearance. A compliance boundary cannot be traded for sales, and a small usability study is not a legal ruling.

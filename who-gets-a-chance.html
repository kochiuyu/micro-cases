PPL-15 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Developmental assignments · Access to opportunity · Readiness and support

The reliable choice. The only person who gets a chance?

Industry context

HKEX’s 2025 annual report describes employee development programmes and the introduction of a competency framework to clarify core skills and leadership capabilities. It illustrates that development involves both opportunities and defined capabilities. It does not establish how a small firm should allocate a particular assignment.

The people, assignment and allocation record below are original fiction. No claim about unequal treatment at HKEX is made.

Your classroom decision

A fictional Hong Kong service company has one four-week assignment: coordinate a trial handoff process between two internal teams and present recommendations to the department head. It is a temporary learning opportunity, not a permanent promotion or a promise of a higher grade. It offers experience in coordinating colleagues and presenting work to senior management. The manager wants both a usable trial and a broader pool of people able to coordinate future work.

The lead receives six protected paid project hours each week, with routine duties covered, including two paid coaching hours weekly within that allocation. The sponsor reviews the plan before the trial, checks progress each week and approves the final recommendation. The trial uses non-critical internal work; the sponsor retains operational authority and can stop or revise it. Support, pay, workload cover and delivery standards are identical for either lead. There is only one funded lead slot; splitting it would not deliver the intended end-to-end experience.

Alex and Bo are the only two eligible colleagues who want this assignment. Both meet the published requirements: reliable routine delivery, knowledge of the handoff process and a satisfactory common planning exercise. Neither has a safety or performance restriction. Alex has delivered comparable coordination projects and volunteers quickly. Bo has not led one, but has handled the underlying work accurately and says they want to learn coordination. The common exercise confirms minimum readiness; it does not prove identical future performance.

Who would you select to lead this supervised assignment, Alex or Bo? Explain how you would balance evidence of delivery readiness with the assignment’s developmental purpose, without promising promotion.

Round 2 · More information

The manager checks the assignment log. Over the previous year Alex received four comparable lead assignments. Bo received none. For those four assignments, managers contacted Alex directly; Bo was never invited to express interest and was not told there was an application route. Records show Bo met the then-applicable readiness requirements and had available paid capacity at those times. There is no record of Bo declining. This establishes unequal access to those opportunities, not Alex’s lack of merit or proof that Bo would have performed equally well.

No assignment has yet been promised to either person. Both remain interested, and the sponsor confirms that the stated support can be delivered for either. The deadline and single-slot constraint are unchanged. Alex would still gain practice with a new audience; Bo would gain the first comparable lead experience. No protected characteristic is supplied or needed for the decision, and no unlawful discrimination finding can be made from this record alone. There is no approved date or budget for another lead assignment.

Your decision

Does the invitation history change your choice? Name the lead, the delivery safeguards you will retain, and one concrete change that gives qualified colleagues fair access to future opportunities without guaranteeing a turn.

Readiness is a gate, not the whole purpose

Both colleagues meet the defined entry requirements. Alex’s successful delivery is real evidence and should not be dismissed. Bo’s lack of lead experience leaves more uncertainty, but is not evidence of failed leadership. Because this assignment is explicitly supervised development as well as production, first-time learning and the future breadth of the team are relevant after the readiness gate has been met.

Ask how the track record was produced

Four previous opportunities helped Alex accumulate both experience and visible evidence. Bo’s zero reflects a record of not being invited, not four refusals or failed attempts. Repeatedly using “has done it before” as the only criterion can preserve that gap. The new information changes how confidently the manager can interpret lack of experience as lack of initiative. It does not erase the genuine skills Alex may have acquired or imply that every opportunity count should be equal.

Choose without turning development into an entitlement

Bo is a defensible choice here: minimum readiness is established, real support is funded, the trial is contained and first-time learning serves the team’s stated purpose. Alex remains defensible if the manager identifies a concrete delivery requirement that the published evidence does not adequately cover, rather than simply preferring familiarity. That reason must be examined against the same standards for both. Neither candidate is owed the slot solely because of a higher or lower past count. Do not invent a catastrophic deadline or silently remove the agreed supervision to force an answer.

Make the support observable

Keep the six protected hours, including two coaching hours each week. Across four weeks this is 24 project hours, eight of them coaching, not 32 hours. Approve the trial plan, use weekly checkpoints and keep operational authority with the sponsor. Assess the delivered handoff recommendation and what the lead learned; do not judge a first-time learner against an unstated expectation of unsupervised expert performance. Pause the trial if a checkpoint exposes an unmanageable delivery risk.

Change access, not just this appointment

Publish each suitable opportunity, its readiness criteria, available support and how to express interest to every eligible colleague. Record who was informed, considered, selected and supported, with job-relevant reasons. Review patterns over several assignments and help interested colleagues close actual readiness gaps. Recognise Alex’s previous contribution without treating this slot as a reward owed to the quickest volunteer. Give Bo a truthful explanation if not selected. Do not promise an unapproved future project, automatic rotation or promotion.

Instructor note

Take an initial vote using the readiness evidence and developmental purpose. Then reveal the invitation log and ask what zero prior assignments now means. Require students to retain both the single-slot constraint and the funded safeguards. Accept a reasoned choice, but challenge assumptions that experience is irrelevant, volunteering proves equal access, or an assignment is a guaranteed promotion. Finish with an observable allocation-process change, not just a promise to “be fair”. This is a fictional management discussion, not a decision about a real employee or a legal discrimination finding.

All employees, project records and resource figures are original fiction. This is a four-week supervised internal coordination trial, not promotion, unpaid extra work or a safety-critical autonomous assignment. Both eligible colleagues meet stated readiness requirements and want the work. Support and pay are equal; six weekly protected hours include two coaching hours, yielding 24 project hours including eight coaching hours. Routine coverage is funded.

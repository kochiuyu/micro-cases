GVR-27 · Hong Kong · fictional organisations · 15–20 minutes · Risk appetite · Aggregate exposure · Delegated limits

Within every limit. Outside the overall boundary?

An organisation-wide boundary needs an operating rule

The Financial Stability Board distinguishes aggregate risk appetite from limits applied to particular activities and organisational units. Basel guidance stresses complete, timely risk data across business lines. Those documents concern financial institutions; their aggregation principles inform this fictional non-financial example, not its legal duties or numerical limits.

HM Treasury’s Orange Book and audit-committee guidance connect risk appetite, consistent information and oversight. The lesson is not that every risk can be added as a simple number. The board below has deliberately defined one additive exposure measure for this exercise.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong community-events company has three business units. Its board permits at most HK$120,000 of outstanding non-refundable supplier advances across the whole company at any moment. This is an exposure ceiling, not a spending target or a forecast loss. All advances are measured gross, in the same currency and on the same basis. No insurance, netting, customer receipt or probability adjustment reduces this measure.

An advance enters the exposure register when the company becomes irrevocably committed, not merely when cash is paid. It leaves only when the supplier delivers the accepted service or an actual refund is verified. Existing obligations will be honoured. A separate routine rule permits a manager to approve a single optional project commitment up to HK$50,000, but this never overrides the board’s aggregate ceiling. Normal supplier, safety and value checks remain mandatory.

The board asks you to recommend an operating design. Separate sublimits would reserve HK$40,000 of outstanding exposure for each unit; changes between those allocations need central approval. A central pool would let units request capacity from the common HK$120,000 through a current register and a pre-commitment reservation gate. Both systems are funded and feasible. Neither gives a unit permission to use somebody else’s capacity without approval.

Sublimits give predictable local capacity but can leave another unit waiting while capacity is unused elsewhere. Pooling can match changing project demand but needs reliable updates, prioritisation and a staffed approval gate. Only the board can change the overall ceiling. No student recommendation changes that ceiling or commits a supplier.

Round 2 · More information

The current register has HK$10,000 outstanding in each unit, HK$30,000 in total. Each unit now proposes a separate, indivisible HK$40,000 advance for a different optional project with a different supplier. All routine checks have passed and each manager notes that HK$40,000 is below the HK$50,000 single-project limit. The projects were genuinely developed independently; nobody split a requirement or hid a commitment. None has yet been approved or reserved.

All three new commitments would become irrevocable today. Their cash transfers would occur on different days, but none of the existing or proposed exposures ends before all three are outstanding together. The existing HK$30,000 is expected to clear next month, not today. No early refund, reduced advance or rescheduled commitment has been offered.

The offers remain valid until tomorrow evening, and the board can review tomorrow. There is no emergency or permission to exceed the ceiling temporarily. All three projects have passed minimum value checks, but no comparative benefit figures or priority ranking are supplied. The central gate can process requests sequentially and reserve capacity immediately, preventing two managers from using the same unreserved balance.

Your decision

Can all three proposals proceed under the existing ceiling? Calculate the total and remaining capacity, then explain what your chosen control design should approve, hold or escalate without treating independent projects as evasion.

Answer the design question

Either design can implement the board’s ceiling. Three genuine HK$40,000 sublimits total HK$120,000 and provide local predictability if existing and reserved exposures count against each. A pool can accommodate unequal demand more flexibly, provided a central owner reserves capacity before commitment and reconciles the register. A daily dashboard alone is not a pre-commitment control.

Recommend the pool when varying demand justifies the coordination and that gate is dependable. Prefer sublimits when local autonomy and simple hard boundaries matter more. Neither requires allowing three HK$50,000 unit exposures simply because the transaction-level approval rule uses that number.

Apply the aggregate test

Existing exposure is 3 × HK$10,000 = HK$30,000. Three proposed advances add 3 × HK$40,000 = HK$120,000. Together they would be HK$150,000, exceeding the ceiling by HK$30,000. Current unreserved headroom is HK$90,000, not HK$120,000. Different payment dates do not remove the overlapping irrevocable commitments.

Under the pool, two advances would use HK$80,000 and bring total exposure to HK$110,000, leaving HK$10,000. That is insufficient for the third indivisible HK$40,000 proposal. Any two fit numerically, but the case provides no reason to prefer one pair. Do not invent an optimal ranking or automatically reward whoever submits first.

Do not confuse the two kinds of limit

Under separate HK$40,000 sublimits, each unit has only HK$30,000 headroom after its existing HK$10,000. Its new HK$40,000 advance would bring that unit to HK$50,000. None can be approved locally under those sublimits without central reallocation. A centrally approved 50/50/20 allocation could accommodate two proposals and preserve the HK$120,000 aggregate ceiling, if the governing delegation permits it.

The single-project HK$50,000 threshold answers who may consider an individual commitment; it does not create extra aggregate capacity. The issue is independent demands on one common boundary, not procurement splitting and not a statistical claim that losses are correlated.

Hold, prioritise and escalate before commitment

Hold unapproved commitments while obtaining an authorised priority decision. The board can choose two now, defer proposals, or explicitly reconsider the overall appetite tomorrow; an increase is not automatic. If it keeps HK$120,000, approving all three today is unavailable. Expected next-month release is not current headroom.

Record each commitment’s gross amount, unit, supplier, start, release condition, existing/reserved status and approver. Update and reserve in one controlled sequence, check the total including pending approved reservations, and release only on verified delivery or refund. Name the risk lead and backup, escalation route and breach response. If data are stale, pause new optional commitments rather than assume unused capacity.

Instructor note

Start by comparing two valid operating designs, not choosing between compliance and noncompliance. After the reveal, require both the HK$150,000 aggregate calculation and the different local-sublimit result. A student who recommends a pool must explain the reservation gate; a student favouring sublimits must explain controlled reallocation.

Unlike GVR-06, no requirement was fragmented and no evasion is alleged. Unlike FIN-16, no correlation, expected-loss or diversification calculation is needed. The measure here is explicitly additive gross outstanding advances. All limits, records, projects and delegations are invented; banking and public-sector sources supply concepts, not rules for this company. No real payment or board decision is made.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

Original fictional classroom design. The company, exposure metric, limits, proposals, dates and approval arrangements are invented. The ceiling is HK$120,000 gross outstanding irrevocable supplier advances, with no probability weighting, netting or insurance deduction. Existing exposure is HK$30,000; three HK$40,000 proposals would produce HK$150,000, HK$30,000 over the ceiling. Two proposals produce HK$110,000 and leave HK$10,000. Separate HK$40,000 sublimits leave each unit HK$30,000 after existing exposure; a new HK$40,000 proposal cannot be approved within that unit allocation. Payment timing does not remove overlapping commitments. All projects are genuinely independent. No comparative project ranking, emergency exception, future refund certainty or automatic ceiling increase is assumed.

  1. Financial Stability Board · Principles for an Effective Risk Appetite Framework · 18 November 2013. Final principles, especially section 3 on limits and section 4 on responsibility. Distinguishes institution-wide appetite and activity-level boundaries; financial-sector context only.
  2. HM Treasury · The Orange Book · updated 29 July 2026. Sections D3–D6 discuss consistent criteria, aggregation and evaluating risk against appetite. The case supplies a deliberately simpler additive measure.
  3. Basel Committee · Principles for effective risk data aggregation and risk reporting · January 2013. Principles 4 and 5 concern completeness and timeliness across the group. Used for register design, not to claim banking requirements apply to an events company.
  4. US Federal Reserve · Interagency Supervisory Guidance on Counterparty Credit Risk Management. Sections III.2 and V.1 address aggregation, meaningful limits, monitoring and escalation. Its warning that different risk measures aggregate differently supports specifying the classroom metric explicitly.
  5. HM Treasury · Audit and Risk Assurance Committee Handbook · updated 28 August 2025. Board appetite, cross-organisational oversight and Annex F questions on cumulative risk and timely controls inform the governance discussion. Not an approval framework imposed on the fictional firm.

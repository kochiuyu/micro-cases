OPS-12 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Inventory placement · Transfer time · Service feasibility

Two stores. One safety stock?

In the news

On 9 April 2024, DHL announced its Hong Kong West Service Center in Tuen Mun and described its transport connections.

A connected hub can support distribution, but that announcement does not establish instant store availability. The retailer, routes, prices and demand trace below are fictional.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong retailer is reviewing reserve stock of one identical boxed desk organiser at two shops. Regular stock handles ordinary planned sales; the reserve is for additional collection requests. Existing accepted customer orders remain protected. No new storage plan has been adopted.

A keeps eight reserve units at each shop. B pools twelve units in a shared stockroom, with scheduled transfers to either shop. The stock is interchangeable and usable; the shop system can reserve a unit without double allocation. The manager favours B’s smaller total reserve and lower monthly cost. The customer-service lead asks whether stock can reach a shop quickly enough.

The proposed reserve quantities are trial settings, not statistically calculated safety-stock requirements. The monthly package figures include all costs that differ in this comparison: holding and storage charges, stock handling and scheduled transfers. Purchases replacing sold units and other business costs are common and excluded. Neither package includes emergency transport.

The retailer wants to retain its one-hour collection offer for eligible additional requests, subject to confirmed availability. Customer requests must be checked before a promise is accepted. No customer can collect directly from the stockroom. Compare the plans provisionally; the detailed transfer timetable and a worked demand trace follow.

Round 2 · More information

Use this fixed classroom trace, not a demand forecast. At 9:00, ten one-unit requests arrive: at each shop, two customers need collection by 10:00 and three others request collection by 16:00. Each plan starts with its full reserve; there are no other reserve requests or replenishments that day. The regular sales stock is fully committed and cannot be borrowed.

The stockroom’s first scheduled delivery makes units ready for collection at either shop at 11:00, including picking, travel, unloading and checking. The second makes them ready at 15:00. Requests placed at 9:00 qualify for either run. Each run can carry all required units. There is no faster inter-shop transfer, paid express option or customer travel substitution in this comparison.

A third fully costed option, C, holds two units at each shop and eight centrally: twelve in total, for HK$1,400 per month. Local units are ready at 9:00 under A and C. The same central timetable applies under B and C. Safe storage and handling requirements are unchanged.

The managers can choose a cautious trial of C, retain A, or use B only with a clearly revised offer for future requests, never a hidden delay to an accepted promise. The exercise asks what each plan can meet in this trace. It does not establish a long-run service percentage.

Your decision

How many of the ten requests can each plan meet by its requested time? Which plan would you trial, and what would you tell a customer before accepting a one-hour collection request?

Count stock at the required place and time

A has five requests at each shop and eight local units, so it can meet all ten requested times. B has enough units for ten requests across the network, but none reaches either shop before 11:00. It therefore misses the four 10:00 requests and can meet the six 16:00 requests.

That is six of ten on time in this trace, not a predicted 60% long-run service level. The four early units are physically available centrally; the failure is their location and readiness time, not a shortage across the network.

A small local reserve changes the feasible promise

C uses its two local units at each shop for the four early requests. Six of its eight central units reach the shops by 15:00, in time for the six later requests. It meets all ten in this trace and retains two central units afterward. A retains six units across the shops.

Do not assign the four local units to later requests first and then claim C cannot serve the early ones. The system knows all requested times at 9:00 and can reserve accordingly. No item or capacity is counted twice.

Compare cost without inventing protection

B’s monthly package is HK$400 below A’s; C is HK$200 below A and HK$200 above B. C retains B’s twelve-unit total while positioning four near customers. These are quoted comparison differences, not guaranteed total-business savings or a universal pooling percentage.

C is a sensible trial if preserving fast access while reducing reserve footprint is the aim. A remains defensible if the business wants a larger local cushion while gathering evidence. B can support a deliberately later future offer, but not the same early promise in the supplied trace. Customer willingness to accept that change is unknown.

Test more than one convenient day

Before expanding C, inspect request times and quantities by shop, transfer reliability, reservation accuracy and cases where local reserves run out before a delivery. A day with three early requests at one shop would exceed C’s local two-unit reserve; the current trace does not rule that out.

Confirm a ready-at time before promising collection. If an item cannot arrive in time, offer a clearly stated later collection or decline that promise. Record on-time requests and reasons for misses, rather than only total units held. Replenishment between days also needs a plan; no multi-day service guarantee is supplied here.

Instructor note

Allow 3 minutes for the initial pooling choice, 4 to draw a 9:00/10:00/11:00/15:00/16:00 timeline, and 5 for comparing A, B and C. Keep stock totals and ready-at times in separate columns. Ask why twelve central units cannot meet four early one-unit requests.

The reveal changes knowledge and adds a feasible hybrid, not the demand correlation or upstream supplier. There is no square-root rule calculation. Trial stock levels are not optimal safety stocks. A offers extra local cushion at a higher cost; do not manufacture evidence that C guarantees the same future service. Protect existing promises and safety rather than solving the exercise by quietly changing a customer deadline.

SUS-32 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Recycling quality · Load acceptance · Useful material recovery

A clean recycling stream. One contaminated load?

Quality across the recycling chain

Hong Kong’s GREEN@COMMUNITY guidance distinguishes accepted materials, preparation and downstream treatment. Overseas public guidance also explains that contamination can affect more than the offending item. The estate and processing contract below are fictional; their numerical threshold is not a Hong Kong public-collection rule.

Your classroom decision

You chair fictional Harbour Court’s estate committee. Choose next week’s recycling policy: A keeps the current list of clean rigid plastic containers; B adds specified clean plastic food trays accepted by the contracted processor. Both materials have a confirmed outlet when they meet its quality conditions. This is not a search for a missing recycling destination.

A planning exercise uses the same 1,200 kg of non-hazardous discarded material under either policy. A sends 1,000 kg to the shared recycling collection and 200 kg to residual disposal. B sends all 1,200 kg to that collection. Waste generation and resident participation are held fixed for this comparison; these are fictional planning inputs, not observed future results.

If its load is accepted, A’s all-in estate cost is HK$1,000 and B’s is HK$1,200. These include collection, routine sorting, processing and all residual disposal for the stated material. The one-week ceiling is HK$1,800. No recycling rebate applies. A rejected load incurs an additional HK$500 handling charge under either policy.

The processor accepts loads only when its contamination condition is met; the exact grading rule must be read before authorising collection. Accepted target plastic produces useful secondary plastic at a stipulated 90% mass yield after contaminants are removed. Remaining mass follows lawful residual disposal. This fixed yield is a teaching assumption, not a performance claim about real recyclers.

Round 2 · More information

The fictional contract grades each entire delivered load by mass. Contamination must be at most 5% of delivered mass, including the boundary exactly. Above 5%, the entire load is rejected before routine sorting: no target plastic is recovered from it under this contract. No alternative receiver or rescue sorting is included.

Composition checks for this classroom scenario are treated as exact: A’s 1,000 kg contains 980 kg of accepted target plastic and 20 kg of nonaccepted material. B’s 1,200 kg contains 1,120 kg of target plastic and 80 kg of nonaccepted material. The 200 kg added therefore comprises 140 kg of target plastic and 60 kg of contamination.

Clean qualifying trays are target material, not contamination. Here nonaccepted material means food residue and nonqualifying packaging deposited with the collection. B’s greater contamination is a stipulated outcome of these arrangements, not a claim that trays always cause rejection or that residents cannot learn to sort them.

A third quoted option, C, retains B’s broader list and adds trained, paid pre-sorting before delivery for HK$600. For this exercise it removes exactly 60 kg of contaminants, loses no target plastic and safely disposes of the removed material. C therefore delivers 1,140 kg. Its all-in cost, if accepted, is HK$1,800.

C’s removal performance and fixed quotation are confirmed assumptions for this one-week comparison, not guarantees for future weeks. Routine sorting after acceptance is already included in the base costs; it cannot rescue B before the contract’s acceptance test. Residents are not asked to handle hazardous material or provide unpaid sorting labour.

Your decision

Choose A, unchanged B or funded C. Calculate each delivered load’s contamination percentage, useful secondary-plastic output and total cost. Explain what additional output C buys and why none of these figures proves the lowest overall environmental impact.

Debrief · Test the whole load

A: 20 ÷ 1,000 = 2%, so the load passes. B: 80 ÷ 1,200 ≈ 6.67%, so the entire load fails. C: 20 ÷ 1,140 ≈ 1.75%, so it passes. Use delivered mass after any pre-sorting as the denominator, not the target-plastic mass.

This is a threshold, not a proportional penalty. Under B, recovering 90% of 1,120 kg would be 1,008 kg only if acceptance were achieved. The stipulated contract instead yields zero secondary plastic from the rejected load. Real contracts can permit other responses; this case does not say every contaminated load everywhere is discarded.

Debrief · Follow usable output

A produces 980 × 90% = 882 kg of secondary plastic. C produces 1,120 × 90% = 1,008 kg. B produces zero. C gains 126 kg over A, not the full 200 kg extra placed in collection. Contaminants and processing losses are not useful recovered material.

The complete 1,200 kg balance is consistent. A leaves 318 kg residual: 200 outside collection, 20 contaminants and 98 processing loss. B sends all 1,200 kg to residual disposal. C leaves 192 kg residual: 60 removed before delivery, 20 removed afterwards and 112 processing loss. No mass disappears.

Debrief · A funded policy choice

A costs HK$1,000. Rejected B costs HK$1,200 + HK$500 = HK$1,700. Accepted C costs HK$1,200 + HK$600 = HK$1,800, with no rejection charge. All fit the ceiling. Under the supplied comparison, unchanged B costs more and produces less secondary plastic than A.

C buys 126 kg additional secondary plastic for HK$800 more than A: HK$800 ÷ 126 ≈ HK$6.35 per additional kilogram. This is an incremental estate cost, not a carbon-abatement cost or the market value of plastic. No environmental value per kilogram is supplied.

Choose C if the committee values the extra useful material enough to spend the additional HK$800 and accepts the stated performance conditions. Choose A if preserving that budget is the priority. Neither is a uniquely calculated optimum. Retain the narrow list until an affordable, verified improvement can meet the quality condition; do not select B for its collection headline.

Debrief · Check quality before scaling

For later weeks, measure composition and recovery rather than assume these exact masses repeat. Verify safe pre-sorting capability, worker time, acceptance grading, rejection charges and downstream output records. Clear instructions, separate collection or a different contract might help, but none is a free, proven substitute in this exercise.

Report collection, accepted inputs, recovered secondary material and residual disposal separately. Greater useful recovery is not automatically lower total environmental impact: transport, sorting energy, water, material quality and actual substitution for virgin plastic also matter. Consider prevention and reuse for future procurement without pretending they change material already discarded in this fixed-week exercise.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–4: choose provisionally and identify missing acceptance information. Minutes 4–9: reveal composition and the load threshold, then calculate pass or fail. Minutes 9–14: reconcile output, residual mass and cost. Minutes 14–18: compare A with C and draft a claim that says useful output rather than merely kilograms collected.

SUS-02 reconciles waste streams without a processor acceptance threshold. SUS-11 asks whether a certified material has a booked treatment route. Here both target plastics have an outlet; the nonlinear load-quality condition determines whether usable material reaches it. Do not infer a Hong Kong statutory threshold, blame residents or assume a sign alone guarantees compliance.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All estate quantities, costs, contract terms and sorting outcomes are fictional. The at-most-5% contamination threshold is a stipulated mass-only whole-load rule, not a Hong Kong legal or public-scheme standard. Accepted target-plastic mass yields 90% secondary material; the remaining 10% is residual processing loss. Rejected loads receive no material recovery under this specific contract. All costs include the stated handling and lawful disposal with no rebate; C’s additional sorting cost includes safe paid work. Exact composition and removal performance are accepted for the one-week classroom calculation, not forecasts for real estates. No comparative life-cycle impact or financial value of recovered material is provided.

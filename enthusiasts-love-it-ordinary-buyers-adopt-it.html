ENT-31 · Fictional Hong Kong venture · 12–15 minutes · Early adopters · Complete offering · User adaptation

Enthusiasts love it. Can ordinary buyers adopt it?

Industry context: the user may complete the product

Geoffrey Moore’s account of moving beyond early adopters distinguishes customers willing to help realise a new product from those who expect a dependable, complete offering. Eric von Hippel’s research also shows that users can develop or adapt products for their own needs. Neither idea makes enthusiastic users unrepresentative in every market or proves that a venture should abandon them.

The Hong Kong gardening venture, customers, observations and amounts below are fictional. This is a teaching application of those ideas, not a report of a real company or evidence that Hong Kong households behave alike.

Your classroom decision

You are a co-founder of a Hong Kong venture selling a modular countertop herb-growing kit. Twenty hobby gardeners have bought the current kit at its normal advertised price. Eighteen report successfully setting it up and continuing to use it after one month. Eleven have asked for an advanced module that allows more arrangements of growing trays. These are requests, not orders for an upgrade.

You have HK$18,000 and eight weeks for one development cycle. A develops the advanced module for experienced gardeners. B develops a ready-to-start package: a preassembled standard tray, clearer setup guidance and a defined support service. Each plan uses the entire development budget, including its own prototype evaluation. You cannot complete both in this cycle.

The founders want to explore selling to households with little gardening-kit experience, but are willing to remain a specialist business if broader adoption is not viable. Existing customers retain the promised support under a separate funded budget. Both paths preserve the same safety, accessibility and consumer protections. No new sales promises have been made.

Evidence or choiceWhat is known now
Current paid customers20 hobby gardeners; 18 using the kit after one month

A: advanced moduleHK$18,000; eight weeks; builds on the current kit
B: ready-to-start packageHK$18,000; eight weeks; includes prototype support testing

These observations do not estimate market size, profit or future conversion. Neither option is a full-scale launch.

Your decision

Would you fund the advanced module or the ready-to-start package for the next eight weeks? Which evidence matters most?

Round 2 · More information

The team checks what happened before the first customers began using the kit. Of the eighteen continuing users, fourteen had adapted the tray arrangement or created their own setup checklist using prior experience. These changes may have supplied part of the solution, or simply reflected personal preferences. Their necessity has not been established. The remaining four used the supplied arrangement and guidance. No unsafe modification is involved.

Separately, twelve consenting adults with little kit experience try the current package in a supervised usability session. Four complete the ordinary setup task without coaching; eight need help choosing the initial arrangement and interpreting the sequence. All receive assistance before leaving. They were deliberately recruited for this task, not randomly sampled from Hong Kong households. Their gardening experience is not a proxy for age, education, disability or general ability.

No one has yet tested package B. The observations identify an adoption barrier in the current offer, not the size of a mainstream market or the effect of the proposed redesign. A future support service must have a deliverable scope and price; founder help cannot silently become an unlimited free promise.

Your decision

After seeing how the first customers succeeded, which development path would you choose, and what would you need before claiming it is ready for less experienced households?

What the early success establishes

The paid customers demonstrate a real use for the current kit among these hobby gardeners. Their adaptations are valuable information, not evidence of failure or a reason to ignore them. But eighteen continuing users do not show that the supplied package alone enabled eighteen independent setups. Their adaptations may have helped them succeed, but the observations do not establish what would have happened without those changes.

The later session points toward a missing setup pathway for the intended new audience. It does not prove that those households will pay, keep using the kit or prefer B. The early buyers are not automatically formal “lead users” merely because they bought first or adapted a tray; that research concept has a more specific meaning.

Choose the next business, not the most features

B is the stronger development choice if the founders keep their goal of exploring less experienced households: its work addresses the observed barrier. A remains defensible if they deliberately choose a specialist niche and investigate whether the requested module can become a viable paid offer. Selecting A while claiming the same evidence proves broad readiness is the inconsistency.

Before expanding sales, test the complete B offer with likely users: setup completion, help needed, support time, willingness to pay and subsequent use. Include the intended range of users and access needs. State the available help, its cost and limits. A polished prototype does not establish a sustainable service.

Do not turn the small exhibit into a forecast

The first group’s 18 of 20 continuing users is 90%. The later group’s 4 of 12 uncoached completions is about 33%. These measure different outcomes in different recruited groups at different stages. Subtracting them is not an estimated treatment effect. Fourteen adaptations among eighteen continuing users describe this group, not all buyers. There is no tested B outcome to compare.

Instructor note

Allow three minutes for the first vote, four for the reveal and five for the second decision and debrief. Ask supporters of A to state the business they intend to build. Ask supporters of B to distinguish the observed problem from proof that their solution works. End with one sentence completing: “Our first customers showed…, but they did not establish…”.

Keep the reveal hidden until the first vote. Do not label less experienced users inferior or treat all early purchasers as innovators. The question is whether early customers may supply complements that a later audience needs the offer to provide. Adaptations alone do not prove a missing complement; the separate novice session identifies a current setup barrier. This is not cohort retention, market-size calculation or hiring an installation partner.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All businesses, people, costs, budgets, tasks and results are fictional. No actual gardening kit is evaluated and no agricultural, installation or safety procedure is prescribed.   1. Geoffrey Moore · Crossing the Chasm · author’s overview. The author distinguishes early-adopter commitment from pragmatic customers’ demand for demonstrated use and a complete offering. Practitioner framework, not a causal study or a universal sequence every venture follows.
  2. Eric von Hippel · Democratizing Innovation, Chapter 1 · MIT Press, 2005. Original scholarly overview explains users developing or adapting products for their own needs. It supports taking user contributions seriously, not classifying all first buyers as lead users or forecasting this fictional venture.
  3. GOV.UK Service Manual · Finding participants for user research. Official guidance calls for actual or likely users, explicit recruitment criteria, varied participants and attention to recruitment bias. Used as research-design guidance, not Hong Kong law or a probability-sampling claim.
  4. GOV.UK Service Manual · Map and understand a user’s whole problem. Official design guidance explains why individual transactions must fit the wider task and why coherent journeys matter. Applied cautiously to a physical product and support offer, not evidence about gardening-kit demand.
  5. GOV.UK Service Manual · Usability benchmarking a website or whole service. Official guidance distinguishes actual task completion, time, abandonment and perceived difficulty, and considers participant experience. The twelve-person fictional session is diagnostic, not a formal benchmark meeting its recommended sample range.

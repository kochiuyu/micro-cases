ACC-24 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Warranty provisions · Evidence-based estimates · Liability versus cash funding

Expected repairs. Already a liability?

An estimate is not a pot of money

Product-warranty reporting involves estimates of future repair or replacement costs. In its 2025 annual-report discussion, Magna describes assumptions about affected units and repair-related costs, and says it revisits estimates as warranty activity develops. That is a company disclosure under its reporting framework, not a repair-rate benchmark for a retailer.

Our retailer, product, promises and numbers are invented. Use the supplied simplified accounting policy; no external document or real-company warranty terms are needed.

Your classroom decision

A fictional Hong Kong retailer has sold and delivered 2,000 identical desk lamps. Its written, enforceable promise covers manufacturing-defect repairs during the next six months. This is included assurance that the lamp meets its original specification, not a separately sold maintenance service. No refunds, extra services, safety recall or injury claims are involved. The retailer itself must fulfil the promise; no supplier or insurer reimburses it.

At the first internal closing review, no repair has occurred and no warranty provision has yet been booked. Finance confirms a present obligation from the completed sales, a probable outflow for the batch as a whole and a sufficiently reliable estimate. The supplied policy is to record an expense and a provision, an estimated liability, for the weighted remaining repair costs across this large population. This is not a vote on whether to honour warranties.

The service team supplies the following complete initial mix for these 2,000 sold lamps. The three outcomes are mutually exclusive and exhaustive over the coverage period. Each cost includes all relevant parts, paid labour, handling and transport; a major repair already includes any associated minor work. Costs and percentages are estimates, not completed claims. Discounting is immaterial for this short-horizon exercise; tax and other transactions are outside scope.

The owner has separately earmarked HK$20,000 of existing bank cash for repair payments. This internal designation neither transfers money to customers nor legally restricts the bank account. It is not an expense entry or proof that all likely costs are funded. Finance must propose the liability estimate using the evidence, while the owner separately plans liquidity.

Under the supplied policy, update the existing provision to the revised weighted estimate and record only the increase as additional expense. No immediate cash transfer follows merely from this entry. A cash-funding plan is separate, and neither the initial nor updated estimate guarantees the exact eventual claims. Your decision

What provision and accounting adjustment are supported by the revised fault evidence? Reconcile liability, expense and earmarked cash, and explain what the retailer must still plan to fulfil its promises.

Estimate the population, not one lamp

Initially, expected minor repairs are 2,000 × 8% = 160, costing HK$19,200. Expected major repairs are 2,000 × 2% = 40, costing HK$20,000. The initial provision is HK$39,200. Equivalently, expected cost per sold lamp is 90% × HK$0 + 8% × HK$120 + 2% × HK$500 = HK$19.60; multiply by 2,000. These counts are estimates, not a list of identified customers.

Why not zero, the cash earmark or the worst case?

No repair is the most common outcome for one lamp, but the stated policy measures the whole population. HK$20,000 is an internal funding designation, not a cost estimate. Charging every lamp the major-repair cost would assume a different outcome mix unsupported by the evidence. A cautious estimate is not an arbitrary cushion: explain the applicable population, outcomes and relevant evidence.

Replace the mix

Updated minor cost: 2,000 × 14% × HK$120 = HK$33,600. Updated major cost: 2,000 × 4% × HK$500 = HK$40,000. Revised provision: HK$73,600. Additional expense and liability: HK$73,600 − HK$39,200 = HK$34,400. Do not add the full revised estimate to the old provision. No settlement occurred, so no utilisation deduction is needed.

Keep three records separate

The liability is HK$73,600; cumulative warranty expense for this batch is HK$73,600; cash paid on claims remains zero. The owner’s earmark remains HK$20,000. That designation is HK$53,600 below the current estimated obligation, but this is not evidence that the retailer’s total available cash is insufficient: its other bank funds and future receipts are not supplied. Plan payment timing and resources without changing the accounting estimate to fit a cash preference.

Document the promise and the update

Keep the sales population, warranty scope, dated technical evidence, outcome definitions and cost basis together. Review actual claims against the estimate and investigate differences. Uncertainty does not erase a supported present obligation; it also does not justify inventing extra liabilities for possible future sales. The current case contains only included assurance coverage. Separate service warranties require a different accounting assessment.

Instructor note

Ask learners to calculate both per-unit and batch estimates, then explain the cash earmark to the owner in one sentence. Reveal the new mix and require a bridge from HK$39,200 to HK$73,600. Reject zero, HK$20,000, arbitrary worst-case cushions and double counting; this is an evidence-based estimate, not a preference poll. Distinguish it from refund-adjusted sales targets and receivable impairment. In real work, the framework, contract, evidence, discounting and disclosure requirements need professional assessment. The supplied policy is sufficient for this exercise and does not invent legal warranty duties.

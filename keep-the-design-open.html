OPS-09 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Operations · Delayed differentiation · Design-mix flexibility

Print now. Or keep the design open?

In the news · Smaller runs, more flexibility

On 16 September 2024, the Hong Kong Productivity Council (HKPC) announced findings from its Hong Kong manufacturing study, discussing small-volume customisation and manufacturing flexibility.

This is local context, not evidence about the fictional pouch maker below.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. An independent Hong Kong maker is preparing 100 fabric pouches in two original prints: leaf and wave. Construction and quality are otherwise identical. Customers want their selected design and will not substitute the other.

The maker must choose one of two written whole-order quotes this afternoon. No money is committed. Plan A finishes a standard 50/50 mix now; Plan B keeps common plain pouches until customer selections close at noon Friday, then prints the selected mix for Saturday collection.

Both plans are affordable and can meet the deadline under the supplied capacity conditions. The team’s initial planning scenario is 50 leaf and 50 wave requests, not a confirmed order or established forecast. The decision concerns when to commit the designs, not how many pouches to buy.

A · Standard early finish50 leaf and 50 wave, nowHK$2,000
B · Flexible late finishRequired mix after Friday selectionsHK$2,400

Each pouch sells for HK$40. Any unsold finished pouch has HK$5 net clearance value after extra clearance costs; sufficient clearance demand is assumed. Unchanged overhead is excluded, so the resulting surplus is not complete business profit.

B’s fixed quote includes materials, printing, reserved late capacity, packing and required quality checks. The slot can complete and check 100 good pouches of either mix before Saturday; designs and materials are ready, with no assumed breakdown or defect delay. There is no extra supply or reprinting before collection.

These are the two offers available before today’s deadline. Other early mixes or hybrid quantities would need different quotes and capacity commitments.

Your decision

Which quote would you provisionally choose, A or B? What evidence about the design mix or finishing reliability would reverse your choice?

Round 2 · One fact changes

The sales coordinator brings the selection tally from a completed comparable pop-up: 80 customers chose leaf and 20 chose wave. The same designs and price were offered, with both available throughout selection.

One event and a potentially different audience cannot establish the next mix. Add an 80/20 scenario alongside the initial 50/50 scenario before committing; no probabilities are supplied.

Under B’s existing quote, the 100 common blanks can become 80 leaf and 20 wave in the reserved slot, without extra stock or charges. Under A, the 30 surplus wave pouches cannot become the missing leaf pouches. No third or unpriced hybrid offer has appeared.

Your decision

Does keeping the design open justify HK$400 extra? Compare both design-mix scenarios, choose a plan and identify the evidence that makes your choice defensible.

When the mix is balanced, early finishing pays

With 50 leaf and 50 wave requests, either plan sells all 100 pouches for HK$4,000, with no leftovers.

A leaves HK$4,000 − HK$2,000 = HK$2,000. B leaves HK$4,000 − HK$2,400 = HK$1,600. In that scenario, keeping the mix open costs HK$400 without adding sales.

When the mix shifts, common blanks protect the choice

With 80 leaf and 20 wave requests, A sells 50 leaf plus 20 wave: 70 pouches. Its 30 remaining wave pouches have net clearance value of HK$150. Surplus is 70 × HK$40 + 30 × HK$5 − HK$2,000 = HK$950.

B finishes the requested 80/20 mix and sells all 100. Its surplus remains HK$1,600, HK$650 above A in this scenario.

Total demand and total stock are still 100. The advantage comes from delaying the irreversible design choice, not creating more units. Clearance value matters: A’s fall from HK$2,000 to HK$950 is HK$1,050, not HK$1,200.

A price for flexibility, not an automatic winner

A is ahead by HK$400 in the balanced scenario; B is ahead by HK$650 in the imbalanced one. Neither wins in both, and one historical tally supplies no probability that determines an expected-return ranking.

A can be defended by convincing evidence of a balanced mix. B can be defended by material mix uncertainty and the value placed on fulfilling customer selections. The same arithmetic holds for a 20/80 reversal because the two designs have symmetric costs and prices.

Make the late step credible before relying on it

Common blanks help only if designs, materials, printing capacity and checks are ready in time. Those conditions are expressly supplied here and included in B’s quote; they should be verified before a real commitment.

Late finishing is not permission to rush quality checks or assume a free production slot. Equally, the two supplied quotes do not prove every other mix or hybrid policy is impossible. A new option would need its own price and capacity basis before comparison.

Instructor note

Take a provisional vote under the initial 50/50 planning scenario, then introduce the completed event’s tally. The reveal is historical evidence that motivates another scenario, not a confirmed future order or a forecast guaranteed to repeat.

Keep the model focused on finished mix: 100 units and 100 requests in each scenario, no substitution, no reprinting and net clearance value already allowing for extra clearance costs. Compare revenue plus clearance less the quoted relevant cost, not total business profit or environmental impact.

Ask what the HK$400 purchases: the ability to choose variants later, backed by a paid, feasible finishing slot. Do not invent a costless hybrid, missing probabilities or an environmental saving from leftover counts. The key tradeoff is commitment timing, distinct from choosing total inventory or sequencing batches.

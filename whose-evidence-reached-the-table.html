PPL-17 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Shared-information bias · Team decisions · Evidence integration

Everyone agrees. Has everyone’s evidence been heard?

Procurement is a team process

On 24 October 2024, Hong Kong’s ICAC held a seminar for 60 internal auditors from 25 government bureaux and departments, using cases including procurement and contract administration. That event supplies local professional context, not evidence of a hidden-profile problem or misconduct. The private company, suppliers, meeting and figures below are original fiction.

Your classroom decision

A fictional Hong Kong training company must recommend one printer for a month of course packs. Its three-person purchasing team has completed the required eligibility, quality, confidentiality and conflict checks. Both suppliers meet every mandatory requirement and the HK$12,000 budget ceiling. The authorised manager will approve the documented recommendation; a team vote cannot replace that approval.

The pre-agreed evaluation considerations are total quoted cost, ordinary turnaround, flexibility when bookings change, and access to delivery records. No automatic lowest-price rule or numerical weighting applies in this fictional procedure. These criteria cannot be changed to favour a supplier. The common comparison sheet shows A at HK$10,000 with a two-working-day normal turnaround, and B at HK$11,000 with a three-working-day turnaround. Both meet the usual five-working-day planning window. Each member has checked a separate operational annex but those details have not yet been combined.

An informal show of hands favours A, three to zero. The chair can record the majority recommendation now and use the remaining ten paid meeting minutes to prepare next month’s schedule. Alternatively, the team can spend those ten minutes sharing each member’s annex evidence against the existing criteria, then record independent revised views before deciding. Schedule preparation would move to an already-funded administrative slot tomorrow; neither route misses a deadline or skips a required check. Faster closure has an opportunity benefit, but agreement alone does not establish that the annexes have been considered.

Round 2 · More information

Before any recommendation is submitted, all three annexes are brought together. These were already-verified current written terms, held legitimately by different members and authorised for internal sharing. Nobody has fabricated, concealed or newly discovered a supplier defect. The common sheet was simply incomplete as a discussion aid.

The booking coordinator’s annex says B permits one quantity adjustment up to noon on the working day before production; A fixes quantities two working days before production. The delivery coordinator’s annex says B includes one split delivery to two training venues; A supplies one venue only. An existing funded internal transfer can cover A without missing a session. The administrator’s annex says B provides a downloadable delivery record; A provides an equivalent record on request, with one working day allowed for retrieval. Both satisfy the record-retention requirement.

All these terms are included in the quoted prices. A retains its HK$1,000 lower price and faster ordinary turnaround. Next month’s frequency of changes, need for split deliveries and administrative time savings have not been measured, so no net saving or superior supplier is established. The team must now make and document a recommendation under the unchanged criteria, while preserving the approving manager’s authority.

Your decision

After combining the annexes, would you recommend A or B, and why under the existing criteria? Describe one discussion rule that would make overlooked evidence less likely without treating every new fact as equally important.

Three votes are not three independent confirmations

Everyone saw A’s lower quote and faster normal turnaround. Repeating those facts can strengthen agreement without adding evidence. A unanimous initial preference is a description of the team’s views, not proof that the full information has been integrated.

Pool evidence before defending a preference

Ask each member for relevant facts not yet on the shared table, their source, and the criterion they bear on. Record both suppliers’ advantages and constraints. Then allow each person to reassess privately before discussing the recommendation. Separate facts, expectations and preferences; repetition should not count as additional support.

A better process does not prescribe B

B’s flexibility and ready records may justify HK$1,000 more where changing bookings and split venues matter. A remains defensible where stable quantities, the funded transfer and requested records are sufficient, especially if faster ordinary turnaround is valuable. Do not count three annex benefits against two common benefits as if each had equal value. No probabilities or cost estimates support an expected-saving calculation here.

What the exercise can and cannot establish

Shared-information bias means discussion can favour facts many people already know over relevant facts held by fewer people. This fictional exercise illustrates that mechanism; it does not measure its prevalence, show dishonest staff, or prove a ten-minute intervention improves real procurement outcomes. More discussion can still repeat the same material unless its structure brings different evidence forward.

Instructor note

Pause before the annex reveal. Ask learners to distinguish a provisional supplier preference from a defensible recommendation process. After the reveal, accept either supplier when linked to the unchanged criteria and acknowledged unknowns. Challenge both “three votes proves A” and “three new benefits proves B”. No private role cards or external reading are required: all evidence is on this page. Keep approvals, confidentiality, conflict controls and mandatory checks outside the trade-off.

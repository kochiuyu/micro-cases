Smaller payments. Cheaper loan?

In the news · Flexibility in repayment

On 29 April 2026, the Hong Kong Monetary Authority announced further measures supporting small and medium-sized enterprises. These included more flexible loan repayment arrangements and tenor extensions, alongside support for business transformation.

Repayment flexibility can help a business manage cash, but flexibility and a lower total financing cost are different benefits. The repair workshop and loan quotes below are fictional. They are not offers under that programme, and no eligibility is assumed.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong appliance-repair workshop needs HK$100,000 for replacement equipment. Two lenders have sent financing quotes. Each would advance exactly HK$100,000. Quote A asks for HK$4,500 a month; Quote B asks for HK$2,500 a month.

The owner favours B: “We keep HK$2,000 more each month for wages and parts. That must be the cheaper loan.” The bookkeeper has the monthly figures but has not yet checked the full payment schedules.

Your job is to make a provisional recommendation, not approve an incomplete contract. The business values a cash buffer, but its future monthly cash flows have not been supplied. No return on retained cash is assumed.

Would you provisionally favour B for its smaller payments? What must you check before calling it cheaper or deciding the workshop can afford either loan?

Round 2 · One fact changes

The full schedules show A requires 24 monthly payments of HK$4,500; B requires 48 monthly payments of HK$2,500. Both begin next month.

For this simplified comparison, these payments include all financing charges. There are no deposits, balloon payments, extra fees or variable rates. Assume scheduled repayment throughout, with no early settlement or refinancing. Both loans advance the same HK$100,000.

Your decision

How much would each loan require in total, and what would financing cost above the HK$100,000 advanced? Which benefit does B really offer, and what business evidence would justify paying for it?

Multiply the payment by the number of payments

A requires HK$4,500 × 24 = HK$108,000. Its financing cost above the cash advanced is HK$8,000.

B requires HK$2,500 × 48 = HK$120,000. Its financing cost is HK$20,000. Over the complete schedules, B requires HK$12,000 more than A.

These are undiscounted totals under the stated all-in assumptions. They are not annual percentage rates, and they are not a present-value ranking. Do not divide the total financing cost by the number of years and call the result an APR.

A cash buffer now comes with payments later

During months 1–24, B requires HK$2,000 less each month. This is genuine near-term cash-flow relief. It may matter when wages and parts must be paid before customers settle invoices.

During months 25–48, A has finished, while B still requires HK$2,500 each month. “HK$2,000 saved every month” is therefore not a description of the whole borrowing period.

Smaller instalments do not make B the lower-total-cost loan. Nor does the lower total make A automatically the better business decision if its earlier payments create an unacceptable cash shortfall.

Choose against the workshop’s cash plan

Prepare a month-by-month cash forecast, including equipment installation, customer collections, wages, parts and a prudent buffer. Stress-test slower collections or weak trading. Check whether A remains manageable and whether B genuinely resolves the shortfall rather than merely postponing it.

If both are safely affordable and other terms are equivalent, A has the lower total financing cost in this example. If early cash pressure is serious, B may be worth its higher total cost. Explain that trade-off explicitly rather than describing B as cheaper.

For real quotes, compare the full schedules, rates, fees, security, guarantees and early-repayment terms. Those are not supplied here. No lender or product is recommended, and the exercise does not establish that either loan should be signed.

Instructor note

Begin with the owner’s legitimate concern: keeping the workshop operating. Ask students what a monthly payment leaves out. Reveal the duration only after the first decision.

Keep three quantities separate: cash advanced, total repayments and financing cost above the advance. Have students compare months 1–24 with months 25–48. A good answer can favour B while acknowledging the HK$12,000 premium and identifying the cash-flow evidence needed.

No rate calculation is required. Do not treat simple annualised total cost as APR or assume a discount rate, investment return or actual affordability. The lesson is repayment timing versus total cost, not a rule that all longer loans are bad.

GVR-10 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Governance · Risk capacity · Expected loss

The smaller average loss. The bigger risk?

In the news

On 16 March 2026, HKEX published its Listing Committee Report 2025. It described governance enhancements effective from July 2025, including risk management and internal controls.

This is historical listed-company context, not a legal requirement for the fictional privately owned business below.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. Harbour Displays owns blank cardboard display panels for promotional projects. It can fund one additional storage-loss upgrade this year: better protective handling sleeves and packing, or raised, sealed storage.

Each supplier-reviewed plan costs HK$3,000, fully installed and maintained for the year. Both together require a separate HK$6,000 budget decision. The owner can escalate funding or reduce exposure if neither plan is acceptable; there is no free combined option.

Existing building, fire, worker-safety, electrical, customer-delivery and environmental precautions remain fully funded. No active leak or emergency is known. The comparison concerns residual financial loss to company-owned materials, not permission to trade away safety.

The table gives deliberately simplified one-year planning assumptions. Each event can occur at most once, otherwise causing no loss. Both events could occur. Stated losses include the exercise’s relevant replacement, clean-up and disruption costs and recoveries; no additional insurance payment is assumed.

A changes the handling-event probability, not its HK$10,000 severity. B changes the water-event severity, not its 5% probability. The unchanged event remains under each plan.

Both upgrades cost HK$3,000, so A has a HK$2,000 advantage in expected loss saved. An expected loss is an average across the stated uncertain outcomes, not a promised annual bill, guaranteed saving or worst-case loss. The assumptions are fictional, not vendor-effectiveness evidence.

Your decision

Which upgrade would you choose provisionally? Compare the expected loss saved, and name one fact about the business that could change your choice.

Round 2 · More information

A cash-resilience review establishes that Harbour Displays can absorb at most HK$60,000 in total losses of these kinds over the year without using money needed for committed wages, rent and customer work.

That capacity is available after the HK$3,000 upgrade payment and other commitments. No further emergency credit or owner injection is committed.

The owners confirm their internal boundary: keep the modelled residual exposure within this funded capacity, or present an explicitly funded alternative before retaining more. This is a fictional governance decision, not a legal threshold.

The probabilities and loss estimates have not changed. A still leaves a possible HK$120,000 water loss. B limits that event to HK$40,000; adding a possible HK$10,000 handling loss gives HK$50,000 across both modelled events.

Your decision

Keep A or choose B after the capacity review? Explain what the HK$2,000 expected-saving advantage does not tell you, and what you would need before retaining the larger exposure.

The average comparison remains useful

The expected losses add to HK$14,000 before either upgrade, HK$8,000 under A and HK$10,000 under B. Adding each plan’s HK$3,000 cost gives expected total financial costs of HK$11,000 and HK$13,000 respectively.

A therefore retains its HK$2,000 expected advantage after the reveal. That arithmetic does not change just because another consideration matters. Independence between the two events is not required to add their expected losses.

Capacity asks a different question

A leaves a possible HK$120,000 water loss, or HK$130,000 if both events occur. Either exceeds the stated HK$60,000 capacity. Reducing handling frequency does not reduce that water severity.

Under B, the two modelled losses together reach HK$50,000, below the supplied capacity. This is a maximum within the deliberately limited model, not a guarantee against all losses or a certification of safety.

Connect the choice to the owners’ boundary

With the supplied assumptions and boundary, B fits funded capacity while A alone does not. Retaining A’s larger exposure requires a credible funded or exposure-reducing alternative and appropriate approval, not just relabelling the risk as acceptable.

Changing preferences does not create cash. Insurance, credit and extra owner funds are not assumed available. If capacity were materially greater or estimates changed, A might be defensible; low-probability risks do not automatically outrank frequent ones.

Approval is the start of control, not the end

Assign responsibility for completing the upgrade and checking its condition. Review whether loss estimates, exposure and available funds remain valid as the year unfolds.

Keep mandatory safeguards and ordinary commitments protected. Escalate an unacceptable residual exposure rather than treating a favourable average or a signed approval as the entire risk decision.

Instructor note

Give the first vote space: A tackles frequent loss and has the better expected saving; B limits a much larger possible loss. The reveal supplies a binding internal capacity condition, so do not manufacture equal support for A alone afterward.

Students should distinguish event probability, loss conditional on that event, expected loss and funded capacity. The buffer is already after the upgrade payment: do not subtract HK$3,000 again. No real financing, legal or engineering recommendation follows from the fictional figures.

The setting excludes safety trade-offs and active emergencies. The sources support governance concepts, not the numerical probabilities or effectiveness of these invented storage controls.

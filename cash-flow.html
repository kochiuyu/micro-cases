FIN-03 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Cash flow · Working capital

The biggest order, the smallest cash balance

Hong Kong in the news · July 2025

On 18 July 2025, Atradius published a survey of Hong Kong business-to-business payment practices. It described pressure on cash flow from overdue invoices, even while firms continued offering credit to maintain sales and customer relationships. A sale and a cash receipt can arrive at very different times. Would you accept a profitable order if the money arrives after the bills?

Your classroom decision

You run a fictional Hong Kong promotional-gift packing workshop. A new customer offers your biggest order yet: HK$120,000. You have spare capacity, so it will not displace other work. The initial quote requires full payment today, before materials are bought.

You have HK$40,000 in available cash and must keep at least HK$20,000 as a safety buffer. Other business receipts and payments are separately funded; the exhibit contains every cash movement relevant to this decision. No costs have yet been committed, and declining the order leaves your HK$40,000 unchanged.

Your decision

Round 2 · One fact changes

The customer now requests one change: pay the HK$120,000 on day 90 instead of today. The price, delivery date and cost schedule stay the same. Assume the customer will pay exactly on day 90, so this is a timing problem before it is a default-risk problem.

You have no approved overdraft or other committed finance. Material suppliers and packing staff will not defer payment. The customer is willing to discuss a deposit today, with the balance on day 90. It has not agreed to one yet.

Your decision

Accept as requested, negotiate a deposit, or decline? What is the minimum deposit that lets you complete the order without breaching the HK$20,000 buffer?

Debrief · Follow the cash

Round one: profitable and fundable. The order adds HK$40,000 before tax and financing costs: 120,000 − 50,000 − 30,000. Cash rises from 40,000 to 160,000 when the customer pays, falls to 110,000 after materials and ends at 80,000 after staff are paid. The lowest balance after acceptance, including the starting point, is HK$40,000. The buffer is never threatened.

Round two: the same margin, but no way to pay on schedule. Without a deposit or finance, paying materials today would take the cash balance to −HK$10,000. After staff costs, the projected balance would be −HK$40,000. Those negative figures show an unfunded requirement, not an overdraft you actually have. A positive day-90 ending balance cannot pay today’s supplier.

Negotiate the timing, not just the price. With a deposit D, the lowest balance before day 90 is 40,000 + D − 50,000 − 30,000. Keeping this at or above 20,000 requires D ≥ HK$60,000: a 50% deposit. With that deposit, cash is 50,000 after materials, 20,000 after staff, and 80,000 after the remaining 60,000 arrives on day 90.

A HK$40,000 deposit merely avoids a negative balance; it does not preserve the safety buffer. Do not commit to the order until the agreed deposit is received or suitable finance is confirmed. If neither is available and the buffer is binding, declining is defensible despite the attractive margin.

Take it further · What if the customer pays late?

Now relax the certainty assumption. A late customer payment, rejected delivery or extra production cost could deepen or extend the gap. Borrowing can bridge timing, but fees reduce the margin and a loan is not available merely because the order is profitable. Receivables finance has eligibility, timing and recourse conditions: an unpaid invoice is not automatically cash today.

The Hong Kong survey is background evidence, not a forecast for this fictional buyer. A broader 2025 Coface survey covered nine Asia-Pacific markets, including Hong Kong; its regional averages should not be treated as this customer’s payment probability. Ask what evidence you need before extending credit.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–3: students vote on the original quote and trace the cash movements. Minutes 3–7: reveal day-90 payment and ask them to identify the first impossible payment. Minutes 7–12: pairs propose a minimum deposit and defend it against a smaller one. Keep the HK$20,000 buffer visible.

Ask “accept” voters where today’s supplier money comes from. Ask “decline” voters whether a deposit could save the deal. Ask students proposing 40,000 to calculate the day-7 balance. Then ask what changes if the customer pays late. Distinguish profitability, liquidity and credit risk: one order can pass the first test and fail the second even with certain eventual payment.

ACC-04 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Accounting · Inventory valuation · Audit evidence

The stock is all there. Is it worth the same?

In the news · Counting stock is not the whole question

Tristate Holdings’ 2025 annual report, filed on 23 April 2026, identified the valuation of inventory in its brands business as a key audit matter. The auditor discussed uncertainty around fashion demand and selling prices below purchase cost.

A key audit matter identifies an important audit focus; it is not an allegation of missing stock or misconduct. Our small wholesaler, count discrepancy and figures below are fictional, not events reported at Tristate.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. You are the accountant at a Hong Kong accessories wholesaler. Its company-wide ledger records 1,000 fabric pouches at HK$80 each, for an inventory balance of HK$80,000. No valuation reduction has been recorded.

A count sheet from the ordinary storage shelves lists only 900 pouches. The monthly internal report is due later today. You have a short window to inspect movement records and other storage areas before finalising it. This is not a statutory filing deadline.

The owner asks whether the apparent 100-unit difference means an immediate HK$8,000 loss. You have not yet reconciled locations, checked ownership or reviewed condition.

For this classroom report, the company measures inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Net realisable value means the estimated selling price minus necessary completion and selling costs. A physical shortage and a reduction in value are different possible reasons for changing the balance.

Initial evidenceAmount
Company-wide ledger1,000 × HK$80 = HK$80,000
Ordinary-shelf count900 units
Unreconciled difference100 × HK$80 = HK$8,000

The difference is not yet an established loss. The shelf count is not a complete company-wide reconciliation.

Your decision

Would you record an HK$8,000 shortage now, keep the balance unchanged with an unresolved-difference warning, or use the available time to reconcile the count first? What evidence would you request?

Round 2 · One fact changes

Dated dispatch and receipt records confirm that 60 company-owned pouches were in transit between the company’s own locations at the count cutoff. They were not sold. Another 40 pouches were separately counted in an inspection cabinet and were excluded from the ordinary-shelf count. They have cosmetic damage, not a safety defect. None of these units is counted twice.

The total is therefore 900 + 60 + 40 = 1,000. Nothing is missing. Reviewed evidence supports a recoverable value at least equal to cost for the 960 undamaged units.

The 40 damaged pouches can be sold honestly as seconds. The reviewed estimate is HK$40 selling price per pouch, less HK$10 per pouch in total necessary completion and selling costs. These are estimates, not guaranteed future receipts. No further costs are needed for this classroom valuation.

Your decision

Now that every unit is accounted for, should inventory remain at HK$80,000? What adjustment, if any, would you make, and what evidence supports it?

Reconcile quantity, then assess value

The count reconciliation supports 1,000 company-owned units. The initial HK$8,000 shortage is therefore not supported. Internal movement changes location, not ownership or total units.

But the damaged stock raises a separate question. Its estimated net realisable value is HK$40 − HK$10 = HK$30 per unit, below its HK$80 cost.

For each damaged pouch, the reduction is HK$80 − HK$30 = HK$50. Across 40 pouches, the write-down is 40 × HK$50 = HK$2,000.

Explain the revised balance

Undamaged stock: 960 × HK$80 = HK$76,800.

Damaged stock: 40 × HK$30 = HK$1,200.

Revised carrying amount: HK$78,000. The report recognises the HK$2,000 valuation reduction, not an HK$8,000 missing-stock loss. The adjustment itself is not an immediate cash payment.

Do not keep both reductions. If a provisional shortage adjustment was entered earlier, correct it when the reconciliation establishes the facts, then record the supported valuation adjustment.

What evidence answers which question?

Existence and ownership: physical counts and transfer records establish which company-owned units were present or in transit at the reporting cutoff. Check dates and avoid double counting.

Valuation: condition, expected selling price and necessary costs support the amount recoverable from those goods. A successful count does not establish that every unit remains worth its original cost.

Document the estimates and review them when better evidence arrives. Neither optimistic selling prices nor unexplained “conservative” reductions substitute for evidence. The stated HK$10 includes all necessary costs for this example; do not assume that every real-world overhead always belongs in that deduction.

Key idea: “We have it” and “It is worth this amount” are different claims.

Instructor note

Allow 2 minutes for the count discrepancy, 3 for the location and condition reveal, and 5–7 for the revised report. Ask students to write quantity and value on separate lines. Test three errors: treating a transfer as a sale, keeping the HK$8,000 shortage after reconciliation, and using HK$40 rather than HK$30 for damaged stock. Discuss why neither ignoring uncertainty nor inventing an unsupported loss is good reporting. This is a simplified internal accounting exercise, not advice about a particular entity’s reporting obligations.

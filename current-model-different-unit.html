STR-29 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Organisational ambidexterity · Exploration and exploitation · Shared capabilities

Improve the current model. Protect a different one?

Different horizons need more than a different box

Research on organisational ambidexterity examines how firms pursue efficient established work alongside uncertain new opportunities. Separate units can protect exploration, but useful separation also requires coordination and access to shared assets. The firm and allocations below are fictional; the research does not identify one structure that always wins.

Your classroom decision

A mature Hong Kong office-furniture repair firm wants to test a new workspace-reuse planning service. Its mainstream business completes defined repair jobs and earns payment per job. The proposed service would help clients decide how to reuse existing furniture before an office move; its customers’ buying process, useful service package and willingness to pay remain uncertain. The firm has approved a twelve-week learning pilot, not a commercial launch.

A places the pilot in the mainstream unit. Two existing staff together receive a nominal 60 paid hours a week for pilot work, with redeployment still controlled by the mainstream manager. The unit retains its billable-utilisation target, and routine coordination with repair specialists is straightforward. B places the same staff and 60 hours in a separately measured experimental unit inside the same company. Its manager protects pilot time and reports evidence about customer needs and viable delivery, rather than near-term billable utilisation. B must organise explicit interfaces with mainstream operations.

Both designs use the same funded staffing and twelve-week envelope. A further four specialist hours a week are budgeted for the pilot from the existing operations pool; no additional hire, overtime, unsafe work or breach of repair commitments is allowed. Administration and interface work must fit within the 60 pilot hours. A offers operational proximity and flexibility. B offers a clearer learning mandate but risks isolation. The owners have not yet decided which structure will govern the next twelve weeks.

A: mainstream ownership, billable-utilisation logic, easy routine contact, manager retains redeployment discretion. B: separate internal pilot ownership, learning evidence, protected pilot time, interfaces to specify. Same 60 pilot hours plus four specialist hours per week for twelve weeks. The pilot must test demand and delivery, not promise sales.

Your decision

Would you put the pilot in A or B? Explain which performance logic should govern exploratory work and what the chosen structure must preserve from the mature business.

Round 2 · More information

A scheduling audit of the previous six-week preparation phase finds that four of six planned customer-learning sessions were cancelled when pilot staff were reassigned to billable repair work. The mainstream manager followed the unit’s utilisation priorities; there is no evidence of laziness or hostility to innovation. Two sessions ran, but provide too little evidence to settle demand. The audit diagnoses a recurring allocation conflict, not proof that a separate unit will succeed. The proposed twelve-week allocation has not started.

The pilot also needs the repair team’s condition-inspection capability to tell customers which items are suitable for reuse. A separately labelled unit has no automatic right to book the specialists. The operations head confirms that the budgeted four hours a week can be reserved for the pilot without breaching existing repair commitments, provided booking, scope, priority and escalation rules are agreed. The condition checks remain under qualified operational control. Without that agreement, B could preserve interviews yet fail to deliver a credible test.

The owners can approve a shared-service arrangement within the existing budget: a named inspection contact, a weekly four-hour slot, requests submitted two working days ahead, documented scope and a sponsor who resolves clashes. Any exceptional diversion must be logged and the pilot schedule explicitly revised, not hidden. A can also be repaired by changing its utilisation treatment and protecting time; leaving its incentives unchanged is not the only alternative. No customer outcome or expected profit is yet demonstrated.

Your decision

Would you keep or change the organisational design? Specify both a protection for experimentation and an operating interface, then name evidence that would justify continuing or stopping the pilot.

Answer the first decision: fit the logic to the task

A can work if leaders genuinely protect learning despite the mature unit’s incentives; physical proximity alone is insufficient. B makes different accountability easier to see, but an empty box on an organisation chart creates neither learning nor capability. Exploration here tests an uncertain customer proposition; exploitation delivers and improves established repair work. Both matter. Do not excuse unlimited spending in the name of innovation or demand mature-job utilisation from every learning hour.

Answer the revealed decision: B with a bridge

I would choose B for the twelve-week pilot, conditional on the funded inspection interface being agreed before delivery tests. Protect the 60 pilot hours and exclude them from the mainstream utilisation denominator for judging the pilot’s performance. Keep the four specialist hours visible in resource plans and assess operations against the commitments it can actually meet. The audit supports addressing conflicting priorities, not blaming a manager or claiming that separation itself causes success.

Specify the shared capability

Use the named specialist contact, weekly four-hour reservation, two-working-day request lead time and documented inspection scope. The pilot manager owns customer-learning priorities; qualified operations staff retain condition-check authority. A senior sponsor resolves resource clashes and logs any exceptional reallocation with a revised schedule. Keep client permissions and quality standards unchanged. If the interface cannot be honoured, redesign or pause the test rather than invent free specialist capacity or bypass inspection.

Separate measurement without removing discipline

At weeks four and eight, review completed versus cancelled tests, what assumptions were supported or challenged, customer evidence and the resource cost of reliable delivery. At week twelve, continue only if evidence supports a credible customer problem and a feasible service at an acceptable cost, with a clearly funded next test. Otherwise revise or stop. Count learning quality and usable evidence, not merely interviews held. Track repair commitments alongside pilot milestones so one horizon does not quietly subsidise the other.

Instructor note

Require students to name decision rights, metrics and interfaces, not simply say “ambidexterity”. A modified mainstream design with protected time and changed targets is a defensible alternative. B remains inside the company; this is not a spinout or ownership decision. The four cancellations are descriptive process evidence, not a randomised structural comparison. Source accounts overlap and include theory, review and an illustrative large-company history; none validates this small firm’s twelve-week schedule.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All firms, staff allocations, cancellations, interfaces and review dates are original fiction. Both paths fund 60 pilot hours and 4 specialist hours weekly for 12 weeks: 720 pilot hours plus 48 specialist hours, 768 in total. Coordination is inside the 720, not an extra allowance. The earlier six-week preparation phase is separate; four cancelled sessions out of six equals two completed, not a causal effect or demand estimate. B’s specialist allocation is feasible but must be operationally reserved; budget alone does not grant booking rights. No additional hiring, overtime, unsafe inspection or breached customer promise is assumed. Pilot learning is not a guaranteed profit forecast. A may be modified, so the lesson is incompatible performance logics plus integration, not permanent superiority of separation. Scholarly sources overlap substantially, and IBM is an illustrative historical case, not a controlled trial or direct small-firm comparator.

  1. March: Exploration and Exploitation in Organizational Learning, 1991. Original theoretical paper and models examine scarce resources, different feedback horizons and the vulnerability of exploration. No staffing ratio is prescribed.
  2. O’Reilly and Tushman: Organisational Ambidexterity: Past, Present and Future, 2013. Original scholarly review, publicly rendered manuscript. Differentiated units require targeted integration and leadership; contextual alternatives remain relevant.
  3. Raisch and colleagues: Balancing Exploitation and Exploration for Sustained Performance, 2009. Original conceptual synthesis in an institutional open archive. Explores differentiation/integration tensions rather than treating separate units as sufficient.
  4. O’Reilly and Tushman: Ambidexterity as a Dynamic Capability, 2008. Original theory/review article. Discusses senior leadership, interfaces and conflicting operational alignments; propositions are not pilot-effect estimates.
  5. O’Reilly, Harreld and Tushman: IBM and Emerging Business Opportunities, 2009. Original historical case analysis. Describes distinct milestones, protected funding and cross-company capability access; insider-informed illustration, not independent causal proof.

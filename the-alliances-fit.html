STR-36 · Shenzhen · 15–20 minutes · Alliance portfolios · Strategic complementarity · Product architecture

The alliance works. Do the alliances fit?

Look beyond a successful pair

An alliance portfolio is a firm’s collection of direct partnerships. Research asks how partners’ resources fit the firm, rather than assuming that attractive partners automatically produce an attractive whole. The fictional decision below separates a partnership’s standalone benefit from its contribution to a shared product strategy.

Your classroom decision

You lead fictional Bayline, a Shenzhen maker of software for decorative exhibition displays, not safety-critical equipment. Its established, nonexclusive alliance with specialist installer A serves custom exhibition halls. Proposed nonexclusive alliance B would provide access to modular-display distributors. Neither partner is a direct rival of Bayline.

For the coming two-year product cycle, choose K: keep A alone; or J: add B while fully honouring A. Both arrangements are lawful, technically feasible and fully funded, including separate engineering, support, testing and management capacity. J creates no staffing bottleneck or missed obligation. B has not yet been signed.

B independently offers useful access to a different customer segment. Conservative internal business cases show positive incremental contribution after all relevant two-year costs for each alliance, including J’s separate product maintenance. These are fictional planning assumptions, not guaranteed sales. No numerical forecasts or probabilities are supplied for comparing total value.

Bayline’s current strategic priority is a reusable core product: improvements to its central control logic should carry across partner channels without separate redevelopment. It may deliberately revise that priority, but should not describe two distinct product businesses as one shared core. The team has not yet compared the partners’ detailed roadmaps.

Round 2 · More information

The completed architecture review finds that A requires a specialised closed interface with tightly coupled central control logic. B requires an openly documented modular interface and independently replaceable components. These are the specific fictional roadmaps, not universal properties of closed and open systems. Neither partner will change its requirement during this cycle.

Both architectures work. However, meeting both roadmaps requires two distinct core codebases and separate release paths for the full two years. Improvements to central control logic must be separately redeveloped and tested; they cannot be transferred as a shared core update. General staff experience and administrative tools can still be shared.

The review rules out a common core, adapter or staged migration that meets both partners’ requirements during this cycle. Separate teams, full maintenance and all relevant costs were already included in J. This is not a surprise overrun or an inability to deliver. It is a strategic product fork: two continuing product directions.

Bayline has clear permission to implement both architectures and use its own general know-how. Confidential partner material stays within its authorised purpose and access boundaries. B’s published interface contains only approved information; open interfaces do not require publishing proprietary implementation or A’s secrets. Neither agreement demands exclusive dealings or unauthorised disclosure.

Your decision

Keep K or choose J? Explain whether your choice preserves or deliberately changes Bayline’s shared-core priority. What portfolio benefit would justify two product directions, and what must you avoid claiming about reuse, profits and confidentiality?

Debrief · Two levels of fit

B can fit Bayline bilaterally by bringing a useful distributor channel. Portfolio fit asks a further question: what happens when B is combined with the existing alliance and the firm’s intended product direction? The new channel is real in the scenario; common-core reinforcement is not.

Complementarity means resources make a useful contribution together, not simply that partners are different. Different customers can diversify access while incompatible core roadmaps prevent the intended form of technical reuse. Assess these dimensions separately. A portfolio can have commercial benefits without the architectural coherence management initially expected.

Debrief · A conditional choice

K is consistent with retaining the stated shared-core priority for this cycle. It preserves A and avoids adding a second core, while forgoing B’s useful channel. This does not prove that K maximises profit or that the closed architecture is generally superior.

J is defensible if management deliberately chooses two supported product businesses to obtain the additional channel and judges that direction preferable. Funding and positive standalone contribution make J feasible, not automatically strategically preferable. Accept the fork openly rather than approving J under a promise of shared-core development that the review contradicts.

Debrief · What the evidence cannot price

There are no amounts, probabilities, discount rates or values for future reusable capabilities. Students cannot calculate a portfolio net present value or establish which option has greater long-run value. They also cannot invent hidden coordination costs to make J unprofitable: the supplied two-year business cases already include all relevant costs.

Before committing in practice, compare evidence about the two segments’ durable demand, the value of a shared core beyond this cycle, and the economics of maintaining separate product businesses. A positive two-year case does not settle those longer-horizon judgments. Do not silently promise future convergence; no such path is established here.

Debrief · Architecture is not permission

Technical incompatibility and legal permission are different questions. Both routes are permitted, while the desired shared-core combination is unavailable. Respecting confidentiality remains a continuing condition under either option. Do not solve a strategic mismatch by exchanging restricted code or assuming that an open interface makes every implementation public.

Nor does an alliance automatically give Bayline control over a partner’s roadmap. Negotiation could inform a future cycle, but changed requirements are not an available solution now. The current choice is K or an acknowledged dual-product J, not a free third architecture or cancelling A without consequences.

Instructor note

Ask students to draw Bayline at the centre of two partner links, then draw the product cores beneath them. The first picture can look commercially attractive while the second contradicts the intended reuse strategy. Accept either final choice only when its strategic priority, foregone benefit and unsupported claims are explicit.

All firms, roadmaps, permissions, resource sufficiency and business-case findings are fictional. Research supplies questions about resource fit and interdependence, not proof of this technical incompatibility or legal clearance. This is not a vote between industry standards, a scheduling exercise or a decision to share a distinctive asset with a rival.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

Compare standalone alliance fit with portfolio coherence. K preserves the stated shared-core priority and forgoes B; J deliberately changes the strategy to two fully funded product businesses. Both are feasible and all relevant two-year costs are included, so do not invent capacity shortfalls or hidden losses. A and B cannot share the core during this cycle; general know-how may transfer within permissions. Positive fictional contribution without amounts does not establish NPV, a long-term profit winner or future convergence. Both existing obligations and confidentiality remain intact. No actual legal or technical clearance is implied.

  1. Dovev Lavie · Alliance portfolios and firm performance (2007). Original software-industry study examines resource complementarity and value creation versus appropriation. Observational findings do not establish this fictional portfolio’s return or architecture.
  2. Ron Adner · Ecosystem as Structure (2017). Original conceptual article examines activities, interdependence and partner alignment. Used for questions about roadmap consistency, not to label every alliance portfolio a single ecosystem.
  3. Seong-Young Kim · Influence of Firm and Partner Resources on Firm Performance in the Alliance Portfolio (2014). Full original article, hosted in a research-text archive, studies resource compatibility and technological complementarity in semiconductor alliances. Associations are context-dependent, not causal predictions for Bayline.
  4. Carliss Baldwin and Kim Clark · Modularity after the Crash (2001). Original working paper explains formal design rules and modular independence. It does not imply that any two architectures can be merged, or that modularity requires publishing all implementation details.
  5. WIPO · Guide to Trade Secrets and Innovation: Collaborative innovation. Official substantive guidance explains purpose-limited information sharing, background rights and agreed use of collaborative results. General context only, not jurisdiction-specific legal advice or validation of the fictional permissions.

ECO-28 · Fictional self-employed adult · 15–20 minutes · Present bias · Voluntary commitment · Value of flexibility

Choose freely now. Restrict a future choice?

A restriction can be a chosen service

Laibson’s model explains how time-inconsistent preferences can create a motive to constrain future choices. Ashraf, Karlan and Yin tested a savings account with voluntarily chosen withdrawal restrictions. Their results establish neither a universal need for commitment nor the best terms for any individual.

Anett John’s field experiment cautions that choosing a commitment product does not ensure it improves welfare. CFPB guidance separately emphasises accessible emergency funds and checking account withdrawal terms. The classroom arrangements below are invented and hold returns equal to isolate commitment from yield; no real account is being recommended or opened.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. Mei, a self-employed adult illustrator, has HK$12,000 set aside for an optional studio upgrade at the start of month 7. No purchase is contracted and postponing it would not threaten her livelihood. She independently wants to protect this goal. All taxes, household and business commitments are funded separately. An adequate liquid emergency reserve remains untouched under either option; it is not part of the HK$12,000.

Mei compares Flexible, allowing any withdrawal immediately, and Commitment, holding the entire HK$12,000 until the start of month 7. The offered comparison is all-or-nothing for this amount; no smaller commitment balance is available in this exercise. Both preserve principal, have zero fees and zero interest over the six months, and carry the same stipulated provider risk and applicable consumer protections. There is no reward for choosing the restriction. No account has been selected or funded.

Commitment has two documented early-release exceptions only: an urgent essential repair to her primary home, or an uninsured essential healthcare bill. Qualifying evidence permits release of the documented amount without fee; all remaining funds stay restricted. Neither discretionary shopping nor business development qualifies. Mei cannot simply cancel the restriction or pay a penalty to withdraw for another purpose. The rules are transparent, lawful classroom assumptions, not claims about actual banking products.

The choice is voluntary. Mei has time to read the terms, ask questions and decline without losing access to Flexible. Initially she has not described how often she changes her spending plans or any possible use before month 7. She asks whether the inability to withdraw can ever be a benefit rather than only a cost. No one else gets to choose her savings goal or define all present consumption as waste.

Why might Mei voluntarily prefer Commitment even though it pays no extra return? Which facts about her own preferences, emergency access and foreseeable needs would you want before supporting that choice?

Round 2 · More information

Mei describes a recurring pattern. Weeks ahead, she prefers saving for the studio upgrade over buying optional decorative supplies. When an advertised sale becomes immediately available, she often spends HK$3,000, then later regrets delaying the upgrade. Prices, product information and her resources are unchanged in these comparisons; the key change is that consumption has become immediate. She anticipates the same temptation again and herself wants help resisting it. This account is consistent with present bias, not a diagnosis or proof from a single purchase.

She also identifies a different possibility: a place may open in a useful professional course during month 2, requiring HK$3,000 immediately. Attendance is optional and not needed for current income, but Mei regards it as a potentially worthwhile development opportunity. Availability and benefit are uncertain; no probability or money value is supplied. It falls outside Commitment’s release rules. There is no spare non-emergency cash, affordable credit or assured new saving to pay for it while preserving the full studio fund.

Mei’s liquid emergency reserve remains adequate for the stipulated essential risks. The course is not an emergency and she does not want to spend that reserve on it. She wants students to distinguish resisting a purchase she expects to regret from preserving a choice she might endorse after new information. She is still deciding before depositing, so either arrangement remains available on the original terms.

Under Flexible, paying HK$3,000 for either the sale purchase or the course would leave HK$9,000 of the studio fund, HK$3,000 short of the current goal. Commitment preserves the HK$12,000 for month 7 unless a qualifying exception is used, but would prevent the course payment. It does not guarantee she will value the studio upgrade most when month 7 arrives.

Your decision

What would you advise Mei to consider now? Compare the self-control benefit with the excluded course option, state the budget consequences, and explain why neither maximum savings nor maximum flexibility alone proves which arrangement is better for her.

Answer the opening question

A voluntarily chosen restriction can help a person follow a goal they expect their future immediate preferences to undermine. With identical returns and fees, Commitment’s potential value is behavioural, not extra interest. Ask Mei whether the pattern is recurrent, whether she recognises it beforehand, whether the restriction addresses it and what legitimate needs it would block.

Adequate emergency liquidity is a separate prerequisite, not a reason to lock every remaining pound or dollar. Read the exact exceptions and enforcement terms. Here there is no discretionary early exit: treating the account as accessible “if really necessary” would misdescribe the product. Declining it is a valid informed choice.

Distinguish a reversal from learning

The recurring sale example holds prices, information and resources fixed while immediate consumption becomes more attractive. That is consistent with present bias: relative patience changes as the tempting option reaches the present. Ordinary impatience or liking attractive supplies is not enough to establish this pattern. Mei’s own anticipated regret and desire for support are relevant.

The course is different. A newly available, useful opportunity could rationally change her plan. Blocking it is a real cost of a broad commitment rule, even though it is not an emergency. Do not relabel every future withdrawal as failure or assume the initial studio goal must always prevail.

Answer the revised decision without choosing her welfare

My provisional advice is retain Flexible while Mei assesses how much she values the possible course relative to protection of the studio goal. Flexible preserves the option but leaves the recognised temptation active. If, after considering that cost, Mei knowingly values protecting the upgrade more and accepts losing the course opportunity, Commitment is also defensible. No supplied probability or utility makes one mathematically optimal.

Under Flexible, a HK$3,000 withdrawal leaves HK$9,000, a HK$3,000 shortfall against HK$12,000. If the course is chosen, she must deliberately accept postponing or reducing the upgrade unless new resources actually materialise. Under Commitment, the full goal remains funded at month 7 absent a permitted emergency release, but course access is lost. Do not promise both uses from the same money.

Use lighter support honestly

Mei could voluntarily label the Flexible balance for the upgrade, turn off sale notifications or use a personal reflection period before nonessential spending. These may help but do not change the account’s immediate access and are not proven effective in this case. She can decline them, and they should not transfer control of her money to another person.

A differently designed account with a smaller restricted balance or an additional release category could change the tradeoff, but it is not one of the offered products. Do not invent that flexibility after commitment. Any future product would need its actual terms, costs and consumer protections checked afresh.

Instructor note

Keep the discussion about self-chosen goals, not the moral superiority of saving. Ask what makes the sale pattern time-inconsistent and why the course is not necessarily another temptation. Present bias is a possible interpretation of the stipulated pattern; future need uncertainty is a distinct reason for liquidity.

Unlike FIN-18, this is not a known-payment maturity or interest calculation. Returns are equal and emergency funding is preserved. All amounts, release categories and reported preferences are invented. Empirical savings increases and voluntary take-up do not by themselves prove improved welfare. No account opening, transfer or real personal financial recommendation is part of the exercise.

STR-46 · Hong Kong and the Greater Bay Area · 15 minutes · Value of information · Market entry · Test design · Expected payoff

One pilot. Which partner helps you decide?

Greater Bay Area initiatives support evidence for regional decisions

In December 2025, Hong Kong’s Health Bureau announced a real-world study centre and a Greater Bay Area collaboration platform. The release described a role for evidence in future development and registration submissions. This fictional simulation asks a commercial question about operating-room logistics equipment, not clinical performance or approval.

Your classroom decision

A Hong Kong equipment supplier can trial a non-clinical instrument transport trolley with one regional partner. No patients or clinical data are involved. Partner A provides HK$70,000 of net pilot contribution and a report from a showroom demonstration. The report gives no clue about demand at other sites.

Partner B offers HK$40,000 of net pilot contribution but gives a field signal before the supplier must choose whether to launch elsewhere. The supplier initially assumes B’s signal perfectly distinguishes strong and weak demand. Both demand states are equally likely. Launch contributes as in the table; declining adds zero. Compare total expected contribution over the two decisions with no additional costs.

Your decision

Which partner should the supplier choose on the first assumptions, and how would it make the later launch decision after each report?

Round 2 · More information

The supplier audits the field test. B calls a strong market “positive” 80% of the time, and calls a weak market “negative” 80% of the time. State chances and launch contributions are unchanged. A’s report still provides no new information.

Your decision

With B’s imperfect signal, when should the supplier launch? Which partner now offers more expected total contribution, and what equal accuracy in both states, assuming it is at least 50%, would make the two partners tie?

Debrief · Value decisions, not report length

With A, launching unconditionally adds 0.5 × HK$150,000 − 0.5 × HK$100,000 = HK$25,000. Including the pilot gives HK$95,000. With a perfect B signal, launch only in the strong state. Launch then adds HK$75,000 in expectation, giving HK$115,000 total.

Debrief · See what a positive signal means

With B at 80% accuracy, a positive signal comes from strong demand with joint probability 0.5 × 0.8 = 0.4 and from weak demand with probability 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.1. Launch after positive only; the net effect is 0.4 × HK$150,000 − 0.1 × HK$100,000 = HK$50,000 across all cases. Launch after negative would have a negative conditional payoff.

Debrief · Set the minimum useful accuracy

At 80%, B now gives HK$90,000 including the pilot, below A’s HK$95,000. In HK$ thousands, launch only after positive gives B total 40 + 75a − 50(1 − a) = 125a − 10 at accuracy a near the tie. Set it equal to 95: a = 84%.

Takeaway · Run a test that can change the action

B’s pilot is valuable only to the extent that it helps select suitable launch conditions. Ask for a record linking the signal to demand at comparable destinations. It may be insufficient to show that the prototype worked smoothly at the partner’s site.

Instructor note

Allow 15 minutes: 3 to define actions, 4 for the perfect signal, 5 to draw the decision tree and 3 to discuss evidence. Use joint probabilities in the expected contribution calculation, then ask which assumption would change the ranking.

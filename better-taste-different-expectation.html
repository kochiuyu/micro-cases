MKT-23 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Sensory expectations · Brand cues · Comparable taste tests

Better taste. Or a different expectation?

Experience includes more than the liquid

In published experiments, researchers have investigated whether information supplied before tasting can influence reported preferences or pleasantness. Lee, Frederick and Ariely studied ingredient disclosure around beer tasting; Plassmann and colleagues studied price cues while participants sampled wine. Their specific experiments motivate a question, not a promise that any brand will improve any drink.

Our non-alcoholic drinks, people, sessions and figures are invented. No health, nutrition or neurological claim is made for them.

Your classroom decision

You lead positioning for a fictional Hong Kong maker of a non-alcoholic sparkling drink, A. B is one competing drink in the same category. Neither formulation changed between two small tasting sessions. The brief lists these counts: in each session, 60 people tasted both drinks and selected the one they preferred; all completed the comparison.

The first session hid the brands behind codes. The second showed the actual brands. The summaries do not yet say how participants were recruited or how samples were served. The marketing team sees A winning when hidden and losing when identified. Someone suggests that A’s current brand is holding back a better-tasting product.

You are choosing the emphasis of a provisional creative brief, not authorising a superiority claim. A taste-discovery route would invite shoppers to sample and judge; a product-story route would explain verified flavour and ingredient facts without comparison. Both fit the budget and deadline. You can also defer the positioning choice for one further test; no campaign is committed. Neither route may claim these summaries establish population preference or a causal brand effect.

Round 2 · More information

The session records arrive. The blind group was recruited from adult category users at a neutral venue. Both drinks were served at 8°C in identical cups, with half receiving A first and half B first. The branded group consisted of different adults recruited from B’s fan mailing list. Both drinks were served at 22°C at a separate event, and everyone received A first. They saw the actual brand names on cards.

The drinks came from the same respective production batches, with no spoilage, dilution or recipe change. Recruitment, temperature, venue and order nevertheless differed along with brand visibility. These are descriptions, not estimates of how much any factor mattered.

You have funding and access for one next voluntary, safe session with 120 new adult category users from the same recruitment pool, excluding employees and prior-session participants. Ingredients and allergy information are disclosed appropriately, without masking safety information. The target question is whether showing the actual brand names changes relative preference for these unchanged drinks under standardised serving conditions.

Choose either a larger codes-only comparison of A versus B, or a parallel comparison randomly assigning 60 people to codes-only and 60 to actual brand cards. For the parallel plan, both groups taste identical batches at the same temperature, volume, venue and neutral instructions; A/B serving order is balanced within each group. Identical cups are used; only the truthful name cards differ. No invented claims or false identities are used. Participants may decline or withdraw. The 120-person allocation is a supplied pilot budget, not a power calculation or guaranteed precision.

Your decision

Which claim can the team support now, and which next test would you fund to investigate the apparent reversal? Specify what changes, what stays constant and what the result still would not prove.

Read the counts before explaining them

A was chosen by 36/60 = 60% in the codes-only session and 24/60 = 40% in the branded session. That is a 20-percentage-point difference between session proportions. It is not a count of 12 individuals who changed their minds: the groups contain different people. Pooling them gives 60/120 = 50%, but mixes different settings and cannot identify a brand effect.

Do not turn a reversal into a mechanism

The results are compatible with several explanations. Existing preference for B, serving conditions, order, brand information and random variation could contribute. Their effects cannot be separated from these summaries. The brand might matter, but neither its direction nor size is established here. A stronger blind result also does not establish an objectively superior recipe or a market-wide winner.

Choose the message within the evidence

An internal summary can report exactly what happened in each session, with recruitment and serving limitations. Do not publish “customers prefer A”, “better taste proved” or “our label reduces liking by 20 points” from these results. Even a numerically accurate headline can imply a broader comparison than the test supports.

A sampling invitation remains a defensible provisional route because it lets customers try the product without promising victory. A factual flavour/ingredient story also remains feasible. Choose according to the communication goal, not a fictional ROI calculation: no campaign sales, repeat purchases or costs beyond the shared budget are supplied. Deferring comparative positioning for the next test is reasonable.

Match the next comparison to the question

For the stated brand-visibility question, fund the parallel random-assignment plan. The codes-only alternative is useful for a recipe question, but cannot estimate a name-visibility difference by itself. Prespecify the primary response: preference for A after tasting both, with any withdrawal or missing answer recorded separately. Use the same wording and completion rules in both arms; do not quietly discard incomplete responses.

Compare preference proportions between assigned groups and report uncertainty. Standardisation and random assignment make the contrast more interpretable; they do not ensure a positive effect or balance every characteristic in one small pilot. Keep brands truthful and serving staff neutral; do not reveal a hypothesis or coach responses.

Name what the improved design still cannot tell you

The parallel plan estimates a relative-preference response to displaying both actual names in this session. It does not isolate A’s name from B’s name, individual visual features, underlying psychological mechanisms or chemical taste. It does not establish future sales, repeated use, willingness to pay or representativeness of all shoppers. A preference outcome is not a health or nutritional outcome.

No new results are supplied. A larger future study should choose its sample size for a meaningful effect and intended uncertainty. If the business instead wants to change packaging graphics or evaluate market demand, that requires a different comparison, not a stronger headline on this pilot.

Instructor note

Start with the two counts only. Ask students to distinguish a provisional creative direction from an established causal explanation. Reveal recruitment and serving differences before asking them to choose a next test. Reward a precise question and consistent conditions, not automatic loyalty to blind tasting: a brand-aware experience can be commercially relevant, but it answers a different question. Do not ask students to drink anything; all work is a paper exercise. No claims are cleared for real advertising by this exercise.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All drinks, session counts, recruitments and budgets are fictional. Published alcohol experiments are limited background, not direct evidence for this non-alcoholic product or instructions to conceal ingredients. Same batches do not make separate sessions comparable. Counts describe distinct samples and are not population estimates. Optional source links are unnecessary for either decision.

  1. Lee, Frederick and Ariely · Try It, You’ll Like It · Psychological Science, 2006. Original experimental paper compares blind, before-disclosure and after-disclosure conditions. Supports the limited proposition that information timing can matter in a studied setting, not that all brands improve experience.
  2. Plassmann et al. · Marketing actions can modulate neural representations of experienced pleasantness · PNAS, 2008. Original controlled wine-price-cue study. Used only to motivate extrinsic-cue questions; no neural claim or effect size is transferred to the fictional drink.
  3. Society of Sensory Professionals · Recommendations for publications containing sensory data. Professional guidance distinguishes liking, descriptive and discrimination objectives; addresses recruitment, order, masking and study context. Supports a fit-for-purpose method, not automatic validity of this illustrative pilot size.
  4. NIST/SEMATECH · Completely randomized designs. Official design guidance explains random allocation of factor levels and balanced replication. Supports assignment logic, not representativeness or guaranteed balance in every small realised sample.
  5. US FTC · Policy Statement Regarding Advertising Substantiation, 1984. Official statement distinguishes objective express/implied claims and prior support. Comparative historical policy context, not a ruling on Hong Kong advertising or legal clearance for proposed copy.

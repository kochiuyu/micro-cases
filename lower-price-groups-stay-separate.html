ECO-21 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Group pricing · Price discrimination · Resale and arbitrage

A lower price for some. Can the groups stay separate?

A concession needs more than a price label

Hong Kong’s URBTIX notice distinguishes concessionary tickets and states that their holders need acceptable eligibility proof for admission. Separately, Ticketmaster’s US help page describes transfer of eligible tickets with a replacement barcode, while distinguishing transfer from payment. These are different systems, not evidence that all tickets can be resold or that concessions always prevent resale.

The event and all prices, values, quantities and transfer permissions below are original fiction. The economic question is how a permitted resale route changes a seller’s pricing calculation.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong organiser is preparing a new adult arts demonstration. It can choose U, one HK$100 admission price, or G, HK$60 for existing adult members of a separate arts club and HK$100 for other adults. The concession has been checked as lawful and is clearly disclosed for this exercise. It is not a secret personalised price, membership subscription or reward for providing more personal data. The club’s membership is fixed for this event; nobody joins just to obtain a discount.

There are exactly 100 potential attendees in the model. Each wants at most one admission and buys for own attendance only if its price is below the stated value. Values already account for travel, time and other participation costs. The event and service quality are identical across tickets. Capacity is 120, with no crowding or displacement in the model. Each attendee adds HK$20 complete incremental cost. Fixed venue and staffing costs are unchanged; compare contribution before those fixed costs, not net profit. Prices include all organiser charges.

The initial worksheet assumes members use the lower-priced tickets themselves and no tickets cross between groups. That separation has not yet been verified. Existing membership can be confirmed without collecting identity-document copies or new personal profiles. No tickets have been announced or sold, and no existing concession promise is withdrawn. First choose provisionally and state what must be checked about purchase, transfer and admission.

Round 2 · More information

The organiser checks the proposed sales arrangement before launch. Under G, membership is checked only when buying. Members may purchase additional tickets for others; valid tickets may be transferred and resold to non-members under the stipulated lawful terms. At entry the venue checks ticket validity, not club membership. This is not a forgery or an evasion of a purchase limit. These rules make the no-resale worksheet assumption unreliable.

To calculate one consequence, use this explicit scenario, not an estimated market equilibrium: members still attend with 40 concession tickets; 45 of the other adults obtain additional concession tickets through members at HK$80 each; the remaining 15 buy directly at HK$100. Enough willing members can supply those 45 additional tickets. Each transfer has a complete HK$5 transaction cost paid by the member reselling it. No other transfer cost, fraud, uncertainty or unfulfilled ticket is included.

Calculate participation before receipts

Under U, each of the 60 other adults values admission above HK$100; the 40 members value it below that price and do not attend. Receipts are 60 × HK$100 = HK$6,000. Attendance cost is 60 × HK$20 = HK$1,200, leaving HK$4,800 contribution.

With G and the no-resale assumption, all 100 attend: 40 × HK$60 + 60 × HK$100 = HK$8,400 receipts. Cost is HK$2,000, giving HK$6,400 contribution, HK$1,600 above U. This is group-based price discrimination in the economic sense: the same admission and cost have different prices by group. It is not proof that an arbitrary group label always predicts willingness to pay.

Check who receives each payment

For one additional ticket, the member pays the organiser HK$60, receives HK$80 from another adult, and pays HK$5 transaction cost. Net resale gain is HK$15. The recipient pays HK$20 less than the HK$100 direct price. That gap gives both a reason to use the permitted route. It is an incentive calculation, not a recommendation to break any real ticket terms.

Across 45 transfers, the organiser replaces 45 HK$100 sales with 45 HK$60 sales, losing HK$1,800 of receipts. Attendance and its cost remain unchanged. G’s contribution becomes HK$6,400 − HK$1,800 = HK$4,600, now HK$200 below U. Do not count the member’s HK$80 receipt as the organiser’s revenue.

At x diverted full-price sales, G’s contribution is HK$6,400 − HK$40 × x, holding the stated attendance fixed. It ties U at x = 40. Fewer diversions favour G; more favour U within this model. This is a sensitivity calculation, not a claim that resale must reach 45 or that a single secondary-market price will emerge.

The quoted HK$80 and 45 transfers are scenario inputs. More competing resellers or different access costs could change them. Forty members is not an upper bound of 40 transfers when additional purchases are allowed. But unrestricted purchases alone do not prove unlimited real demand.

A pricing decision, not a surveillance project

U is the higher-contribution choice among the two unmodified offers in the stated scenario. It also attracts only 60 attendees, versus 100 under G. An organiser valuing wider access may consciously prefer G despite the HK$200 contribution difference. Neither the original higher contribution nor a reduced organiser margin alone establishes overall fairness or welfare.

Before relying on a concession, check who qualifies at purchase, who may use it, what transfers are allowed and the cost and accessibility of any eligibility-at-use process. A prospective check could confirm existing club eligibility at entry without retaining identity-document copies, but its feasibility, queues, privacy, terms and cost require review. Do not count a proposed control as already effective. Alternatively, retain a simple uniform offer or design a different transparent programme, then recalculate its actual terms.

Instructor note

Use 3 minutes for participation and the first vote, 4 for the transfer payment trail, and 5 for the revised choice and sensitivity threshold. Ask who receives HK$60, HK$80 and HK$5, and whether 45 transfers add people. Distinguish identity at purchase from eligibility at use. The two known prices are candidate offers, not globally optimal tariffs; this is not two-part pricing, aftermarket pricing or a welfare theorem. All demand and resale inputs are fictional, no empirical elasticities or legal conclusion are supplied. Do not improvise invasive checks as a free solution.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All event, club, permissions, values, prices, quantities and costs are fictional. Groups have 40 and 60 adults; capacity is 120. U: 60 attendees, HK$6,000 receipts minus HK$1,200 cost gives HK$4,800 contribution. G without resale: 100 attendees, HK$8,400 minus HK$2,000 gives HK$6,400. G scenario: 85 concession tickets at HK$60 plus 15 ordinary tickets at HK$100 gives HK$6,600; attendance cost remains HK$2,000, leaving HK$4,600. Reseller margin is HK$80 minus HK$60 minus HK$5 = HK$15; recipient saves HK$20. The 45 diverted sales reduce organiser receipts by HK$1,800; the schedules tie at 40 diversions, holding attendance fixed. Resales are intermediary transactions, not extra attendance. Price and volume are scenario inputs, not equilibrium or forecasts; no real income assumption follows from membership. No hidden fees, identity copies, profiling or retroactive restriction is allowed.

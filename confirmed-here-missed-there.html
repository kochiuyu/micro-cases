HSP-26 · England · fictional tour organiser · 15–20 minutes · Service continuity · Cross-provider handoffs · Journey ownership

The check-in works. Does the handoff work?

Local confirmation is not a complete journey

Service-blueprinting guidance from Nielsen Norman Group maps the visible guest experience alongside backstage work and dependencies. A provider can complete its own task while a cross-provider journey still fails. Its practical method calls for observing actual workflows rather than drawing only the ideal process.

UK package-travel rules place responsibility for package performance on the organiser even when suppliers deliver individual services. ICO guidance asks organisations to identify the purpose, recipients and minimum necessary information when sharing data. The fictional exercise assumes the organiser’s obligations and lawful operating arrangements are already established; students redesign a handoff, not interpret a real traveller’s legal claim.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. An English tour organiser sells a two-night package under one contract covering lodging, local transport and a museum visit. Independent contracted providers deliver those parts. The organiser remains responsible for the combined promised service under either staffing design. Emergency contacts, an on-duty manager, accessibility support and required assistance remain available; none is traded away.

For routine changes, design A offers one named guest-facing coordinator with a trained backup who works with the specialists. Design B lets guests contact the hotel, transport and activity specialists directly; the organiser’s duty manager remains available for unresolved cross-provider issues. Both fit the same funded staffing budget and have adequate capacity. A reduces the guest’s need to choose a provider but adds a relay for specialist answers. B gives direct access to expertise but needs clear cross-provider ownership. No measured response-time advantage is supplied.

The team will test a routine late-checkout request before deciding which design to release. Assume appropriate lawful bases, supplier agreements, privacy notices, secure channels and authorised staff access already cover the necessary itinerary changes. This does not authorise sharing entire guest files. All test records use fictional adult guests and no live booking is changed. Staff must distinguish acknowledgement of a request from confirmation that every affected service can deliver it.

Round 2 · More information

In a pre-launch simulation, the hotel correctly records the guest’s request for checkout at 09:15 instead of 08:45 and sends “late checkout confirmed”. Its own lodging arrangement is feasible. No one alerts transport or checks the museum. Transport still holds the original 09:00 pickup. The museum still expects 10:00 entry. Every provider’s local record is internally correct; their combined plan is not.

It is 16:10 on the previous day. To support the later checkout, transport can offer a 09:30 pickup, arriving at the museum at 10:05 after a fixed 35-minute journey in this exercise. Entry at 10:00 has a strict cutoff, so that slot will not work. The museum can hold a 10:30 entry until 17:00 today. The visit lasts one hour. The return pickup stays at 12:00 from the same nearby meeting point, with no extra transfer time assumed. Later entry therefore reduces free time after the visit from one hour to thirty minutes. All services, access requirements and prices otherwise stay the same.

Transport needs an accepted final change by 16:45 today. Both the original itinerary and the revised one remain available until then; the original bookings have not been cancelled. The guest has requested only later checkout and has not accepted the changed visit time and shorter free period. A staff member cannot treat the hotel’s local confirmation as the guest’s agreement to that whole revision.

The authorised handoff can use the booking reference, service date, revised checkout/pickup/entry times, affected service identifiers, confirmation status and named staff owner. Each provider receives only the fields needed for its task. The organiser already has a secure way to reach the guest. No passport copy, payment-card detail, medical history or whole guest profile is needed. A staff proposal to copy every provider into the full booking file is therefore not an acceptable fix.

Diagnose the broken handoff

The hotel solved checkout, not the tour. The change crossed lodging, transport, activity and guest-choice boundaries. Correct local data and a sent confirmation do not establish compatible service commitments. Adding another shared inbox or copying everyone may increase messages without assigning responsibility for closure. A coordinator is useful only if the role includes authority, verification and backup.

Check the whole sequence

Original: 09:00 pickup + 35 minutes = 09:35 arrival for 10:00 entry; visit ends 11:00, leaving 60 minutes before 12:00 return. Revised: 09:30 + 35 minutes = 10:05, too late for 10:00 entry. The available 10:30 slot ends 11:30, leaving 30 minutes. The trade-off is thirty minutes less free time, not a higher price.

Do not promise the 09:30 pickup with the old museum slot or silently reduce the guest’s visit. Ask the guest to accept the whole revised sequence and its consequence. A declined revision does not justify abandoning the guest or removing a confirmed service; retain a mutually understood workable plan and explain its checkout and pickup requirements.

Close the loop with a named owner

Under A, the coordinator owns the request through closure and the trained backup inherits it on absence. Under B, the receiving hotel specialist opens the cross-provider task and explicitly hands ownership to the organiser’s duty manager; acceptance of that task must be recorded. The guest should not need to discover the missing link.

One feasible test protocol is to seek provisional transport and museum acceptance and the guest’s informed choice now, with a 16:30 internal check. Record what each provider can actually deliver, not just a message receipt. If an acceptance is missing, the owner contacts the relevant duty contact by 16:35 and escalates before the 16:45 transport cutoff. After guest acceptance, obtain final provider confirmations, update the authorised itinerary and send one clear guest-facing confirmation. Do not cancel the original reservations until the replacement chain is secured within the holds.

If the chain cannot be secured by the cutoff, mark the revision unconfirmed, explain the constraint to the guest and confirm how the still-held original itinerary will work. If that no longer meets the guest’s needs, keep the duty manager responsible for authorised alternatives and assistance rather than inventing a guaranteed late option. The simulation supplies no price or capacity for another alternative.

Share enough, not everything

The transport provider needs the booking reference, date, pickup time and location already held, operational confirmation status and a responsible staff contact. The museum needs its service reference, date, entry time and party booking already held. The hotel needs the agreed checkout and whether the onward plan is secured. Share the minimum fields needed for that provider, through the approved channel; preserve a restricted cross-provider status record for the owner.

These limits do not prevent a necessary handoff. They prevent sending unrelated sensitive or payment data as a substitute for deciding who needs what. A booking reference can still be personal data in context, so it is not automatically anonymous. Retention, correction, security and access remain governed by the established arrangements, not an unrestricted new spreadsheet.

Choose the service design, then test it

A is attractive for this multi-provider package because one visible owner can reduce guest coordination effort. B can also work if specialists transfer cross-provider ownership explicitly and the duty manager closes the loop. The simulation reveals a missing handoff; it does not prove centralisation is universally faster or that specialist expertise is dispensable.

Before release, simulate an absent owner, an unacknowledged change, a provider refusal and a second change arriving before the first closes. Test compatible confirmed services, clear guest communication and deadline escalation. Count completed, compatible journeys rather than only answered calls, records updated or messages delivered.

Instructor note

Pause after the first contact-design vote. Reveal the internally correct but incompatible records and have students trace one guest request across the three providers. Require the 10:05 arrival, missed 10:00 slot and thirty-minute free-time reduction before discussing who confirms what. A good answer names one accountable owner without claiming that person performs every specialist task.

Unlike HSP-02, the task prevents a package discontinuity in a pre-launch simulation rather than compensating for an already failed stay. Unlike IS-05, the issue is ownership and acceptance across providers, not units or data-format conversion. All staff, guests, holds, deadlines and capacity assumptions are invented. No live personal data are shared, and no source supplies an incident rate or measured benefit of either design.

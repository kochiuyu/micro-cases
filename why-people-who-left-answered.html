PPL-47 · Hong Kong · 15 minutes · Exit interviews · Nonresponse · Workplace learning

Fifteen people told us why. Who did not answer?

The exit reason is not the turnover rate

PCCW’s 2025 sustainability report separately defines voluntary turnover against average permanent headcount. Original exit-interview research also distinguishes recorded exit reasons from reasons reported later through another channel. The retailer below is fictional; its counts describe one departure cohort, not a real firm or sector.

Your classroom decision

You advise a fictional Hong Kong bookstore chain. In a quarter, 40 employees leave voluntarily. All are eligible for the same optional exit interview, and every participant answers whether late roster changes were a reason they left. It can be one of several reasons. The table is an aggregate report; no names or quotations are retained.

The manager wants to spend the whole improvement allocation on a chain-wide scheduling redesign. Alternatively, the team can first fund a small voluntary diagnostic: a neutral interviewer, a confidential staff feedback route and an audit of existing aggregate roster data. Staff can decline without penalty. Existing scheduling commitments and protections do not change.

In the exercise, treat each recorded answer as the answer that respondent would give to this question at that point. The answers of the nonrespondents are entirely unknown. Even fully observed reasons would be reports, not causal estimates of how many resignations any policy could prevent.

Your decision

Which next step would you fund? Find the answer rate and the share of interviewees mentioning late changes. How far could that share move across all leavers if the missing answers were known?

Round 2 · More information

An approved review releases larger, fictional role-group counts only. Of 20 storefront leavers, 18 answered and 12 mentioned last-minute roster changes. Of 20 stockroom leavers, six answered and three mentioned them. No individual responses are disclosed or linked to rosters. The company cannot assume the rest of each group would answer like those it heard.

Across a same-quarter sample of rostered shifts, 30 of 100 storefront shifts and 10 of 100 stockroom shifts were changed within the agreed notice benchmark. These are shifts, not people, and the log does not identify who resigned or why. This evidence supports checking the scheduling process, not estimating its effect on departures.

Your decision

How would you use the group comparison and roster audit? Calculate each group’s interview answer rate and propose one next test that can improve schedules without pretending to have identified why everyone left.

Debrief · Report the denominators

The cohort answer rate is 24/40 = 60%. Among interviewees, 15/24 = 62.5% mention late changes. Among all 40 leavers the possible fraction runs from 15/40 = 37.5% if none of the missing 16 would mention them, to 31/40 = 77.5% if all would. This is a missing-answer range, not a confidence interval.

Debrief · The missing people differ by role

Storefront answered at 18/20 = 90%, versus 6/20 = 30% for stockroom. Mentions among respondents are 12/18 = 66.7% and 3/6 = 50%. These cannot prove which role has a greater underlying exit concern. The roster audit’s 30% and 10% quantify exposure for recorded shifts, not an employee’s reason for leaving.

Debrief · Learn before spending the whole budget

Use a neutral, consent-based route to hear from remaining staff and eligible leavers who opt in, without managers seeing attributable answers. Audit how and why late changes arise, then test a narrow scheduling improvement with protections intact. Compare the same operational measure before and after, and report interview participation separately. Avoid small-cell breakdowns or claims that one fix will stop turnover.

Instructor note

Give students the aggregate table first. Ask them to draw an unknown box around the 16 missing responses. After the reveal, discuss why substituting roster evidence for missing individual reasons is not valid, even if it improves where management looks next.

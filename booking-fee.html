ECO-03 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Fee incidence · Platform pricing

No booking fee. A lower price?

Global platform in the news · November 2025

In its 6 November 2025 shareholder letter, Airbnb reported moving hosts using property-management software from split host-and-guest service fees to a single 15.5% host fee during October. Hosts could adjust their listing prices to maintain net earnings. This was a change for that group, not every host worldwide.

Moving a fee to the seller does not by itself tell us what the buyer will ultimately pay. The following Hong Kong workshop marketplace is fictional and uses fixed dollar fees, not Airbnb’s percentages or actual booking results.

Your classroom decision

You run a fictional platform where independent Hong Kong instructors sell workshop places. Each instructor chooses the listed price. Today, a customer pays that price plus a HK$20 booking fee, and the platform deducts another HK$10 from the instructor’s receipt. A proposed change removes the buyer’s fee and raises the instructor deduction to HK$30 per completed booking.

The marketing team wants to announce “HK$20 cheaper for every booking.” The product team expects a simpler checkout to help sales. Instructors are free to change their own listed prices or the number of places they offer; no promise to hold prices has been obtained. Assume workshop quality, other costs and taxes are unchanged for the comparison. No demand or participation response has yet been measured.

Your decision

Would you launch the new fee structure broadly, retain the current one, or run a pilot? Would you approve the “HK$20 cheaper” message, and what assumption does it require?

Round 2 · One fact changes

In private, separate conversations, several instructors say they would independently raise their listed price from HK$200 to HK$220 if the deduction becomes HK$30. That preserves their HK$190 receipt per booking. Others may leave prices unchanged or offer fewer places. These are stated intentions, not verified market-wide outcomes; the platform has not coordinated their prices.

At a HK$220 listing under the new structure, the buyer pays HK$220, the instructor receives HK$190 and the platform receives HK$30. The separate booking-fee line disappears, but that buyer’s total price has not fallen. No workshop cost or quality has changed.

Debrief · Who pays after repricing?

With an unchanged HK$200 listing, the buyer saves HK$20 and the instructor receives HK$20 less. If the instructor raises the listing to HK$220, both buyer total and instructor receipt return to their previous levels. The platform still receives HK$30 on that completed booking. Fee allocation alone does not create a HK$20 saving for everyone.

Who is formally charged and who ultimately bears the economic effect are different questions. Price changes, the ability to switch platforms, customer sensitivity and instructor participation can influence the outcome. Some instructors may absorb part of the change; some may pass it on; some may reduce supply. The case does not supply enough evidence to estimate those responses or a market-wide benefit.

A cleaner price is not a guaranteed saving

A simpler all-in price can still improve comparison or the checkout experience. But the current listing-plus-fee and the new all-in listing must be compared using total payable, not the name of one line item. “No separate buyer booking fee” accurately describes the proposed structure; “every booking is HK$20 cheaper” does not hold if listing prices rise.

Do not infer unchanged platform profit from unchanged fee receipts per booking. Booking volume, instructor participation, refunds, acquisition spending and support costs can change. A full rollout could be justified by measured benefits, but those benefits are not in the headline arithmetic.

Test both sides of the market

A monitored pilot should keep instructors’ pricing decisions independent and explain deductions before they opt in. Compare total customer prices, instructor net receipts, available places, completed bookings and platform net contribution. Track comparable workshops rather than treating a different mix of classes as a price change.

Use a contemporaneous comparison where feasible and report changes in promotions, seasonality or workshop mix that could explain results. Ask departing instructors why they leave; a higher completion rate among remaining listings can hide reduced choice. Decide in advance what would justify expansion, revision or rollback. No universal conversion increase is assumed.

Back to the evidence

Airbnb’s fee-allocation change and its separate 2025 total-price-display announcement concern different design choices. Neither company announcement proves that every host passed fees through or that the change reduced customer prices. Its later explanatory material explicitly illustrates host repricing.

Economic incidence offers a useful analogy: the side that formally pays is not necessarily the side that bears the final burden. A platform is not identical to a simple tax model, because participation and market power also matter. Research on tax visibility in US grocery shopping shows that presentation can affect behaviour, but its numerical effects cannot be imported into this workshop market.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–3: calculate the fixed-price proposal and vote on the rollout and slogan separately. Minutes 3–7: reveal instructor repricing and recompute the three receipts. Minutes 7–12: write a truthful customer message and choose a pilot metric that could contradict the team’s favourite story.

Challenge “the instructor pays now” with the buyer’s final price after repricing. Challenge “nothing changes” with price visibility and possible participation responses. Do not turn the classroom into a recommendation for instructors to coordinate prices: each seller chooses independently. This is fee incidence and platform design, not tax or competition-law advice.

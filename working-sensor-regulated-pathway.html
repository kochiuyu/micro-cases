ENT-37 · Singapore · 15–20 minutes · Regulated venture design · Intended use · Evidence and runway

The demonstration works. The regulated pathway is longer.

The claim shapes the venture

A sensor demonstration, a clinically supported diagnostic claim and a deliverable research tool are different commercial milestones. Singapore’s HSA requires approval for specified nonclinical supply routes; “research use” is not a blanket exemption. Medtech development programmes combine engineering, quality, clinical, regulatory and commercial work rather than treating approval as a final advertising task.

Your classroom decision

Fictional SignalSpring in Singapore has a bench sensor that produces repeatable readings on synthetic reference materials. No patient samples, patient records or diagnostic decisions have been involved. The demonstration establishes only this narrow technical result. Founders must choose one development route; they cannot fund both simultaneously.

C would ultimately support a specified clinical diagnostic claim for healthcare buyers. It needs evidence appropriate to that claim, quality controls and all applicable permissions before clinical supply. No approval or clinical performance has been established. The team’s initial commercial worksheet assumes receipts can begin at month 4; that timing is unverified.

R would supply a bounded laboratory tool for experiments on synthetic reference materials only, with no human testing, diagnosis, patient management or clinical interpretation. For this fictional route alone, required permissions and qualified review are stipulated secured within that exact scope. They do not cover C or a customer’s clinical use.

The venture has S$240,000 available after separately protecting all existing obligations and a fully funded orderly-stop provision. Core staff, facilities and quality costs are S$20,000 each month under either route. C additionally requires S$60,000 at time zero; R requires S$40,000. These are complete fictional cash costs, not regulator fees.

The preliminary worksheet assigns C S$25,000 and R S$10,000 monthly net customer cash contribution from month 4 onward. Each contribution is receipts after every customer-specific delivery and support cash cost, before the separate core monthly cost. Neither commercial amount nor timing has yet been confirmed by a buyer.

The board’s immediate objective is a properly bounded route that can operate through month 12 without new funding, while retaining the protected stop provision. No loan, grant or investor commitment is available. Compare scope, evidence and cash timing, not just the larger proposed contribution; no sales promise has been made.

Round 2 · More information

C’s prospective buyer confirms it needs diagnostic interpretation for patient decisions, not a research-only reader. It will pay nothing before appropriate evidence and all required authorisations. A qualified development assessment puts the earliest possible readiness at the end of month 18, with further delay possible. This is a fictional project estimate, not an HSA review-time promise.

R’s research buyer confirms the strictly nonclinical synthetic-material scope and supplies a funded schedule. For this exercise only, S$10,000 net contribution is received at each month-end from month 4 through month 12 inclusive. Delivery costs have already been deducted. Payment arrives before that month’s core cost; there are no deposits or other receipts.

R’s instructions, demonstrations, support and customer acceptance must all remain nonclinical. The team cannot sell R to satisfy C’s diagnostic request or append a research label to patient-facing claims. Any changed intended use requires a new qualified regulatory and evidence assessment before commitments; the fictional permissions cannot be stretched.

No clinical receipts occur during the first eighteen months under C; readiness at month 18 would still not guarantee approval or sales. Core costs continue monthly and the initial C cost remains necessary. R has no assumed receipts after month 12. The supplied schedule does not establish renewal, growth or permanent profitability.

Your decision

Which route meets the board’s twelve-month funding condition? Calculate each route’s cash position and C’s minimum funding gap through month 18. Explain why R’s lower contribution does not validate C’s clinical claim or guarantee a sustainable business.

Debrief · The demonstrated capability is narrower

Repeatable readings on synthetic materials do not establish diagnostic performance in an intended patient population. C requires a defined clinical use, evidence supporting that use and the relevant authorisations. Buyer interest is not approval. R solves a different research task for a buyer that explicitly accepts its limitations.

The opening worksheet should remain provisional. Its S$25,000 C figure is not usable cash when the buyer will not pay before the required pathway is completed. Do not rank a month-4 research receipt against an unsupported month-4 clinical receipt as if both were available.

Debrief · C runs out before readiness

C leaves S$240,000 − S$60,000 = S$180,000 after the initial outlay. At S$20,000 per month without receipts, that funds nine months. It reaches zero after month 9, before the month-12 checkpoint and well before the earliest stated readiness. Continuing into month 10 would require new funding.

Twelve months require S$60,000 + 12 × S$20,000 = S$300,000, a S$60,000 gap. Eighteen months require S$420,000, a minimum S$180,000 gap against available funds. This is funding through earliest readiness, not a complete budget to approval or commercial success; any delay or additional requirement needs separate funding.

Debrief · R survives the checkpoint

R leaves S$200,000 after its S$40,000 initial cost. Three months of core costs reduce this to S$140,000 before the first receipt. Months 4–12 contain nine receipts, totalling S$90,000. Total twelve-month core costs are S$240,000, so closing cash is S$240,000 − S$40,000 − S$240,000 + S$90,000 = S$50,000.

After month 3, each month reduces cash by S$20,000 − S$10,000 = S$10,000. Cash remains nonnegative throughout; the final S$50,000 is the minimum in this schedule. R meets the board’s checkpoint condition without spending protected funds. This conclusion depends on the stipulated timely receipts and complete costs.

Debrief · Choose a business model, not a label

Choose bounded R for this twelve-month objective. Defer C pending a credible clinical-development plan and sufficient committed funding; do not promise its buyer a clinical launch. C is not proven commercially worthless. Its current financing and evidence pathway do not meet this decision’s constraints.

R still consumes S$10,000 monthly after receipts begin. If that same pattern continued, S$50,000 would cover only five further months, but renewal receipts are not supplied, so even that extension is hypothetical. Cash remaining at a checkpoint is not profit, proven market demand beyond the buyer, or permission to fund C for free.

Debrief · Keep claims and delivery aligned

Document the allowed research task, accepted performance limits, customer restrictions and support boundaries. Investigate requests for clinical interpretation rather than quietly fulfilling them. Research-use wording alone does not turn an intended diagnostic product into an unrestricted tool. Actual classification and permissions require qualified, current jurisdiction-specific assessment.

WHO and international evidence guidance illustrate that analytical measurements and clinical performance are distinct. Their programmes do not automatically apply to this fictional device, confer Singapore approval or certify its safety. No human research, health claim, trial or medical-device transaction is authorised by this classroom exercise.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–5: identify what the bench result proves. Minutes 5–10: reveal the two buyers’ different intended uses and timing. Minutes 10–15: reconcile cash month by month. Minutes 15–20: write an honest R offer and specify what evidence and financing would be required before reopening C.

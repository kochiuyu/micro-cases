A better product. Who will install it?

A working product is not the whole customer offer

The IEA’s 2022 heat-pump report identifies trained-installer availability as a constraint alongside customer costs and manufacturing capacity. England’s official guidance also distinguishes training from the certification and support arrangements used to deliver installations. These are different parts of a functioning offer, not interchangeable achievements.

A June 2026 government review surveyed recipients of heat-pump training support. Many respondents had not yet completed an installation. The survey does not establish why any particular customer waited, or prove that a manufacturer’s subsidy will increase sales. The original fictional case below isolates that strategic question.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. You manage an established heat-pump manufacturer selling into a new English district. The current model works as specified and meets all required standards. It is unfamiliar locally, but demonstrations show interested households understand its function. Existing customers’ servicing, warranties and required safety work remain fully funded regardless of today’s choice.

You have £12,000 and management capacity for one development programme this quarter. A adds a more convenient scheduling interface to the product. B supports two independent, already qualified installation firms with product-specific familiarisation, service documentation, technical support and initial spare-parts access. B is not a substitute for qualifications, certification, building approval or a proper property assessment. Its scope and price include the manufacturer’s promised first-year support.

Households have mentioned both easier controls and finding someone local to fit and maintain the unit. The sales team has not yet checked which issue is preventing orders. Neither programme is committed. Both preserve the existing model, product price and required support. You may defer both; no compulsory obligation is being put to a vote.

A · Product interface£12,000More convenient scheduling controls
B · Installer support£12,000Two firms gain brand-specific support and local service readiness
Your decision

Would you put this quarter’s development budget into better controls or installer support? Identify the customer problem each programme could solve and the evidence you need before choosing.

Round 2 · More information

Before you decide, staff follow up with 60 distinct interested households that have not ordered. Forty name the absence of a locally available installer and maintenance service as their main obstacle; twelve name the controls, and eight name price. Each household reports one main obstacle. These are conversations with interested households, not a representative market survey, causal evidence or purchase commitments. No household is counted as an order.

Both installation firms would join B on nonexclusive terms. Each needs £6,000 a year to maintain the proposed additional local service capability, net of B’s launch support. Each completed installation contributes £200 after its direct labour, materials, travel and allowance for service work. These fictional incremental figures use the same first-year horizon; there is no additional unfunded support cost within it. Each firm is already authorised and equipped for the relevant work, and remains responsible for meeting applicable requirements.

Their illustrative planning scenario is 20 installations of your brand and 15 of other brands per firm during that year. These would be additional jobs, not displaced existing work. Each has capacity for all 35. Other-brand work is commercially available to pursue, not a guaranteed order book. Qualified firms can use parts of their service capability across brands while obtaining each brand’s required technical support separately. They will not commit to serving only your brand.

Your company earns £400 per additional unit sold after all direct manufacturing, sales and normal warranty costs. In this planning scenario, its 40 unit sales are additional to the no-programme baseline. That is an assumption to test, not something the interviews establish. Programme A has no credible incremental sales estimate yet. Compare B’s first-year cash contribution with its £12,000 cost, excluding tax, financing and any value after year one. No grant receipt, competitor payment, acquisition of an installer or restriction on its other work is assumed.

Your decision

Would you fund B on nonexclusive terms, choose A, or defer? Calculate whether the illustrative volumes work for both the manufacturer and each installer, and explain which missing evidence could reverse your recommendation.

Find the missing complement

Customers need usable heating, not just a technically sound unit. Local installation and maintenance are complementary services: improving controls cannot substitute for a firm able to fit and support the product. The conversations suggest a service bottleneck among these interested households, but do not measure the whole market or establish how many additional sales B will produce.

A manufacturer can obtain access to complementary capability through independent partners without acquiring them. Some installer skills and resources serve several brands; product-specific documentation, parts and support remain necessary. Nonexclusive participation may help sustain the local capability your customers need, even when competitors benefit too.

Two businesses, two thresholds

Each installer’s illustrative contribution is 35 × £200 = £7,000, leaving £1,000 after the £6,000 annual capability cost. Its break-even is 30 completed jobs. Your brand alone supplies 20 × £200 = £4,000, a £2,000 shortfall. The other 15 jobs contribute £3,000 and make the combined scenario viable. They are opportunities to pursue, not guaranteed work.

The manufacturer’s scenario is 40 additional units × £400 − £12,000 = £4,000. Its separate break-even is 30 additional units. At 24 additional units, it loses £2,400. Other-brand installations earn it no unit margin. These are first-year incremental cash comparisons, not forecasts or complete valuations of future partner relationships.

Recommend with conditions

B targets the main obstacle reported by this interested group. Fund it if management judges the additional sales and each installer’s workload sufficiently credible, accepts the small first-year cushions, and obtains clear service-coverage and support commitments within the quoted budget. Do not promise installation dates before actual capacity and property suitability are confirmed.

Choose A if stronger evidence shows controls drive valuable demand where service already exists, or defer if neither investment has a credible route to recover its cost. Ask for evidence on additional rather than shifted sales, completed jobs, service coverage, actual margins and retention of qualified capacity. Counts of training attendees alone cannot answer those questions. No supplied data establish a unique expected-profit winner.

Instructor note

Allow 15–20 minutes. Take a first vote before opening the update, then separate three questions: what blocks customer use, what sustains each installer, and what repays the manufacturer. Have students calculate the 30-job installer and 30-additional-unit manufacturer thresholds before defending a recommendation. Keep the illustrative scenario distinct from the 60 conversations: the matching number forty is not a conversion calculation.

The case is about a mature product’s investment boundary and access to complements, not first-mover timing (STR-13), technology licensing (ENT-08), or developing a harder-to-copy partner routine (STR-22). Keep qualifications, safety and existing promises outside the discretionary choice. Public evidence gives industry context and strategic concepts; all local customer reports, costs, margins, capacities and volumes are invented. No current subsidy eligibility or installation advice is supplied.

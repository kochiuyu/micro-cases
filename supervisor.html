PPL-03 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Promotion · Team performance

The best seller. The best supervisor?

Research in the news · April 2019

On 24 April 2019, researchers explained a promotion puzzle in a CEPR article: sales success helped workers win management jobs, but it was not the same as success in improving their teams’ sales. Their study used records from 131 US firms. Promotion can reward past effort and select someone for a different job. Those purposes need not point to the same person.

Your classroom decision

You own a fictional Hong Kong homewares shop. Your supervisor is leaving. Lena and Marco both want the role, have the same tenure and meet its basic requirements. You must appoint one tomorrow, with a three-month probation and the same supervisor salary whichever person you choose.

The supervisor will spend most of the day coaching four sales colleagues, assigning work and resolving problems, with little time for personal selling. You have spare staff to cover the selling hours of either promoted candidate at the same cost. Both candidates know Lena led last month’s sales ranking. You have not promised the role to the top seller.

Your decision

Who gets the supervisor role, Lena or Marco? Name the strongest reason for your choice and the evidence you wish you had.

Round 2 · One fact changes

The outgoing supervisor shares records from six shifts covered by each candidate. For the same four colleagues, sales per staffed selling hour averaged HK$1,200 when Lena led and HK$1,500 when Marco led. These figures exclude the acting supervisor’s own sales. Total colleague hours, stock availability and recorded visitor counts were similar.

The shifts were not randomly assigned. You do not know whether customer needs, basket sizes or other conditions differed. Both candidates remain interested; pay, coverage costs and probation are unchanged. These are fictional observations, not proof that one candidate caused the difference.

Your decision

Do the team records change your appointment? Specify what you will check during probation and how you will recognize the other candidate.

Debrief · Rewarding one job, selecting for another

The job has changed. Lena’s HK$1,200 personal sales per hour exceeds Marco’s HK$900. That is relevant evidence of selling performance. It does not directly measure coaching, coordination or solving colleagues’ problems. Equally, a lower personal-sales total does not establish better leadership.

Measure the team without counting the leader twice. The four colleagues’ recorded rate is HK$300 per hour higher under Marco, or 25% above the HK$1,200 comparison rate. Because the supervisor’s own sales are excluded, the comparison is not simply rewarding a star for making more sales personally. Yet the unrandomized shifts leave alternative explanations. Similar visitor counts do not guarantee equally easy customers or the same sales opportunities.

Choose for the role, then test your reasoning. Marco’s team evidence gives a reason to prefer him, especially if the new role is mostly coaching. Lena’s selling expertise and credibility may still be valuable, and the shift difference may not persist. State which uncertainty your appointment accepts. Neither “always promote the star” nor “never promote the star” follows from these records.

During probation, use consistent measures of colleagues’ sales per hour, returns, customer complaints and coaching quality, not sales alone. Compare comparable shifts and record changes in staffing and customer mix. Give feedback and a scheduled review rather than waiting three months to discover a mismatch. Recognize personal contribution through appropriate pay, specialist responsibility or development opportunities without inventing a promise of promotion.

Back to the research · Stars are not all alike

The sales study considered both management-job fit and the incentive value of promotion. It does not say every top salesperson is a poor manager. Separate research on NBA players who became coaches found that stars could improve team outcomes in that setting. The Hong Kong Baptist University explainer uses that contrast to ask how skills transfer between roles.

These are different settings and studies, not contradictory verdicts on Lena or Marco. The earlier 2018 account of the sales research used a larger draft sample; the case uses the final study’s 131-firm sample. No named company or real employee is being judged by the invented shop records.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–3: vote using personal sales and the new job description. Ask whether the vote rewards achievement or predicts leadership. Minutes 3–7: reveal team rates and vote again. Ask each side for an alternative explanation of the gap. Minutes 7–12: write one probation measure and one fair message to the person not appointed.

Push back on character judgments unsupported by the evidence. Ask Lena supporters why selling success should transfer; ask Marco supporters why the observational team gap should be trusted. Keep the distinction between sales and profit visible: margins are not supplied, so the case does not calculate a profit-maximizing appointment. A new test or longer assessment could be useful later, but tomorrow’s decision still requires naming one person.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for the classroom decisions is provided above. These sources document the background and are optional reading.

The shop, candidates, vacancy, records, shift conditions and appointment rules are fictional. Personal and team sales are revenue, not profit. The six-shift observations are not randomized causal evidence. The real research is a motivation for the decision, not evidence about these characters. Research publications and their explainers are distinct sources but not independent replications. Artwork depicts fictional people.

  1. Benson, Li and Shue, CEPR/VoxEU, 24 April 2019. Dated research-news hook: matching people to management roles versus rewarding current performance. Author explanation of the study below, not independent replication.
  2. Benson, Li and Shue, Promotions and the Peter Principle, accepted manuscript, 29 July 2019. Final study of 38,843 workers at 131 US firms; sales performance predicts promotion while collaboration experience helps predict management performance. Observational study, not a rule about every star.
  3. Yale Insights, Why Is My Boss Incompetent?, 20 April 2018. Interview explains promotion incentives and role differences. Earlier draft sample differs from final study; its 214-firm figure is not used in this case.
  4. Hong Kong Baptist University, From the showroom to the boardroom, 2023 series. Institutional explainer of contrasting NBA coaching research. No exact day shown; not evidence about Hong Kong retail managers.
  5. Kim and Makadok, Where the stars still shine, first published 15 March 2022. Primary NBA study of star performers becoming managers. Contextual counterpoint, not a causal estimate for the fictional shop; summarized by the preceding source.

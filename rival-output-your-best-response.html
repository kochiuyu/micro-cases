All casesMICRO CASE · ECO-35
ECO-35 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Cournot competition · Best responses · Consumer and total surplus

A rival adds capacity. Does your best quantity change?

Your quantity changes the price

When a few producers supply an identical product, one firm’s sales affect the price received by its rival. Cournot analysis makes that interdependence explicit through independently chosen quantities. A best response to fixed rival output is not the same thing as an equilibrium in which both firms remain free to choose.

Your classroom decision

You advise producer A in a fictional Hong Kong seasonal market for standardised decorative sand, sold in divisible kilograms. A and B are the only suppliers. Buyers regard their product as identical. Each firm initially chooses 0–60 kilograms independently and simultaneously before sales; neither observes the other’s choice.

Let qA and qB denote kilograms supplied and Q = qA + qB. All output sells at the same market-clearing price P = 150 − Q, in HK dollars per kilogram. Each firm’s total production cost is HK$30 times its own quantity. These schedules and capacities are invented.

There are no fixed costs, taxes, externalities, inventory carryover, quality differences or other revenues. All production is safely feasible within the stated capacities. Firms maximise their own profit, (P − 30) times their quantity. The demand schedule measures willingness to pay; market price is not an agreed price.

For the quantities relevant here, A’s profit-maximising response to qB is qA = (120 − qB)/2; B has the corresponding rule qB = (120 − qA)/2. Check that a proposed choice is nonnegative and within capacity. An equilibrium requires both choices to be best responses simultaneously.

A colleague proposes 60 kilograms: “It is our capacity, and at last season’s benchmark price of HK$70 we would earn HK$2,400.” That benchmark is not a guaranteed selling price. Your team must select A’s quantity using the model, without contacting B or agreeing on production.

Round 2 · More information

Before A commits, an independent classroom briefing changes the scenario. B has added capacity from 60 to 80 kilograms and has irrevocably committed exactly 60 kilograms to this season’s market. Treat that actual output commitment as externally given and certain, not merely an announcement of possible capacity.

A can still choose 0–60 kilograms. Demand and both firms’ HK$30 unit costs are unchanged. B cannot revise or divert its committed 60 kilograms; A cannot influence that commitment. No additional setup cost is charged in this model. Its investment motive and real-world enforceability are outside the exercise.

For the supplied demand curve, consumer surplus is one-half × Q × (150 − P): buyers’ willingness to pay above their actual spending. Total model surplus equals consumer surplus plus both firms’ profits. This narrow measure excludes distributional judgments and all effects already assumed absent.

The briefing supplies fictional facts, not confidential information obtained from a competitor. Students choose A’s output separately. They must not recommend exchanging future production intentions, agreeing quotas or coordinating prices. A commercial situation would require its own evidence and appropriate competition-law advice.

Your decision

Given B’s committed 60 kilograms, what should A produce, and what price and profits result? Compare consumer and total surplus with the initial equilibrium. Would the capacity increase alone justify the same response?

Debrief · Solve both choices together

Substitute each best-response rule into the other. The solution is qA = qB = 40 kilograms, within both initial capacities. Total output is 80 and P = 150 − 80 = HK$70. Each firm earns (70 − 30) × 40 = HK$1,600; combined profit is HK$3,200.

If A alone supplies 60 while B supplies 40, total output becomes 100 and price falls to HK$50. A earns (50 − 30) × 60 = HK$1,200, less than HK$1,600. Multiplying 60 by the old HK$40 margin incorrectly holds market price fixed while increasing market quantity.

Debrief · Respond to the commitment

With B fixed at 60, A’s best response is (120 − 60)/2 = 30 kilograms. Then Q = 90, P = HK$60, A’s profit is HK$900 and B’s is HK$1,800. Combined profit is HK$2,700. A’s capacity does not bind.

If A keeps its old 40 instead, Q = 100 and P = HK$50. A earns HK$800, so adapting to 30 improves its profit by HK$100 relative to ignoring the commitment. It still earns HK$700 less than its original HK$1,600; a best response cannot undo the changed rival output.

Debrief · Why the rule maximises profit

For fixed qB, A’s profit is (120 − qB − qA)qA. Write m = (120 − qB)/2. This equals m² − (qA − m)², so profit is highest at qA = m when that choice is feasible. With qB = 60, m = 30 and maximum profit is HK$900.

The initial equilibrium uses both firms’ rules. The revised outcome is a conditional best response to a binding commitment, not an unconstrained simultaneous-choice equilibrium. If B were free to revise against A’s 30, its own best response would be 45, not 60. We have not solved an optimal capacity-investment game.

Debrief · Capacity is not actual supply

Increasing B’s capacity to 80 alone would leave the original 40/40 equilibrium feasible and unchanged under these demand and cost assumptions. B already had enough capacity to choose 40. The revised answer depends on its binding 60-kilogram output, not on the existence of unused equipment.

An uncertain, reversible or merely advertised plan would require a different model. No probability distribution is supplied for such uncertainty. Do not substitute maximum capacity for expected sales or infer that every rival expansion warrants a production cut. The downward response here follows the specific linear-demand, constant-cost assumptions.

Debrief · Count buyers as well as producers

Initially, consumer surplus is ½ × 80 × (150 − 70) = HK$3,200. With the commitment and A’s response, it is ½ × 90 × (150 − 60) = HK$4,050, an increase of HK$850. Buyers obtain more output at a lower price.

Total model surplus rises from HK$3,200 + HK$3,200 = HK$6,400 to HK$4,050 + HK$2,700 = HK$6,750, a gain of HK$350. Combined producer profit falls HK$500. Therefore a profit decline does not establish a total-surplus decline, and A’s private objective is not the market-wide welfare criterion.

Debrief · A benchmark, not a coordination plan

With marginal cost HK$30, the model’s surplus-maximising total quantity is 120, where 150 − Q = 30. Both firms’ revised capacities can support it. Total surplus there is ½ × 120 × 120 = HK$7,200. The initial and revised surplus shortfalls are HK$800 and HK$450 respectively.

This benchmark is an analytical comparison, not an instruction for competitors to agree output. The exercise omits actual investment costs, uncertainty and wider impacts, so it does not establish that B’s expansion is socially worthwhile in reality. Nor does it authorise an agreement or establish legal compliance.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–5: solve the simultaneous choices and reject the fixed-price expansion calculation. Minutes 5–10: reveal B’s committed output and recompute A’s response. Minutes 10–15: distinguish capacity from output. Minutes 15–20: calculate consumer surplus and compare private profit with total surplus.

ECO-20 concerns lost independent customer alternatives in a proposed merger. ECO-27 concerns effort spent competing for a fixed prize. Here independent productive quantities change the market price and gains from trade. Keep the initial simultaneous game separate from the later fixed-output scenario; neither is a forecast for actual producers.

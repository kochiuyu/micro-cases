STR-16 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Ecosystem strategy · Compatibility · Product differentiation

Join the standard. Keep the distinctive feature?

A historical standards milestone

The Connectivity Standards Alliance released Matter 1.0 on 4 October 2022. Samsung Research describes its participation and explains how a shared data model supports basic interoperability across supported device types. This is an example of an Asian technology company participating in a wider ecosystem, not evidence that one interface wins every market.

The Hong Kong equipment maker, display devices, customer interviews and participation rules below are fictional. The fictional feature restriction is not a claim about Matter or Samsung.

Your classroom decision

A fictional Hong Kong maker is choosing the interface for its first tabletop exhibition-light controller. This is decorative display equipment, not emergency lighting. Both designs meet the same safety, security and reliability requirements. Either has a complete, funded support and maintenance plan. No customer has ordered and no compatibility promise has been made.

Route A joins an established shared interface used by several complementary display devices. It offers ordinary brightness and timed on/off commands through compatible controllers. Route B uses the maker’s own supplied controller and interface. It offers a distinctive smooth, precisely timed multi-light sequence requested by some designers, but cannot connect to the other ecosystem’s controllers. Both are complete products, not one safe design versus an unsafe one.

The available launch budget funds exactly one route, including its engineering, applicable testing, participation fees and support. Both are affordable at the same total launch cost. Selling price and contribution per completed order are equal; no demand forecast is supplied. A combined interface, adapter or second version would need additional engineering and a new budget. The founders must choose a target customer and first route, not assume they can serve everyone immediately.

Your decision

Choose the shared-standard route or the proprietary route for the first product. Which customer benefit would you prioritise, and what must you know about the equipment customers already use?

Round 2 · More information

Interviews with 20 deliberately selected prospective buyers reveal two groups. Twelve already own compatible ecosystem controllers. They value operating the new lights through those controls without adding another controller; ordinary brightness and timing meet their stated needs. Eight are first-time buyers with no such equipment. They value the distinctive sequence and say using the supplied proprietary controller would be acceptable. These are stated preferences from a small convenience group, not orders, market shares or conversion rates. Prices were described consistently.

A documented technical review confirms that the fictional shared profile covers ordinary brightness and timing but not this maker’s distinctive sequence. Under the fictional programme’s current participation rules, route A cannot offer that sequence in this launch. It can retain its own casing, physical controls and brand. Route B delivers the sequence but remains incompatible with the existing ecosystem controllers. There is no approved adapter, funded dual interface or promised profile update that resolves this trade-off.

Both routes still satisfy safety and support requirements at the original cost. Nothing is yet committed. The founders can target buyers adding to existing systems with A or buyers seeking the specialist sequence with B. No evidence establishes the size, willingness to pay or acquisition cost of either wider market. They must decide a first positioning and specify what to validate before making a production-volume commitment.

Your decision

Keep or change your product-route choice? Name the customer group you would serve first, the capability you would give up, and the evidence needed before forecasting sales.

A product does not work in isolation

An installed controller is a complement: it can make another compatible device more convenient to use. For the twelve interviewees who own one, A avoids a second control arrangement. This is a customer benefit, not proof that A has lower production cost or greater total profit.

Compatibility and differentiation are different dimensions

B’s sequence is a distinctive benefit for the eight first-time buyers. Their lack of existing equipment reduces the immediate value of compatibility. A is not featureless: casing, physical controls and brand remain available for differentiation. B is not automatically superior because it has an extra function.

Participation has a defined scope

The fictional profile and programme rules determine which commands this launch can share. Do not turn a broad interoperability claim into a promise that every special feature works through every controller. Conversely, do not generalise this fictional restriction into a claim that real standards always prohibit proprietary extensions.

Choose a segment, not a vote winner

A is defensible when the founders prioritise buyers expanding compatible installations. B is defensible when they prioritise a specialist sequence for buyers willing to adopt the supplied controller. Twelve versus eight does not establish a 60% versus 40% market split. Equal margins do not turn interview counts into expected contribution.

Validate the position before scaling

For A, check the actual controllers and commands in the intended segment, then verify end-to-end use. For B, test whether the sequence matters enough to justify a separate control arrangement at the stated price. In either case seek credible paid demand and costed acquisition evidence before choosing a production quantity; do not assume a future adapter will erase the choice.

Instructor note

Ask students to draw a buyer, the controller already owned, and the proposed product. Then compare that picture with a first-time buyer. Accept either route with a coherent segment and capability trade-off. The decision concerns a producer’s ecosystem participation, not a buyer’s software exit cost or the timing of an opening while waiting for a complement. No sales arithmetic, security ranking or universal standards recommendation can be inferred from the interview counts.

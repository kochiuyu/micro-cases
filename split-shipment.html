OPS-02 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Expediting · Supply-chain constraints

Fly the whole order, or just what is missing?

Hong Kong in the news · April 2024

On 25 April 2024, Hong Kong trade reporting described businesses shifting different portions of freight from sea to air during Red Sea shipping disruption. The Hong Kong Productivity Council/DHL survey found that most relevant respondents made no shift, while others moved only a fraction. These were firms’ survey responses, not shares of all Hong Kong cargo.

The operational question is not always “air or sea?” It can be “which goods must arrive early for the customer to do the job?” The following costs, goods and delivery choices are fictional, not a report of a particular exporter’s actions.

Your classroom decision

You manage logistics for a fictional Hong Kong supplier. An overseas customer ordered 1,000 welcome kits for a series of events. Each usable kit contains one cloth bag and one matching printed insert. The first event needs 300 complete kits on Monday; the remaining 700 are needed on Friday. A sea-service delay means an all-sea shipment would arrive on Friday.

All goods are finished and still in your warehouse, so you can split them before booking. Under the fictional agreed terms, fewer than 300 usable kits by Monday triggers a one-off HK$30,000 credit to the customer. The customer still accepts and pays for the full order, less that credit. Product costs and ordinary sea freight are the same baseline under every plan.

Your decision

Which plan would you authorize before noon, and why? What would you ask the customer before spending the extra freight budget?

Round 2 · One fact changes

The customer’s stock check reveals 300 compatible cloth bags left from a previous event. They have no matching inserts. The customer confirms in writing that putting your new inserts into those bags would meet Monday’s requirement and avoid the entire HK$30,000 credit. Their team can assemble 300 kits before the event.

The forwarder quotes an additional HK$9,000 above the same all-sea baseline to send only 300 inserts by air, with all remaining goods by sea on Friday. This includes splitting, handling, documentation and final delivery. The customer accepts the later delivery of your 300 unused bags as well. A door-to-door plan and space are available for the inserts under the same arrival assumptions as the other quotes. No prices or deadlines otherwise change.

Your decision

Would you revise the shipment? Calculate the saving against your first choice, then name the checks needed before you promise 300 usable kits on Monday.

Debrief · Pay for useful early output

In the first round, sending 300 complete kits costs HK$24,000 instead of the HK$30,000 credit: a HK$6,000 saving under the stated delivery assumptions. Sending all 1,000 by air spends HK$36,000 more than that split plan without serving an additional Monday need. All-sea is defensible only if additional risks or constraints overturn the stated comparison; those would need evidence.

After the stock check, airfreighting only the 300 missing inserts costs HK$9,000 and still avoids the credit. It saves HK$15,000 against the 300-complete-kit plan, or HK$21,000 against all-sea. It is HK$51,000 cheaper than airfreighting everything. These are incremental comparisons, not the order’s profit: the full selling price and baseline production cost are not supplied.

Find the missing component

The useful output is 300 complete kits at the customer on Monday, not 300 cartons dispatched or 1,000 bags flown. Available compatible bags mean inserts are the missing component. Faster movement of already-available bags does not add usable kits. Confirm that the stock count, compatibility and assembly capacity are real; otherwise the apparent bottleneck has been misidentified.

Written acceptance matters. Using the customer’s existing bags must actually satisfy the agreed requirement; the supplier cannot unilaterally redefine delivery or assume that partial goods avoid a credit. The other 700 kits and the 300 extra bags must still arrive as agreed on Friday.

Verify the whole journey

Check pickup cutoff, confirmed uplift, export and import documentation, clearance time, destination delivery and a named recipient. Confirm the customer can assemble before the event. Track arrival at the customer, not just departure from Hong Kong. An air booking is not an unconditional guarantee.

The case supplies arrival assumptions to isolate the allocation decision, not to eliminate real logistics uncertainty. Agree escalation and a fallback with the customer if the inserts miss their milestone. If delay risk differs across plans, compare expected freight and credit costs using credible probabilities rather than treating every air option as equally reliable. No such probabilities are supplied here.

Back to the evidence

The Hong Kong trade survey illustrates partial rather than all-or-nothing shifts from sea to air. The trade-news article reports the same survey; it is not independent replication. Its historical responses do not provide today’s rates, route capacity or delivery promises.

DHL’s process guidance explains why pickup, documentation, clearance and final delivery matter alongside the flight. ACCA’s constraint discussion helps frame the missing-component question: speed is valuable where it increases completed usable output. This case uses ordinary incremental costs, not a full throughput-accounting model.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–3: choose one quoted plan and calculate total extra cost. Minutes 3–7: reveal the stock check, then compare HK$9,000 with the chosen plan rather than automatically claiming HK$21,000 saved. Minutes 7–12: draw a bag-plus-insert kit and list the steps between a warehouse dispatch and 300 usable kits at the customer.

Ask advocates of “air everything” what the extra 700 early kits achieve. Ask advocates of “send the inserts” what evidence makes the bags usable and the agreement valid. Challenge claims of guaranteed arrival. The credit is a stipulated classroom agreement, not a legal remedy or a typical market tariff.

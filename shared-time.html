PPL-06 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · People management · Hybrid work · Team coordination

Choose your days. Coordinate your work.

In the news · Designing hybrid work

On 15 February 2022, Trip.com Group announced a hybrid-work policy beginning that March. The historical announcement described remote work at specified times, subject to management discretion and local adaptation, rather than an unrestricted choice of any days.

A later randomized study of 1,612 employees in a Chinese technology company reported better retention without deterioration in the measured performance outcomes under its particular hybrid arrangement. Neither source establishes the best schedule for every team. Our design studio below is fictional.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A six-person Hong Kong product-design team is planning a hybrid-work trial. Everyone can work remotely two days a week. Much of the drawing, costing and documentation can be completed independently, with files shared online.

The team leader proposes letting each person choose their own days. Colleagues welcome the ability to plan commuting and focused work. Another manager wants everyone in on the same three days, saying this would make collaboration easier.

You are advising the leader before next week’s schedule is finalised. There is no evidence that anyone is working less effectively remotely. The question is how to organise the work, not how to measure commitment by visibility. The trial allows schedules to be agreed with colleagues; no individual’s availability constraints have yet been discussed.

Your decision

Would you let colleagues choose days independently, set common office days, or propose another arrangement? What work and employee constraints would you check first?

Round 2 · One fact changes

Next week’s client sample must be dispatched on Wednesday morning. Before dispatch, the designer and production liaison must jointly check the fit and tactile finish of the same physical prototype. In this fictional workflow, photos and a video call do not capture those properties adequately; both specialists must handle the sample during the review.

The prototype becomes available on Monday. Under their draft schedules, the designer is in the studio on Monday, Thursday and Friday; the liaison is in on Tuesday, Thursday and Friday. Their first planned overlap is Thursday, after dispatch. The other four colleagues are not needed for this review. No one has yet been asked whether a Monday or Tuesday adjustment is workable.

Your decision

What should the leader agree with the two colleagues before confirming next week’s schedule? Does this dependency justify requiring all six people to share three office days every week?

Start with the dependency, not the attendance rule

The task needs two named roles, one physical sample and a review before Wednesday morning. The current overlap on Thursday is too late. The problem is a missed coordination requirement, not proof that either employee is uncommitted or that remote work has failed.

Agree a suitable joint session on Monday or Tuesday if the colleagues can accommodate it. State the review’s purpose, inputs and completion deadline. A purposeful block may solve the dependency without making both people spend an unnecessary extra whole day in the studio.

Consult before changing the calendar

Talk with both colleagues about availability and practical constraints before confirming a change. Give notice and explore whether office and remote time can be rearranged within the trial. Do not promise a time before checking that it works for the people and the sample.

If neither can adjust, examine whether the review method, sample access or dispatch plan can be changed without weakening the required checks. An alternative is valid only if it genuinely satisfies the task. Simply declaring a video call equivalent contradicts the stated limitation.

There is not enough information to select a specific meeting time or guarantee the deadline. Escalate the unresolved dependency early rather than silently sending an unchecked sample.

Keep flexibility where the work permits it

This one review involves two people, not all six. It does not establish a permanent need for three common days. Preserve flexibility for independent tasks while agreeing how shared work will be identified and scheduled.

At the start of each planning cycle, list tasks needing joint time, the roles involved, necessary materials and deadlines. Agree those blocks first, then let colleagues plan the remaining work within the trial’s rules. Publish the work schedule, not private explanations for individual constraints.

Evaluate delivery, not desk visibility

Review whether samples were checked on time, handoffs were usable and colleagues had enough notice and uninterrupted work time. Ask what arrangements helped or caused friction. Office attendance alone does not show work quality, commitment or suitability for promotion.

Keep the trial open to adjustment. A team with frequent shared physical tasks may need a different pattern from one doing mostly independent digital work. Neither individual choice nor a common-day rule is universally best.

Instructor note

Ask students to defend both autonomy and predictable overlap before the reveal. Then make them specify who needs to be together, with what, by when. Thursday is a real overlap, but it misses the Wednesday dispatch.

A strong response proposes consultation and a task-specific adjustment, with an honest contingency if it is infeasible. It should neither order all six into the office nor dismiss the physical-review requirement.

This is a fictional work-design discussion, not a real employee assessment, change to an employment contract or statement of Hong Kong employment law.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for the classroom decisions is provided above. These sources document the background and are optional reading.

The team, trial, prototype, schedules and deadline are invented. The joint physical review requirement is a case assumption, not a claim that all design collaboration must occur in person. Employee constraints and adjustment feasibility are deliberately not supplied. Sources checked 14 November 2026. Trip.com’s 2022 release is historical, not verification of its current rules. The Nature study was read through its public author abstract and disclosures on PubMed, not the full article; its particular arrangement does not establish an optimal schedule for this studio. Co-author James Liang’s Trip.com leadership and equity interest are disclosed in that record. UK and practitioner guidance is used for work-design questions, not imported as Hong Kong law.

  1. Trip.com Group, hybrid-work policy announcement, 15 February 2022. Historical primary company announcement of implementation from March 2022, with specified times and local adaptation. Promotional survey claims are not treated as universal evidence.
  2. Nicholas Bloom, Ruobing Han and James Liang, Nature, published online 12 June 2024; author abstract on PubMed. Six-month randomized trial of 1,612 employees in 2021–2022. The accessible abstract reports retention and measured performance results; not proof that freely chosen or common days are universally optimal.
  3. Acas, Things to consider: home and hybrid working policies, updated 22 May 2025. UK guidance considers roles, employee needs, workplace tasks, core times and client expectations. Used as practical prompts, not Hong Kong legal requirements.
  4. CIPD / Flexible Working Taskforce, Hybrid Working Practical Guidance, November 2021. Printed pages 7–10 discuss team agreements, scheduling autonomy and shared working patterns. The document explicitly states it is not government guidance.
  5. Microsoft WorkLab, How to Make In-Person Time Count, undated. Practitioner guidance on purposeful shared time and experimenting with arrangements. Not a controlled comparison of all schedules or Microsoft’s current employment policy.

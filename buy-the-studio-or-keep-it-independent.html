STR-43 · Hong Kong and the Greater Bay Area · 15 minutes · Potential entry · Acquisition strategy · Counterfactual · Cannibalisation

Buy the studio. Buy a future without a rival?

A proposed deal raised concerns about future competition

In July 2024, Singapore’s competition authority provisionally raised concerns about Grab’s proposed acquisition of Trans-cab, including rival platforms’ access to drivers. The parties withdrew the plan and the authority closed its assessment. The fictional exercise below is about a different industry and concerns potential entry, not the outcome of that legal review.

Your classroom decision

Jade Gauge, a fictional Hong Kong industrial sensor manufacturer, is considering buying a small GBA calibration-equipment studio. The studio designs a portable reader that could complement Jade’s existing hardware. They could instead enter a nonexclusive distribution agreement.

Round 2 · More information

The studio may obtain funding to turn its reader into a complete competing system within two years. If independent, it has a 40% chance of doing so. This would reduce Jade’s existing contribution by 100 whether Jade signs the nonexclusive agreement or walks away. The agreement does not prevent entry or change the distribution contribution.

If bought, the studio will not launch a separate competing system. Treat all other values as unchanged. These are hypothetical scenarios, not claims that buyers could restrict a real firm’s entry.

Your decision

Which route now adds more private contribution compared with a realistic walk-away baseline? At what entry probability does Jade switch its private preference, and what would you ask to distinguish a gain from operating improvements from a gain due to less competition?

Debrief · Count replacement sales once

Acquisition adds 210 + 150 − 110 − 80 − 120 = 50. Distribution adds 90 − 25 = 65. If the studio is not a future rival, distribution adds 15 more.

Debrief · Start from what happens without a deal

An independent launch would cost Jade 100 with probability 0.40, so walk-away is now −40 relative to the original no-entry forecast. Distribution is 65 − 40 = 25 on that same scale and still adds 65 over the revised walk-away case.

Debrief · Identify what creates the private lead

Acquisition remains 50 on the original scale and adds 90 above the revised walk-away case. Its private advantage over distribution is 25. The two routes tie at 50 = 65 − 100 × probability, or a 15% chance of independent entry.

Takeaway · An avoided competitor is not an operating saving

The switch comes from avoiding expected rivalry, not from better production or new engineering benefits. Ask for evidence about the studio’s funding and ability to launch, alternative suppliers, and whether either route improves buyer choice or service. The example gives no legal conclusion.

Instructor note

Allow 15 minutes: 3 to read the initial estimates, 4 for the first comparison, 5 to revise the baseline and 3 to distinguish the reasons for the buyer’s choice. Ask each group to explain whose payoff is missing.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All firms, costs, probabilities, contracts and forecasts in the exercise are invented. The historical Singapore proposal had a provisional assessment and was withdrawn. It is not a precedent or legal conclusion for the hypothetical deal.

  1. Singapore CCCS, provisional assessment of the proposed Grab–Trans-cab deal (2024). Primary statement about possible limits on rivals’ access to important supply, limited to its proposed transaction.
  2. Singapore CCCS, closes assessment after proposed deal is withdrawn (2024). Ensures the history does not imply the proposal closed or was finally decided.
  3. Singapore CCCS, Grab–Uber infringement decision announcement (2018). Primary record of a separate completed transaction and analysis of the competitive landscape; it is not the Trans-cab proposal.
  4. US DOJ and FTC, merger guideline on potential entry. First-party source for the conceptual distinction between potential entry and competition from firms already in the market; not Hong Kong law.
  5. Hong Kong Competition Commission, Guideline on the Merger Rule. Official scope context; do not apply overseas assessment outcomes to fictional Hong Kong industrial equipment.

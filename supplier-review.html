GVR-03 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Conflicts of interest · Procurement governance

The best quote. The right reviewer?

Hong Kong in the news · August 2026

On 13 August 2026, Hong Kong’s ICAC reported that a kitchenware supplier had pleaded guilty to offering a restaurant purchasing manager a kickback tied to orders. The report concerned an admitted bribery offence; it was not merely a family relationship or a difference of opinion about a quotation. No later sentencing outcome is used here.

Procurement integrity also requires managing situations that are not proven corruption. The following café group, quotations and family relationship are fictional. They are not the facts of the reported prosecution, and no wrongdoing should be inferred simply from the relationship.

Your classroom decision

You own a small fictional Hong Kong café group. It needs reusable serving trays. Three suppliers have quoted for the same quantity and written specification. The purchasing manager’s evaluation says all three meet the quality requirements and can deliver within three weeks. Supplier A is cheapest, and the manager recommends it.

Your existing procedure is to verify compliance and delivery capability, then choose the lowest compliant total quote unless a documented business reason supports another choice. You approve the award; the manager prepares the evaluation. The quotation window lasts five more working days, so a focused independent check can fit. No order has been placed.

Your decision

Would you approve A on this record, request a focused check first, or choose another supplier? State the evidence and approval record you need, not just a preference for the lowest or highest price.

Round 2 · One fact changes

Before you sign, the purchasing manager declares that a sibling owns a substantial stake in Supplier A. The manager also selected the samples and scored their quality. This gives the owner a conflict to manage before the award. It does not establish that A is unsuitable or that a bribe occurred.

There is no evidence of a gift, hidden payment, false quotation or defective tray. Prices and delivery promises have not changed. Another experienced colleague with no supplier connection can review the specification, sample selection, scoring and delivery evidence within two working days. You retain final approval and must document the decision.

Your decision

Would you exclude A, approve A with the declaration on file, or pause the award for independent review? Who should perform that review, and could A still legitimately win?

Debrief · Change the process, not the conclusion in advance

A is HK$6,000 cheaper than B and HK$9,000 cheaper than C. Those differences remain real offers, but the new relationship affects confidence in the evaluation process. A declaration is important information; filing it without considering the manager’s influence does not by itself make the process independent.

A proportionate response is to pause the award briefly, record the declaration and have the unconnected colleague review the existing criteria and evidence. The manager should not evaluate or approve this award and should not privately steer the replacement reviewer. Necessary factual clarifications can be documented through the independent reviewer.

Check the earlier stages too

Review more than the final price table. Check whether specifications or selected samples favoured A, whether all suppliers were judged consistently, and whether delivery capability is supported. Preserve the original record and explain any correction. Do not invent new criteria merely to exclude A or leak competing quotations to help a supplier improve its bid.

If the review supports the original assessment, A may still be the best compliant offer under the unchanged procedure. If evidence is missing or criteria were compromised, correct the process and obtain appropriate further evidence before awarding. Paying more to B is not automatically proof of integrity; ignoring the conflict to save HK$6,000 is not adequate governance either.

A conflict is not a verdict

Keep conflict management separate from punishment. A close association can create a real or perceived risk to impartiality without proving corruption. Give the manager and supplier a fair opportunity to clarify relevant facts through the documented process, while protecting bid confidentiality.

If actual evidence of misconduct appears, preserve it and escalate through the company’s appropriate compliance or legal channels; do not turn a routine review into a public accusation. For future awards, obtain declarations before staff shape specifications or select samples, and separate evaluation from approval where practical. Controls should fit the size and risk of the purchase.

Back to the evidence

The reported kitchenware case involved an admitted offer of a kickback. That is materially different from the fictional disclosed relationship, where no improper advantage or false document is established. ICAC guidance distinguishes conflicts from the additional circumstances that can constitute corruption or fraud.

ICAC’s conflict-management guidance recommends declaration and proportionate mitigation, with reasons and actions recorded. CIPS professional guidance also supports impartial criteria, confidentiality and separation of duties. These principles guide the classroom discussion; they do not supply a legal verdict on a fictional person or make every close relationship an offence.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–3: choose an award path using the quotation record. Minutes 3–7: reveal the relationship and ask whether the problem is supplier price, product quality, reviewer independence or several of these. Minutes 7–12: assign the review and approval roles and state the conditions under which A could still win.

Challenge “just disclose it” with influence over earlier specifications and samples. Challenge “ban A automatically” with the absence of evidence about its actual performance and the unchanged criteria. Challenge “cheapest wins” with how compliance was established. Do not ask students to decide criminal guilt or recommend discipline based solely on a relationship.

STR-03 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Strategy · Make or buy · Core capabilities

The cheaper batch and the prototype bench

In the news · A mixed production model

In an interim-results announcement dated 29 August 2025, Hong Kong-listed China International Development Corporation described a customised-products line launched in the second quarter. It said the line combined in-house and outsourced manufacturing to respond to personalised orders.

This is management’s description of its approach, not independent proof of success. It raises a useful question: which activities should a business buy, and which capabilities should it keep?

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. You run a small Hong Kong workshop making fabric equipment pouches for business customers. Some clients reorder an established design; others ask you to develop unusual compartment layouts.

An established supplier offers a lower price for repeat production. Its samples meet your specifications. Your co-owner proposes outsourcing all production next year and converting the prototype bench to storage. The salaried team would handle sales, design coordination and supplier oversight; no redundancies are proposed.

One repeat batch of 1,000 pouchesCost
Make internally: avoidable materials and batch-processing costsHK$50,000
Buy from supplier: delivered, inspected batchHK$40,000

Both options meet the frozen design, quality standard and delivery date. The supplier accepts this batch size. These comparable batch costs exclude unchanged rent and salaried staff costs. They are not total business profit.

Your decision

Outsource every order, keep making everything, or split the work? What must remain under your control?

Round 2 · One fact changes

The order review separates two kinds of work. Fourteen of the last 20 jobs were repeat designs; six required new compartment layouts. These are job counts, not revenue shares or a forecast.

For a changed design, the workshop can produce each physical sample in one working day. The supplier quotes seven working days per revision. Current custom clients require a revised sample within two working days; more than one revision is common. Once approved, a design can move to the supplier for a qualifying repeat batch.

Keeping the prototype bench ready next year requires a separate HK$12,000 maintenance-and-tool budget, plus staff time already on salary. Converting it to storage would remove that quick-sampling capability. No custom-order revenue or margin forecast has yet been prepared.

Your decision

Would you keep the prototype bench? Choose the customer promise your production plan can actually support.

What to notice

The supplier is genuinely cheaper for the specified repeat batch: HK$10,000 less. That does not show that outsourcing every activity serves the same customers.

Making a physical sample quickly is a capability, not just another unit of production. It lets the team test fit with a customer, revise the layout and learn what works. Drawings and supplier coordination may not replace that feedback loop at the same speed.

Three defensible routes

Standard designs at a lower cost: outsource production and deliberately stop promising rapid custom revisions. Check demand for that narrower offer and honour existing commitments before changing it.

Rapid customisation: retain the bench and consider buying repeat batches after designs are approved. Cost the retained activity and check whether customers value it enough to cover its resources. Splitting work adds handoff and oversight tasks; it is not automatically best.

Keep all production: explain what additional flexibility or control is worth the higher repeat-batch cost. Do not defend every internal activity merely because it has always been done that way.

A decision you can defend

For the current two-day custom promise, keep the bench while evaluating a split production plan. The supplier’s seven-day revision cycle cannot deliver that promise. But do not call the bench profitable without a custom-order forecast.

Before approving the annual plan, compare expected custom contribution after its costs, staff capacity and opportunity cost, the HK$12,000 budget, supplier minimum batches and actual handoff performance. If a standard-only proposition is the better business, full outsourcing remains a legitimate strategic choice.

Key idea: choose the customer promise first, then decide which capabilities must support it. A cheaper batch does not settle the boundary of the business.

Instructor note

Use 2 minutes for the opening vote, 3 for the revision-time reveal and 5–7 for comparing customer promises. Ask one group to defend standard-only outsourcing and another to defend retaining custom capability. Do not let the HK$10,000 batch saving become an invented annual-profit forecast. Close by asking which missing evidence would change each recommendation.

ACC-31 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Equivalent units · Process costing · Work in progress

Half finished. How many units of work?

One physical count, two measures of work

Process-costing education distinguishes physical output from the work represented by unfinished output. Materials and conversion can enter at different stages, so a single completion percentage need not describe both.

The company, process and all figures below are fictional classroom assumptions. This is a simplified cost-reporting exercise, not evidence about a real cosmetics manufacturer or its safety procedures.

Your classroom decision

You are the controller of fictional Harbour Skin, a small Hong Kong skincare producer. At month-end, 1,000 identical bottles have entered one production stage: 800 are complete and transferred out; 200 remain unfinished. There is no opening work in progress, loss or spoilage.

All direct materials are added at the start. Conversion means labour and production overhead, incurred evenly as this stage progresses. The supervisor initially certifies the unfinished bottles as 70% converted. Company policy allocates each cost pool using equivalent completed work, not an equal cost per physical bottle.

A draft report instead divides the HK$19,400 total cost by all 1,000 bottles and assigns HK$3,880 to unfinished stock. Before signing it, decide whether that allocation fits the supplied policy.

Round 2 · More information

A review of the same month-end stage records shows that the unfinished bottles were only 40% converted, not 70%. Use the corrected evidence. Their materials are still fully added. The physical counts and the HK$10,000 materials and HK$9,400 conversion cost pools do not change.

This is a correction to the completion estimate for the same reporting date, not a later production step or a cash refund. Recalculate the conversion rate as well as unfinished equivalent units.

Your decision

With conversion completion corrected to 40%, should unfinished stock keep its first-round value or change? Recalculate both unfinished and transferred-out costs.

Debrief · Count work separately

Replace the equal-per-bottle allocation. Materials equivalent units are 800 + 200 = 1,000; the materials rate is HK$10 per equivalent unit. Conversion equivalent units are 800 + 200 × 70% = 940; the conversion rate is HK$10.

Unfinished stock carries HK$2,000 of materials and HK$1,400 of conversion: HK$3,400. Transferred-out cost is 800 × (10 + 10) = HK$16,000. Together they reconcile to HK$19,400. Physical counts also reconcile: 800 + 200 = 1,000.

Debrief · Recalculate the denominator

At 40% completion, conversion equivalent units become 800 + 80 = 880. The revised conversion rate is 9,400 ÷ 880 = HK$10.681818…; retain precision until final totals.

Unfinished cost is 2,000 + 80 × 10.681818… = HK$2,854.55. Transferred-out cost is 8,000 + 800 × 10.681818… = HK$16,545.45. These rounded totals still sum to HK$19,400.

The unfinished value falls by HK$545.45, not HK$600: the conversion rate also changes. No spending disappeared. A smaller measured amount of work now bears the same conversion cost pool. This alone does not establish inefficiency, misconduct or a change in cash.

What the report can and cannot establish

Equivalent units measure completed work for cost allocation; they are not additional physical bottles or permission to ship unfinished products. Reconciliation checks arithmetic, not whether the supervisor’s completion estimate is reliable. Ask what stage records or inspection evidence support that percentage.

The weighted-average approach is used here. With no opening work in progress, there is no prior-period cost to blend. Different materials-entry patterns, losses or multiple departments would require additional analysis; they are deliberately excluded.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–4: vote before calculation. Ask supporters of equal allocation whether every bottle has received the same conversion work. Minutes 4–9: pairs calculate separate equivalent units, rates and allocations. Minutes 9–14: reveal the 40% correction and ask for a second vote. Minutes 14–18: reconcile both cost pools and discuss the evidence behind completion.

Ask students who keep the original conversion rate to identify which denominator changed. Ask those who infer a cash saving to locate a changed payment in the exhibit; none exists. Keep the distinction from ACC-22 explicit: this is partial-stage measurement, not producing extra inventory to alter absorption profit.

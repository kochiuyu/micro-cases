ACC-02 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Cost allocation · Avoidable costs

Close the loss-making branch?

Hong Kong in the news · July 2025

Tsui Wah’s annual report filed on 14 July 2025 described closing two Hong Kong restaurants and opening four after reviewing its network during the year ended 31 March 2025. A network review can involve both expansion and withdrawal. But does a branch’s reported loss tell you what the whole company would gain by closing it?

Your classroom decision

You manage a fictional Hong Kong stationery-and-printing chain. The lease decision for East Branch is due tomorrow. Its next-quarter forecast now shows a HK$30,000 loss after a change in the rule used to share head-office costs among branches. Its sales and branch-level costs have not changed in the forecast.

The chief executive asks whether East should close at the end of this quarter rather than operate next quarter. You must make a preliminary recommendation, then identify the facts needed to confirm it. All figures below are fictional; they are not Tsui Wah branch accounts.

Same next-quarter forecast · HK$ · two allocation rules
ItemOld ruleNew rule
Sales600,000600,000
Branch operating costs450,000450,000
Branch rent100,000100,000
Allocated head-office costs40,00080,000
Reported branch result10,000−30,000

“Allocated” means head office assigns a share of common costs to this branch. The table does not yet tell you which payments would stop if East closes.

Your decision

Close East or keep it for next quarter? Give your provisional recommendation and name the cost information you need before acting.

Round 2 · One fact changes

The finance team confirms that total head-office expenditure will remain HK$200,000 next quarter whether East stays open or closes. East’s HK$80,000 allocation would simply be reassigned to the remaining branches. This is future expenditure still to be paid, not a sunk cost.

If East closes now, all HK$450,000 of branch operating costs and HK$100,000 rent will be avoided next quarter. The lease expires this quarter and can end without penalty. Assume there are no other closure costs, no sales transferred to another branch, no other effects on the chain and no feasible alternative use generating income next quarter. These simplifying assumptions have been checked for this fictional decision.

Your decision

Does closing East improve the whole company’s next-quarter result? Calculate the change, then say whether East’s manager became a worse performer merely because the allocation changed.

Debrief · A charge moved, not a cost saved

Compare the whole company with and without East. Keeping East brings HK$600,000 of sales and HK$550,000 of costs that disappear if it closes. East therefore adds HK$50,000 toward common costs. Closing saves HK$550,000 but loses HK$600,000: the company’s result becomes HK$50,000 worse next quarter. The unchanged HK$200,000 head-office total is present in both alternatives and cancels in the comparison.

Moving a charge is not removing an expense. The new allocation lowers East’s reported result by HK$40,000, from a HK$10,000 profit to a HK$30,000 loss, without changing the forecast of its activity or the group’s head-office bill. If East closes, the remaining branches receive larger charges. Treating that reassignment as a cash saving would confuse the reporting system with the operating decision.

Use different measures for different questions. Central costs are real and must be funded; allocation can support full-cost reporting and longer-run planning. For this closure decision, ask which revenues and costs actually change. For assessing the manager, distinguish what the manager controls from charges reassigned by head office. The allocation change alone is not evidence of weaker managerial performance.

Under the supplied next-quarter assumptions, keep East rather than close it. That does not prove East should operate forever. A feasible alternative use, different demand, genuinely avoidable central resources or further costs could change a later decision. Do not assume a cost is avoidable just because it is labelled direct, or unavoidable just because it is labelled fixed.

Back to the news · A different decision for each branch

Tsui Wah’s report supplies a real example of network changes, not evidence that any real closure arose from an allocation error. The May 2025 provisional restaurant release also described modest sector-wide pressure; aggregate industry data cannot identify an individual branch’s profitability. This fictional shop case makes the accounting distinction visible using deliberately explicit assumptions.

Unlike a sunk-cost question about money already spent, the common cost here is a future payment. It is irrelevant to the difference between these two next-quarter alternatives only because the same amount remains in both. If a genuine portion could be removed, it would belong in the comparison.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–3: vote using the report and ask each side which entries might change after closure. Minutes 3–7: reveal the finance team’s checks and calculate the whole-company difference. Minutes 7–12: ask whether to change the operating decision, the manager’s performance assessment or both.

If students say closing saves HK$80,000, ask who pays the head-office bill afterward. If they say allocations never matter, ask how the group pays for shared resources over time. Require them to distinguish the forecast branch result from the incremental group result. Avoid inventing customer migration or lease penalties: those could matter in real decisions, but they are explicitly excluded here. Ask which assumption they would verify first before using the exercise in a real company.

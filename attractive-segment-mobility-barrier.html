STR-28 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Strategic groups · Mobility barriers · Reputation and access

Enter the attractive segment. Cross which barrier?

Being able to deliver is not the same as being considered

Strategy research distinguishes entry into an industry from movement between groups within it. Reputation and relationships may affect which buyers consider a supplier, even when its technical work is adequate. Public procurement guidance also illustrates why buyers verify relevant experience. The private market, requirements and figures below are invented, not rules for Hong Kong tenders.

Your classroom decision

A Hong Kong exhibition-graphics service firm supplies standard displays to local exhibitors. Its manager has HK$60,000 and one twelve-week business-development slot. A uses both to improve repeat-client account service in its current segment. B uses both to seek access to specialist multi-site brand programmes. Existing contracts, operating staff and technical quality controls remain funded under either option. Neither plan guarantees an additional sale.

A completed demonstration confirms that the firm can already produce and coordinate the specialist work to the required technical standard. B is therefore not a proposal to buy missing equipment or invent a new delivery routine. Its budget pays for buyer research, qualification preparation and legitimate partner development, not a promised purchase of referrals. Before choosing, the manager has not yet checked specialist buyers’ qualification requirements.

An illustrative comparison of completed projects shows a 20% gross margin in the current segment and 35% among established specialist suppliers. Both percentages use revenue less direct delivery costs, divided by revenue. They exclude business-development costs, central overhead and the cost of building a reputation. Project sizes, volumes and future win rates are unknown. A could deepen a proven position; B could begin a valuable move, but the margin comparison alone does not rank either investment’s return.

A: HK$60,000 and twelve weeks on current-client account service. B: HK$60,000 and twelve weeks on specialist access development. Illustrative incumbent gross margins: current segment 20%; specialist segment 35%. Technical delivery is adequate for both. The figures are not entrant profit forecasts.

Your decision

Would you fund A or B this quarter? Explain what the margin comparison tells you, what it leaves out, and which access condition you would check before treating the specialist segment as an attainable opportunity.

Round 2 · More information

A preliminary buyer check, completed before either budget is spent, finds that the target specialist buyers require two independently verifiable completed multi-site programme references and an introduction from an approved exhibition-planning partner before considering a new lead supplier. These are the stated practices of the fictional buyers being targeted, not universal industry rules. The firm has neither accepted programme references nor an approved referring relationship. Its successful technical demonstration does not substitute for either.

One approved planner is willing to discuss a transparently attributed subcontracting route, but has not offered a project or referral. The buyers say relevant subcontracting work may count if its scope and the firm’s contribution can be independently verified. The earliest feasible completed reference from the opportunities currently known would be after six months, outside the twelve-week slot. No alternative direct-access route, acquisition or transferable qualification has been validated. Paying for an introduction would not manufacture completed work.

B can still fund twelve weeks of qualification mapping and partner development, followed by a stop-or-continue review. It cannot credibly promise qualified lead-supplier access or specialist revenue within those twelve weeks. A remains feasible at its original budget. Existing work stays protected, and there is no evidence that the current segment is about to disappear. The manager must choose between strengthening today’s position and beginning a longer, uncertain move, not between a good technical team and a bad one.

Your decision

Which path would you fund now? Identify the mobility barrier, explain why more technical spending would not remove it, and set a concrete continuation test if you choose to develop specialist access.

Name the strategic groups

The comparison concerns two positions within exhibition-graphics services: standard local-exhibitor work and specialist multi-site brand programmes. Customers, channels and credibility requirements differ. It is not simply a choice between two prices for the same freely accessible order. A mobility barrier makes movement between positions costly, slow or uncertain; it need not make movement permanently impossible.

Answer the first decision

Either initial choice can be defended if its assumptions are explicit. A prioritises deeper service in an accessible segment; B buys investigation and relationship development for a potentially attractive future position. The 15-percentage-point incumbent gross-margin gap is a screening signal, not an entrant return estimate. Check relevant references, referral access, development costs, likely volume and timing before projecting profit.

Answer the revealed decision

I would choose A for this quarter because no validated route can deliver qualified access within the current development window, while current-client work remains viable. This is a timing and opportunity-cost judgement, not proof that B has negative long-run value. B is also defensible as an explicitly longer-horizon investment if the owners accept uncertain continuation funding and no near-term specialist revenue. Its goal must be learning and a credible route, not a twelve-week launch claim.

Build evidence rather than buy a label

For B, require written confirmation of what subcontracting evidence the buyers accept, a feasible partner-led project opportunity with honest attribution, and a costed path to two completed references plus an approved introduction. At twelve weeks, continue only if the route and its timing are credible and its expected incremental benefits justify the remaining cost and displaced current-segment work. Otherwise pause. The planner’s willingness to talk is not a sale, referral or accepted reference. Preserve confidentiality and obtain permission before using client evidence.

Instructor note

Ask students to draw the barrier between supplier groups, not between competent and incompetent production teams. Technical quality is already adequate; the reveal concerns buyer consideration, verifiable history and relationship access. Unlike a missing organisational routine, extra technical spending does not solve this case’s diagnosed gap. Avoid treating every buyer restriction as desirable or lawful: the case describes fictional private practices, not their legal endorsement. Challenge both “higher margin means enter” and “a barrier means never enter”.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All firms, buyer rules, completed demonstration, costs, margins and timeframes are original fiction. Each path uses HK$60,000 and the same twelve-week management slot, not both paths together. The preliminary check and demonstration predate that allocation. 35% minus 20% equals 15 percentage points; 35/20 = 1.75 is a ratio of margin rates, not a profit-growth prediction. Six months exceeds twelve weeks. No order value, volume, conversion probability, net present value or guaranteed route is supplied. Two references plus an approved introduction are the target buyers’ hypothetical conditions; subcontracting may count only with verified contribution. Neither technical quality nor buying publicity creates a completed history. Welsh guidance is a dated 2020 illustration; New Zealand guidance is a separate institutional example. Neither governs these private Hong Kong buyers. Scholarly models supply concepts, not forecasts for this firm.

  1. Caves and Porter: From Entry Barriers to Mobility Barriers, 1977. Original article, publicly rendered full text. Develops group-specific mobility barriers within industries; does not prescribe this fictional decision.
  2. Dierickx and Cool: Asset Stock Accumulation and Sustainability of Competitive Advantage, 1989. Original conceptual article. Explains why some reputation and trust assets require accumulation over time rather than instant purchase.
  3. Podolny: A Status-Based Model of Market Competition, 1993. Original article with underwriting-market evidence; distinguishes quality from perceived position and relational access. Sector evidence is not transferred as a graphics-market estimate.
  4. Welsh Government: Supplier selection procurement advice note, 2020. Dated primary guidance discusses relevant experience, verifiable references and proportionate criteria. Historical illustration, not current Hong Kong law.
  5. New Zealand Government Procurement: Conducting due diligence checks. Primary guidance distinguishes claims from verified capability and past performance, using references and multiple evidence sources. Not a requirement for the fictional private buyers.

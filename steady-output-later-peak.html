OPS-17 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Aggregate planning · Workforce continuity · Inventory and backlog

Steady output. Or follow the peak?

Capacity is also people

On 22 September 2026, Hong Kong’s Labour and Welfare Bureau published an updated manpower projection, describing continued pressures in several occupations. It is a broad workforce-planning context, not a forecast for the workshop below. All orders, capacities, costs and staffing arrangements in this case are original fiction.

Your classroom decision

A fictional Hong Kong workshop makes one unchanged standard storage box. Before a six-month cycle begins, it must choose between two prepared plans. L retains five trained production positions and makes 100 boxes every month. C follows monthly orders by varying trained positions, with no stock or late delivery. One filled position provides 20 boxes per month and costs HK$20,000 for that month, whether busy or idle. Both start with five positions and must restore five for the next cycle. Materials and other common costs are funded and identical for 600 boxes; ignore them in this comparison.

The workshop has lawful, voluntarily agreed agency-assignment arrangements. Adding a qualified position costs HK$3,000, including paid induction completed one month before it is needed. Ending an assignment costs HK$2,000, including all required notice and contractual payments. Alternative assignments, safe working limits, training and employee rights are protected. All staffing changes must be arranged in advance; neither plan relies on instant hiring or unpaid downtime. These simplified cost quotes are teaching assumptions, not employment-law advice or actual wage rates.

Starting finished stock and backlog are zero. Month-end finished stock costs HK$100 per box per month and cannot exceed 40 boxes. A customer has expressly accepted that up to 20 boxes due in month 5 may instead arrive in month 6, with an all-in HK$1,000 payment per delayed box; no other late delivery is allowed. This is an agreed optional service variation, not permission to break a promise. Production occurs before month-end dispatch; fulfil earlier backlog first. Both plans end with zero stock and backlog. Compare the two prepared plans, not a claimed global optimum.

Round 2 · More information

Still before any staffing changes or production begin, the customer moves 60 boxes from month 5 to month 6. Orders now read 80, 80, 140, 80, 80, 140. The total remains 600. All changes can still be planned with the required training lead time; there are no sunk adjustment costs. The original optional delayed-delivery allowance is no longer needed, and all revised orders must arrive in their revised months.

L can keep producing 100 monthly. C can revise positions to 4, 4, 7, 4, 4, 7, still returning to five afterward. A manager proposes a third version of C: keep all seven trained positions through months 4 and 5 instead of reducing to four and rebuilding. Those people remain fully paid but produce only the 80 boxes due each month; there is no extra inventory or other productive assignment in this retention variant. It preserves team continuity and avoids one three-position reduction and later addition. No quantified quality, retention or future productivity benefit is supplied. The costs and rights protections remain unchanged.

Your decision

How would you revise the plan when the second peak moves later? Compare L, revised C and the paid-retention variant, and explain why equal total orders do not imply equal timing costs or a universal staffing answer.

Follow the balance across months

For L, month-end net stock is 20, 40, 0, 20, −20, 0. The negative entry means 20 agreed delayed boxes, not negative physical stock. Holding applies to 20 + 40 + 20 = 80 box-months: HK$8,000. The agreed delay costs HK$20,000. Five positions for six months cost HK$600,000, giving HK$628,000 of compared costs. The 40-box storage limit is respected.

Count both edges of the staffing plan

Initial C uses 4, 4, 7, 4, 7, 4 positions: 30 position-months, costing HK$600,000. Starting from five and restoring five afterward requires seven additions and seven endings in total. Adjustment cost is 7 × HK$3,000 + 7 × HK$2,000 = HK$35,000. C totals HK$635,000 with no stock or delay. L costs HK$7,000 less but uses the agreed delivery variation; a service preference can justify C.

A later peak changes the path

Revised L ends months with 20, 40, 0, 20, 40, 0 boxes. Holding is 120 box-months, costing HK$12,000, with no backlog: total HK$612,000. Revised C still uses 30 position-months but now needs six additions and six endings, including the initial reduction and final return to five. Its adjustment cost is HK$30,000: total HK$630,000.

Paying for continuity is not free

Retaining seven positions during both quiet months adds six paid position-months, or HK$120,000. It avoids three endings and three later additions, saving HK$15,000. Relative to revised C, it adds HK$105,000, giving HK$735,000. Continuity might have value beyond the supplied figures, but it cannot be labelled cost-saving here. Conversely, these fictional costs do not show that frequent workforce changes are generally good practice.

Instructor note

Use a running stock balance rather than averaging 600 orders into 100 and stopping. Mark any negative balance as backlog and check customer consent. Require all starting and terminal workforce transitions. Accept a stated service preference for initial C; on supplied revised costs L is lowest of the three, not proven globally optimal. Do not invent unpaid idle time, instant training, lost wages, unapproved delays or a quantified skill benefit. The fixed product never changes: this is aggregate timing, not postponed product differentiation.

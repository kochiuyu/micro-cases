PPL-45 · Hong Kong · 15 minutes · Meeting facilitation · Language access · Employee participation

Everyone understood. Who helped shape the decision?

Understanding words is not the same as being heard

Hong Kong’s 2021 Population Census published separate measures of usual spoken language and ability to speak other languages. Original studies of multilingual teams also distinguish following a discussion from contributing to it. The hotel exercise below concerns meeting design, not staff language ratings.

Your classroom decision

You manage a fictional Hong Kong hotel’s weekly briefing. Reception, housekeeping and event staff must agree how to place a temporary welcome desk in a rehearsal layout. No actual guest, safety procedure or required accessibility check is involved. Useful facts about arrival flow and setup time are distributed across the three roles in each scenario.

Both options provide the same accuracy-checked Cantonese and English briefing materials and paid interpretation if needed. A uses a read-through and open discussion. B replaces part of the read-through with private preparation in either language, then invites each role to contribute orally or in writing before a decision is recorded. The chair can paraphrase with the contributor’s confirmation. No one is forced to speak.

In a hypothetical pilot, 24 comparable briefings per arm use randomly assigned formats, three trained role players per briefing and the same checked answer key. A briefing meets the understanding check when all three roles accurately restate the agreed placement. The scheduled maximum is 16 minutes in either format. No individual ratings or real employee language profiles are retained.

Round 2 · More information

A separate tally shows that seven A briefings and sixteen B briefings documented at least one relevant fact from each of the three roles before the decision. To count, the fact had to be discussed, not just submitted. There is no overlap table linking this count with the understanding check for a particular briefing.

Observers also note that five of B’s sixteen all-role briefings relied on written input from at least one role that would otherwise have no qualifying spoken contribution. Written comments were translated where needed and discussed before closure. This pilot does not show whether those role players would have spoken if offered fewer routes.

Your decision

What would you test in the next cycle? Compare all-role input rates and average briefing time. Explain why neither a higher input count nor an understanding pass alone proves a better decision.

Debrief · Count facts from roles, not people’s accents

Both arms meet the understanding check in 18/24 = 75% of briefings. All-role input is 7/24 = 29.2% in A and 16/24 = 66.7% in B, a 37.5-percentage-point difference. B takes three more minutes on average. An average under 16 minutes does not establish that each briefing meets its time limit.

Debrief · Keep the written route open

Five of B’s sixteen all-role briefings (31.25%) would not qualify under a spoken-only coding of those same runs. That does not identify what people would do in a redesigned spoken-only meeting. Keep a voluntary written route, a short pause to read contributions and an explicit opportunity to correct paraphrases.

Debrief · Did the decision improve?

Prefer B for the next rehearsal because it makes more role knowledge available. Track which placement choices actually satisfy the independently checked layout constraints, how often the timer is exceeded, and whether the same agenda items can fit. Do not use participation as a fluency, enthusiasm or performance score.

Instructor note

Ask one group to draw the flow of information in A and another in B. Reveal the role-input tally after they compare understanding. The written channel is an equal route to the decision, not a substitute for asking whose relevant knowledge was missing.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All venues, team dialogue, scores and attendance are hypothetical. Census language data are context only, not a demographic model or a proxy for any participant’s abilities. No employee personal data, eligibility, pay or appraisal decisions.

  1. Hong Kong C&SD · 2021 Population Census short articles: Use of Language. Opened official dissemination page and read the language findings. Context for multilingual communication, not evidence about a hotel.
  2. Hong Kong EOC · Racial Diversity & Inclusion Charter for Employers FAQ. Opened the original PDF Q1–Q4. Describes a voluntary employer inclusion initiative and feedback, not an employment criterion or endorsement of a particular meeting format.
  3. CSCW · Facilitating Global Team Meetings Between Language-Based Subgroups (2022). Opened author full text. Original group experiment tests sharing translated subgroup context before a common-language meeting; this case does not use machine translation or copy its effects.
  4. OBHDP · A chorus of different tongues (2024). Opened full original article copy and read Study 2 measures and results concerning informal status and voice. It does not establish any link between accent and ability or a causal effect for this meeting format.
  5. Journal of World Business · Impact of language barriers on knowledge processing (2021). Opened accessible original article copy and read its practices discussion and limitations. Qualitative research links communication to team knowledge processing, not a measured hotel intervention.

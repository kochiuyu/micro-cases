MKT-12 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Customer journey · Funnel diagnosis · Conversion measures

Many visits. Where does the journey break?

In the news

On 28 January 2026, Hong Kong’s Census and Statistics Department described growth in online shopping and mobile payments, using earlier household survey results. It reported that over 60% of people aged 15 and over used mobile payments for online or in-store shopping in 2024.

That adoption figure does not show how well a particular shop converts visits into orders. Paper Path and all its journey counts below are fictional.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. Paper Path sells adult paper-craft kits online in Hong Kong. It has stock and fulfilment capacity, but wants more completed, wanted purchases rather than visits alone. It can spend HK$12,000 on one principal improvement this month.

A: commission paid placements alongside relevant craft tutorials, using the same accurate product offer to attract interested visitors. B: redesign the explanation and layout of the existing mobile payment handoff, including prototype checks and implementation. Each complete package costs HK$12,000. Neither supplier promises an uplift; there is no budget for both this month.

The payment system works and security, fraud checks, accessibility obligations, prices, delivery fees, returns, stock and payment choices remain unchanged. Total cost is already clear before payment. B does not remove verification or create urgency. Essential corrections remain routine obligations under either choice; this is optional clarity work, not permission to retain an unsafe or deceptive checkout.

The exhibit follows 1,000 distinct product viewers entering during one four-week period, with seven days of follow-up per person. Each is counted once at each ordered step. For this simplified exercise nobody switches devices, skips a step or makes multiple orders. Instrumentation is checked, and purchases reconcile to confirmed paid orders. These are custom journey stages, not a default analytics screenshot.

A stage count is people, not clicks or repeat attempts. Payment-step viewing does not mean payment information was submitted. A person who stops may return after this window or choose not to buy; not every departure is a defect.

Your decision

Would you fund A’s relevant placements or B’s mobile payment-step redesign first? Use a stage-specific count or rate, and state what that number cannot tell you about the return on your chosen option.

Four of six first-time mobile users pause at the neutral “Continue” control because they are unsure whether the next screen belongs to the shop or the selected payment provider. The explanation is present but not noticed by those four. No one is charged unexpectedly. These six are a convenience sample, not the 160-person cohort.

B would place a concise next-step cue beside the control and clearly distinguish entering the payment provider from final order confirmation. Existing payment options, consent, verification and security remain. There is no tested post-redesign conversion rate. The breakdown and observation locate a plausible problem; they do not explain every abandoned order.

Your decision

Keep or change your allocation? Identify the segment and step you would address, give its conversion rate, and choose one outcome measure plus one safeguard for judging the intervention without claiming the observed gap is its guaranteed uplift.

Use the denominator for the question

Overall purchase conversion is 80/1,000 = 8%. Payment-to-purchase conversion is 80/160 = 50%. Product-to-cart conversion is 300/1,000 = 30%. The biggest raw loss, 700 product viewers, does not automatically identify the most fixable stage.

These rates answer different questions. More relevant traffic could help A, while improved completion among people already considering payment could help B. Neither costed offer supplies enough causal or margin evidence to calculate a financial winner.

Segment before diagnosing

Mobile payment completion is 20/80 = 25%; desktop is 60/80 = 75%. Mobile also contains more first-time buyers, so device totals combine different customer mixes.

Within first-time buyers the rates are 12/60 = 20% on mobile and 12/20 = 60% on desktop. Returning-buyer rates are 8/20 = 40% and 48/60 = 80%. The gap remains within each supplied group, but this is not random device assignment: preferences, intentions and other differences can still matter.

Make a targeted decision, not an uplift promise

B is now a well-supported priority: it addresses an observed mobile handoff uncertainty near purchase. The six-person observation suggests a mechanism, not its prevalence or the number of recoverable sales. It does not establish that all 60 mobile noncompleters were confused.

A remains a genuine way to reach relevant potential customers. Choosing it should acknowledge the existing payment-stage concern rather than assuming additional visitors will convert at today’s overall rate. There is no free combined programme or proven return for either choice.

Measure progress without changing the meaning

For B, track confirmed purchases divided by mobile payment-step viewers, separating first-time and returning buyers under the same follow-up rule. Also track total completed orders, refunds, payment errors and unexpected-charge complaints. A button click alone is not success.

For A, measure qualified landing visits and the resulting completed purchases, with acquisition cost and contribution before claiming profitability. Compare like cohorts or plan a suitable controlled evaluation; before-and-after change alone may reflect customer mix, seasonality or other causes.

Instructor note

Allow 2 minutes for the opening, 3 for the first vote, 3 for the breakdown and 3–4 for the revised choice. Ask A supporters why more visits address the identified uncertainty; ask B supporters how they would avoid turning the desktop gap into a sales forecast.

Reveal the segment table only after the first vote. Students need no external reading. The intended distinction is diagnostic evidence versus intervention effects, not “always fix checkout before marketing.” A defensible answer names a stage, a denominator, a mechanism to examine and an outcome that preserves customer choice.

ACC-27 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Leading and lagging indicators · Management scorecards · Causal performance hypotheses

The result improved. Which warning was missed?

A result is not the mechanism that produced it

Financial results show what an organisation achieved, but do not necessarily explain the process that produced it. Professional performance-measurement guidance recommends linking indicators to objectives, responsibilities and actions. Evaluation guidance also distinguishes a plausible causal explanation from proof that one measure causes another.

The repair network, scorecard and observations below are fictional. This is a management-control exercise, not a safety or clinical performance assessment.

Your classroom decision

You are the controller of a Hong Kong network repairing office equipment. Management wants fewer avoidable repeat visits while retaining profitable service. Its monthly management scorecard has a fixed preparation and review budget for exactly three measures. Two slots are committed: monthly operating profit and the proportion of completed jobs requiring an avoidable repeat visit. Choose either escalation count E or kit-readiness exception rate K for the third slot. Each costs the same to validate and interpret; there is no fourth-slot budget.

All four feeds are accurate, complete and available immediately when their underlying event occurs. Monthly summaries support oversight; the selected process measure also has a funded named owner who can act on individual cases as they appear. Ordinary customer care, mandatory safety checks and complaint handling continue regardless of selection. This choice does not suppress records or defer a required response.

E counts customer cases tagged service escalation; a manager calls it a leading warning because it often rises before related recovery costs appear in the profit report. K records booked jobs with a kit exception at the 48-hour pre-visit check. Management has not yet examined the event sequence behind either label. Round 2 · More information

A route audit establishes what the labels mean. E is opened only after a technician has already failed to finish a first visit and the customer has contacted the recovery desk. The record appears immediately at that contact, but follows the failed visit. Recovery costs may be posted later. E can guide recovery and explain later cost, but it cannot warn before that same first-visit failure.

K is recorded 48 hours before the scheduled first visit when a verified required part is missing from the allocated kit. The visit has not occurred. A parts coordinator can reserve an available correct part from central stock and complete the kit within 24 hours. Funded staffing, stock and transport capacity are sufficient for the 90 currently flagged cases, without taking stock promised to anyone else. Safety checks remain mandatory. Kit completeness does not prevent all other causes of repeat visits.

Select the third measure for preventing missing-part repeat visits. The two committed measures stay. Specify a trigger, owner and action, then explain how management should check the hypothesis that resolving kit exceptions improves first-visit completion. The descriptive monthly association alone does not establish the effect or an exact number of avoided failures.

Your decision

Which third measure would you now select, what action should it trigger, and what evidence would distinguish a useful causal warning from a correlated symptom?

Read improvement and deterioration together

Operating profit rose HK$30,000, or 25%. Avoidable repeat visits rose from 8% to 10%, a two-percentage-point increase. Improved profit does not cancel the service deterioration. E rose by 20 cases; K rose from 12% to 18% of booked jobs. These figures concern different events and cohorts, so do not divide one count by another to manufacture a causal rate.

The first choice needs a causal question

E deserves investigation because it may identify costly customer trouble. K deserves investigation because it might locate an upstream resource problem. Either provisional choice can be defended if it includes a check of the event sequence and the available intervention. A non-financial measure is not automatically leading, and an earlier accounting date is not the same as preceding a service failure.

Leading relative to which outcome?

E follows the failed first visit even though it precedes some cost recognition. It is therefore lagging for that failure and potentially informative about subsequent recovery cost. Immediate reporting does not change its place in the causal chain. Retaining E in the ordinary recovery system is sensible; it is not the best third prevention measure for the stated missing-part objective.

Choose K with an action, not a slogan

Choose K. Each verified missing-part exception at the 48-hour check triggers the parts coordinator to reserve the correct uncommitted central-stock item, complete the kit within 24 hours and record resolution. The monthly scorecard reports flagged jobs as a proportion of all checked bookings and the owner explains unresolved exceptions. This is one measure with its case-level action trail, not a fourth scorecard KPI.

Test the link without claiming certainty

Track job identifiers from the pre-visit check through kit completion, first-visit outcome and reason for any repeat. Compare similar jobs and account for complexity, technician mix and other changes; where feasible use an ethical staged implementation or suitable comparison that does not withhold required care. Check whether the intervention actually changed kit completeness and whether missing-part failures fell. Timing and a plausible mechanism support the hypothesis, not a guaranteed causal estimate.

Instructor note

Allow three minutes for the provisional selection and four for the route audit. Require students to name the outcome before classifying leading or lagging. Ask why E can precede a financial cost yet follow the service failure. End with an owner-action-evidence chain, not a claim that 90 flags equal 90 prevented failures.

All feeds are timely, unlike a data-latency case. The mechanism is pre-visit resource readiness versus post-failure customer escalation, not reporting incentives or forecast-target separation. No staff bonus or punitive ranking is involved. Public health and occupational-safety readings contribute general measurement concepts only; no domain-specific requirement is transferred to the fictional repair business.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All organisations, measures, observations, budgets and interventions are fictional. Descriptive trends do not establish causality. All feeds are timely; the distinction is their place in the service process. General evaluation and safety-measurement guidance is not evidence that the fictional intervention will prevent a stated number of failures.

  1. ACCA, Performance indicators. Original professional teaching connects financial and non-financial measures with objectives, responsibility, reporting and action; also warns against too many measures.
  2. OSHA, Leading Indicators. Explains preventive versus historical measures in occupational safety. Used only for the general distinction, not as repair-service evidence or law.
  3. CDC, Developing a Logic Model (4 November 2024). Distinguishes inputs, activities, outputs and outcomes and makes assumptions explicit. Clinical examples are not transferred to this case.
  4. HM Treasury, Magenta Book: Central Government guidance on evaluation. Sections on impact evaluation explain causal chains, comparison evidence and alternative explanations. Not empirical validation of the fictional intervention.
  5. EU-OSHA OSHwiki, Key performance indicators. Discusses management relevance, predictive value and limits of generic leading indicators. Safety-specific measures are background, not the fictional scorecard.

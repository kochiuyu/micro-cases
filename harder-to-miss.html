OPS-08 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Operations · Quality at source · Mistake-proofing

Check it again. Or make the mistake harder?

In the news · Improving inspection, improving the process

In its 23 October 2025 release of the AI Readiness in Workplace Survey 2025, HKPC discussed visual inspection as a smart-manufacturing application.

That institutional account is not proof of a particular quality gain. Our separate fictional workshop asks a simpler question: could a low-tech change improve the packing step itself?

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A small Hong Kong stationery workshop packs desk kits for adult office gifts. Every kit promises one notebook, one pencil, one eraser and one ruler.

Last week, the existing pre-dispatch inspection found 25 of 500 kits needing correction. The detailed defect breakdown is not yet at the meeting. Every detected defect was corrected before dispatch; no count of errors missed by inspection is available.

The supervisor has one separately funded allowance of six paid staff-hours next week for an improvement. Either proposal below fits it. Normal production staffing and the target stay unchanged. Existing release inspection, safety and legally required checks remain under either choice.

Use of the six-hour allowanceWhat it changes
A · Add another final checkA second independent content-and-condition check before dispatch, covering a broad range of errors
B · Introduce a four-position trayMake, test and teach reusable trays, with one designated position for each kit component

With B, fill all four positions before transferring the set into a box, then check that no part remains in the tray before closing. An unfilled position before transfer or a leftover part afterwards signals a problem to resolve. The tray is a visual aid, not an automatic lock: it can be misloaded or bypassed. Its refill and checking effort must be considered.

Your decision

Would you spend next week’s improvement allowance on another final check or the packing tray? Make a provisional choice and name a defect pattern that would change it.

Round 2 · One fact changes

The operator brings the records: 20 of the 25 kits lacked one item; five had a visibly misprinted notebook cover. The categories do not overlap. Observation also found that loose component piles and interruptions made it easy to lose count.

A preparatory trial, already completed before this decision, used prototype trays for another 500 comparable kits. It did not use next week’s six-hour allowance. Ordinary trained operators used the same four components and release criteria, with a comparable work mix. The trial included tray refilling and the before-and-after transfer checks; operators reported that the routine was workable at normal pace.

Your decision

With the trial and defect breakdown, which improvement would you choose? Which problem does the tray address, and which check must remain?

Target the error, not the worker

Most recorded problems were missing parts: 20 of 25 kits. A second check could detect omissions and other defects, but it does not change how a part gets left out during packing.

The tray makes the intended four-part set visible at the step where an omission can occur. Correct loading, transfer and the final empty-tray check still depend on the routine. A visible warning is not a physical guarantee, and loose piles or interruptions are process clues, not evidence that a particular worker was careless.

Choose the tray with the existing checks retained

B now has stronger support for this workshop’s main recorded problem. The trial suggests a workable routine and fewer omissions, so use the six hours to prepare the reusable trays, test their setup and teach the method. Assign someone to maintain the layout and replenishment routine.

The misprinted-cover count did not change. Keep the original release inspection for print defects, remaining omissions and other problems. A second final check remains defensible if broad immediate detection is the priority or the tray routine cannot be reliably maintained; it is not made useless by the trial.

Measure first-pass work, not just corrected dispatches

The earlier release check found 475 of 500 kits needing no correction: 95%. In the trial it found 491 of 500: 98.2%. That is an observed increase of 3.2 percentage points in passing this inspection first time, with 16 fewer kits requiring correction.

These figures are not customer satisfaction or guaranteed outgoing quality. Both batches had their detected problems corrected; undetected problems are unknown. Inspection, rework and process improvement all use resources, but no measured time or cash saving has been supplied.

Keep watching the routine in ordinary work

Record defect types before correction, maintain consistent release criteria and watch for misloading, bypassing or awkward refilling. If a new component or layout changes the task, check that the aid still helps rather than creating confusion.

The batches were not randomly assigned. Learning, work mix or other changes may contribute to the observed difference. Do not promise 16 fewer defective kits every week. In this low-risk kit case, frequency helps set priorities; a rare serious safety defect would need attention regardless of frequency.

Instructor note

Take a provisional A-or-B vote before revealing the breakdown and historical trial. A is a credible broad detection choice, while B initially needs evidence that omissions matter. The reveal supplies positive process evidence, not permission to eliminate checks.

The allowance is six paid staff-hours in total, not six clock-hours per worker and not an unfunded cut to ordinary production staffing. The preparatory trial is already finished; next week’s allowance funds either the added check or tray introduction, not both. Continuing tray use has handling effort; the trial considered it but supplies no quantified productivity gain.

Count kits, not defect instances: categories are exclusive. Optional calculations are 20/25 = 80% of recorded defective kits with omissions, and first-pass inspection results of 95% and 98.2%. The 80% is not a universal Pareto law. This case concerns internal quality at source, distinct from capacity, queues, batch sizing or supplier remediation.

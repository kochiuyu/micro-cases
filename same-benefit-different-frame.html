MKT-15 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Message framing · Audience interpretation · Ethical persuasion

The same benefit. A different frame?

In the news

On 14 May 2026, Hong Kong’s Consumer Council reported that air-conditioner extended-warranty arrangements varied, including parts coverage, age limits and geographic exclusions. It advised consumers to examine the terms. A service headline cannot replace an explanation of what customers actually receive.

The service, prices and interview evidence below are original fiction. This plan is about arranging a routine inspection, not a warranty or guarantee of uninterrupted cooling.

Your classroom decision

A fictional Hong Kong maintenance company is preparing an invitation for households who want to arrange a routine inspection before summer. The decision-maker is also the service user; this is not a buyer-versus-user conflict. The company must choose one lead headline for this invitation. Both versions have the same cost, placement, photograph, readable type size and equally prominent service terms. Neither uses an urgency timer, shortage claim or warning image.

Optional HK$600 annual booking plan: one scheduled inspection of one eligible air conditioner, plus two reminder messages. Choose an available appointment at least 14 days ahead. No emergency response, repair labour, parts or breakdown-prevention guarantee. Non-members may book the same inspection separately for HK$600, subject to the same availability; reminders are the only additional service. No automatic renewal.

VersionLead wording
A · avoided inconvenienceAvoid leaving routine maintenance booking to the last minute.
B · preserved convenienceKeep routine maintenance booking comfortably planned ahead.
Your decision

Which headline would you choose for households arranging their routine inspection ahead of summer, and why? Explain what stays identical in the offer and what interpretation your wording might encourage.

Round 2 · More information

The researcher conducts eight fictional one-to-one exploratory interviews, four with people who had previously forgotten to book a routine visit and four with people who already keep household maintenance calendars. These are purposively recruited experiences, not representative market segments. All eight see both complete mock-ups; order is alternated. They are not asked to purchase and no behaviour is observed.

Illustrative interview noteOriginal fictional comment
Previously forgot · participant F1A reminds me of the booking task; B feels less concrete.
Previously forgot · participant F2A is useful, but I would still compare the separate inspection.
Keeps a calendar · participant C1A sounds as if I have been careless; B respects how I plan.
Keeps a calendar · participant C2B feels calm, but the reminders add little for me.

These are four illustrative comments, not votes or a complete coding tally. The remaining four interviewees express no clear headline preference. All eight accurately explain the price, optionality, one-inspection scope and exclusions after reading the full mock-ups. Nobody reports an actual emergency; neither headline is said to promise repair coverage. The decision remains which wording to use for the stated invitation, not whether to suppress unfavourable comments.

Your decision

Would you retain your headline, switch to the other, or revise the wording for the stated audience? Use the interview evidence without claiming a sales winner, and specify one comprehension measure and one voluntary behaviour measure for the next check.

Separate the offer from its emphasis

The price, inspection, reminders, availability and exclusions are unchanged. A foregrounds an inconvenience to avoid; B foregrounds a planning benefit to preserve. Neither describes an extra repair entitlement. In technical framing research, avoiding an undesirable outcome can itself be a gain-framed appeal: do not label A a proven loss-aversion technique merely because it uses a negative word.

Interpret the comments, not an imaginary vote

F1 and F2 suggest A can make the booking task salient. C1 and C2 suggest B may feel respectful to people who already plan, but a pleasant tone does not create a need for reminders. These four selected comments are diagnostic examples, not counts of supporters. The other four interviews offer no clear preference, so no majority preference or conversion rate is available. All eight can correctly restate the price, optionality, one-inspection scope and exclusions after reading the complete mock-ups.

Make a provisional audience-specific choice

For an invitation aimed broadly at households planning ahead, B is a defensible calm default; A remains defensible if making the booking task salient is the priority and subsequent checks show no false urgency. A revision such as “Plan your routine inspection ahead of summer” can remove implied criticism, but it is a new version, not an already tested winner. Keep every term equally prominent. The company cannot infer that calendar-keepers will not buy, or target people with invented claims that their appliance is about to fail.

Validate understanding before celebrating response

Ask new participants to explain, without prompting, what HK$600 buys and whether emergency repairs or breakdown prevention are included. Record the specific misunderstandings, not just whether the message feels reassuring. A voluntary next step could be an appointment-availability enquiry after reading the terms; distinguish that action from payment. If running a larger comparison, randomly assign comparable eligible audiences to one version, hold the offer and placement constant, define outcomes and denominators in advance, and monitor misunderstanding and complaints alongside completed purchases. No lift or sample-size claim is supplied here.

A better message cannot fix an unwanted offer

Someone may understand both versions perfectly and decline because they already keep reminders or prefer booking separately. That is an informed choice, not a communications failure to overcome with pressure. Framing changes emphasis; it must not conceal that non-members can buy the same inspection at the same price. No version is certified legally compliant by this case, and no general claim that negative or positive wording always sells better follows from the research.

Instructor note

First ask students to name the identical service and choose a headline. Reveal the interview notes and require separate statements of observation, interpretation and next action. Challenge anyone turning four quotations into a sales percentage or calling A automatically loss-framed. Accept either supported provisional choice or a clearly labelled untested revision. End by asking whether a well-informed refusal should count as failure. The lesson is ethical message emphasis and meaning, not price anchoring, proof of expertise or identifying a different buyer.

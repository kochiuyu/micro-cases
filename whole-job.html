PPL-08 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · People · Job design · Task ownership

More variety. More ownership?

In the news · When the content of a job changes

In a written reply on 26 February 2025, the Hong Kong Government described how reduced typing work had led to serving typists being reappointed, with training, to clerical roles including customer service and support duties.

This is a historical account of changing job content, not an evaluation of employee satisfaction or productivity. The framing studio and choices below are fictional.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong custom-framing studio has three experienced order coordinators. One records customer choices, another prepares quotes from an approved price list, and another arranges collection when the craft team finishes. Craftspeople handle all manufacture and technical or safety decisions.

The coordinators say their work feels repetitive and they rarely see what happens to an order after their part. The manager wants a more satisfying job while maintaining reliable service. The team is choosing a four-week pilot, not a permanent restructure.

Pay, staffing, agreed hours, breaks and reasonable workload will stay unchanged. Both options include paid preparation and a shared order record. There is no extra sales target, unpaid learning or permission to bypass checks.

Four-week pilotWhat changes?
A · Rotate among stationsChange administrative stations each week: more variety and cover across tasks, but an order still passes between people
B · Follow standard orders throughGive coordinators capped portfolios from enquiry to collection, with quote limits, supervisor support and discretion over routine customer-contact sequencing

A preserves the familiar division of work. B could give a clearer view of a completed customer outcome, but needs suitable orders and a workable allocation of effort.

Round 2 · One fact changes

A joint review of 30 recent orders finds 24 standard administrative journeys using the approved menu and the coordinators’ existing skills. Six require technical advice or exceptional pricing and will keep the existing specialist route. Coordinators are not being asked to do craft work.

Two coordinators want to try following standard orders through and hearing the customer’s feedback at collection. The third prefers rotation for now. A review of effort across the stages, not just order counts, shows that the manager can cap the volunteers’ portfolios, redistribute existing tasks and provide a named backup within the agreed hours.

A voluntary ownership pilot is therefore feasible without shifting extra work or all exceptions to the third coordinator. That colleague’s rotation and cover arrangement must be agreed before launch; specialist support and the usual checks remain. No pilot outcome has yet been observed.

The 24 and six are counts of these recent orders, not shares of labour time or a forecast of future demand.

Variety and ownership answer different needs

Rotation changes which task a person performs. It can broaden skills and make absence cover easier, but an employee may still see only one part of each order.

Following a standard order from enquiry to collection makes the whole administrative contribution easier to recognise. The coordinator can see how their record and quote connect with the finished customer experience. That is different from simply adding more tasks to the day.

Use the enabling evidence without assuming success

The review supplies three reasons to try bounded ownership: suitable standard work, willing experienced coordinators and a feasible workload arrangement. A four-week pilot with the two volunteers is now a defensible choice.

A combined arrangement can respect the third colleague’s preference for rotation. It should not turn that person into an unlimited cover worker or the destination for every awkward order. Staying with rotation is still reasonable if the team cannot agree a workable allocation or if staff priorities change.

The review establishes feasibility, not improved motivation, faster service or better retention. Those outcomes have not been measured.

Make discretion and feedback concrete

Ownership need not mean unrestricted authority. A coordinator can choose the sequence of routine customer contacts within service deadlines, use the approved price list and keep the shared record current. Exceptional prices and technical questions still go to the supervisor or craft specialists.

At collection, let the coordinator hear what the customer says about the completed order and review any correction with the team. Keep a named backup so continuity does not depend on one person always being available. This is supported responsibility, not working alone.

Review the job as well as the output

During the pilot, ask coordinators whether they can see the outcome of their work and whether the discretion is useful. Also check actual time demands, missed breaks, quote corrections, missed collection arrangements and backup requests.

If portfolios overload someone, require unpaid extra work or weaken checks, reduce or redesign the allocation rather than asking for more commitment. Review the third coordinator’s workload too. More responsibility is not automatically a better job for everyone.

Instructor note

Take an A-or-B vote before the review. Ask whether each proposal meets the stated concern: repeated tasks and little visibility of outcomes. Do not make rotation a straw option; its variety and coverage benefits are real design aims.

After the reveal, ask for a concrete scope, one decision the coordinator may make, one decision they may not make and one feedback route. The three stages are administrative; staff are already experienced, so this is not a newcomer competence or craft-training case.

The 24 standard orders are 80% of this small set by count, not 80% of work hours, future demand or checks to remove. The supplied effort review is what makes capped portfolios feasible. No performance or satisfaction effect is claimed. A four-week checkpoint should hear all three coordinators, not only volunteers.

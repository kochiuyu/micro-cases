ENT-13 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Entrepreneurial networks · Resource access · Strong and weak ties

Ask the people we know. Meet the people they do not know?

In the news

On 7 September 2026, HKSTP opened a Co-Development and Investment Conference connecting technology ventures, investors and industry partners. Its announced programme included exchanges, facility visits and matching activities.

Such programmes offer routes to people and resources, not proof that an introduction becomes a useful partnership. The founder, meetings and offers below are fictional and are not HKSTP programme terms.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong founder is developing a fold-flat display stand for small exhibitions. Before commissioning a production sample, she needs mechanical-design advice, a possible part-time technical coordinator and a small-batch fabricator. No product launch or customer delivery has been promised.

This month she has eight hours and HK$3,000 for one structured relationship-building activity, including preparation, travel, meetings and follow-up. A uses that allowance for a working session with four trusted printing and exhibition-design contacts. B uses it for a cross-industry clinic arranged by an organiser she has met once. Both fit the same allowance; the booked formats cannot be split this month.

A’s contacts know her working style and can speak candidly. B may lead to unfamiliar skills but requires explaining the project and checking fit. Existing relationships continue whichever activity she chooses. These are exploratory meetings, not paid engineering work, a job offer or a manufacturing contract.

What the venture needs nextWhat an introduction alone does not establish
Mechanical-design adviceRelevant competence and scope
Possible technical coordinatorInterest, availability and agreed pay
Small-batch fabricationCapability, quantity, timing and price
Your decision

Which activity would you choose this month, A or B? Explain what you expect the relationship to provide, rather than simply choosing the event with more contacts or the people you like best.

Round 2 · More information

The founder sketches who knows whom. Her four trusted contacts work together frequently and use the same print-finishing suppliers. They can give a well-informed review of exhibition setup and the design brief, but none claims mechanical-design expertise or a current lead for the technical role. They have not ruled out finding further contacts later.

The clinic organiser has links to a separate product-engineering circle. A mechanical designer and a small-batch metal fabricator have agreed to short exploratory conversations within B’s booked session. The organiser can also ask a technician about an introduction, but has not yet obtained that person’s agreement. None is connected to the founder’s four contacts in the sketch.

The designer’s relevant past work, the fabricator’s capacity and prices, and any technician’s availability still need verification. No one has agreed to supply the required work. A’s trusted review could sharpen the brief; B now offers a concrete bridge to two resource leads. The allowance and one-activity choice remain. Technical work, hiring and fabrication would require separate due diligence, budgets and agreements.

Your decision

Would you keep or change your choice? Identify the resource gap and the person who could bridge it, then specify what you would verify before treating a conversation as usable support or a commitment.

Map resources, not the size of the address book

The four familiar contacts are valuable, but their information overlaps in this particular sketch. Several names can point back to the same experience and suppliers. Count what they can help the venture learn or do, not just how many people attend.

The organiser connects the founder to a professional circle she does not already reach. This is a bridge: someone links otherwise separated groups. B’s value is the possibility of complementary knowledge and capability, not automatically more customers or a better event.

Trust and new information solve different problems

Strong ties are established relationships that may support candid advice and coordination. Weaker ties are less developed relationships that can provide a route outside the familiar circle. Neither label guarantees competence, generosity or useful information.

Choosing B is defensible because two exploratory conversations address the missing technical resources. Choosing A can be defended if refining the specification is the immediate bottleneck and the founder accepts delaying the new-resource search. Do not claim that every weak tie bridges a gap or that trusted contacts never bring new ideas.

Convert a lead into a checked next step

For the designer, ask about relevant work, scope and appropriate review of the stand. For the fabricator, check sample capability, minimum quantities, capacity, lead time and a quote. For a potential coordinator, first seek permission for contact, then discuss role, availability and paid terms. No introduction substitutes for these checks.

Prepare a short non-confidential brief, obtain permission before sharing contact details and agree how to follow up. Do not send private customer information or proprietary drawings merely because an organiser made an introduction. Report progress as leads, verified capabilities and separately agreed next steps, not as partners secured.

Instructor note

Ask students to draw two clusters and one bridge after the reveal. The bridge is the organiser, not a guarantee supplied by the institution. The founder can choose one activity now without abandoning the other relationships forever.

Assess whether the recommendation identifies a resource bottleneck, preserves the value of trust and sets a verification step. Do not award marks simply for choosing novelty. ENT-04 compares the permitted uses of confirmed support awards; this case concerns access to people whose resource commitments are not yet secured. MKT-09 measures additional campaign audience; this case has no buyer-reach calculation. Research concepts do not establish that this clinic will improve sales or venture survival.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All venture, contacts, network connections, budgets, meeting formats, permissions and offers are original fiction. A and B each consume the same eight-hour planning allowance and HK$3,000 all-in activity budget. Follow-up time is included; no free simultaneous second activity is assumed. This funds relationship exploration, not technical labour, hiring or fabrication. Those later costs and feasibility remain unknown. No customer deadline or product release is at risk from taking time to verify. Contact clusters are known only to the extent stipulated in this sketch, not a claim to know every possible relationship. The two B conversations are agreed; the technician referral is still permission-pending. Neither introduced professional has been declared capable or available for the eventual job. A offers relevant exhibition and brief knowledge, and familiar relationships continue under B. A weak tie is not identical to a bridge and a strong tie need not be redundant. No sensitive personal profiling, scraping, unrequested outreach, real contact sharing or contractual commitment is requested. HKSTP programme descriptions are institutional context, not case offers or independent outcome studies. Burt’s conceptual brokerage discussion was read; no general causal or quantified performance claim is imported.

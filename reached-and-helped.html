SUS-07 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Social enterprise · Service allocation · Outcome evidence

More people reached. More people helped?

In the news · Different routes to digital inclusion

On 26 June 2025, Hong Kong’s Digital Policy Office announced another round of Smart Silver outreach activities, including practical digital training and mobile service stations.

The announcement illustrates different service formats. Participation estimates were targets, not evidence of lasting skill gains. Our fictional provider is not presented as a programme partner.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong social enterprise has sponsor funding for four two-hour digital-skills sessions this month. Each accommodates 20 people with the available staff, devices and support. Either plan costs the same; the sponsor does not reward inflated attendance records.

The mission has two priorities: reaching people who lack support, and helping them use a practical digital service with less help. One introductory session offers first contact; a return session offers guided practice and later troubleshooting.

Participation is voluntary and based on expressed support needs, not an assumption that age means inability. The exercise is finding a public transport timetable on a demonstration device, without personal accounts or sensitive information. Which allocation should the team make now?

Choose a service priority, not just the larger number

A reaches 80 different people, while B reaches 40 with repeated contact. Both use 80 attendance places. There is no concealed counting mistake: the real choice is how much breadth to exchange for depth within four sessions.

B can be a reasonable provisional choice if sustained practical ability is the immediate priority. A remains defensible if first contact with unsupported residents is more urgent, with a credible follow-on route rather than an assumed guarantee that a referral will provide help.

Use the pilot without claiming more than it shows

The one-session group’s observed count increased by four and the two-session group’s by nine. The latter started lower, and participants selected their format. The comparison does not isolate the effect of the second session.

Do not call two sessions 2.25 times more effective, label the changes additional people helped by the provider, or project those rates onto this month’s participants. The pilot is useful for shaping a provisional decision and further questions, not a causal impact estimate.

A compromise has an opportunity cost too

A feasible blend is one group of 20 attending twice plus two other groups of 20 attending once. It uses four sessions and 80 places, reaching 60 unique people.

Compared with A, it gives up first contact with 20 people to provide a second session to 20. Compared with B, it gives up a second session for 20 people to reach 20 more newcomers. It does not deliver both extremes for free; explain whose additional support or initial contact is forgone.

Serve now and learn proportionately

Choose an allocation with a stated mission priority rather than waiting for a large experiment before offering useful support. Include participants’ own priorities and access needs in how the service is delivered.

Track unique reach separately from attendance, and assess practical performance, voluntary confidence and need for help at suitable follow-up. Similar measurement can inform improvement without proving the provider caused every change. Do not ask for passwords or sensitive personal histories to measure this demonstration task.

Instructor note

Require an actual allocation choice, not just a proposal to improve the report. Keep the 80-versus-40 unique-person counts visible before the vote. The reveal adds limited outcome evidence rather than changing the denominator.

A strong answer recognises that activities, observed outcomes and attributed impact are different, while still deciding where the next four sessions go. Both mission priorities are legitimate; the pilot does not dictate one universal winner.

All allocations and pilot observations are invented. No real participant’s eligibility, individual ability or access to services is being decided.

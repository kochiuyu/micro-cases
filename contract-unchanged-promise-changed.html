PPL-30 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Psychological contracts · Expectation clarification · Trust repair

Nothing in the contract changed. Did the promise?

The written terms are not the whole relationship

Psychological-contract research examines beliefs about reciprocal obligations in employment relationships. These beliefs may differ between people even when formal terms are unchanged. Research also questions whether perceived broken promises can be cleanly separated from disappointment with what is delivered. The fictional case concerns clarification and credible follow-through, not a finding about legal rights.

Your classroom decision

A Hong Kong design consultancy runs a voluntary development programme for existing employees. Participants receive twelve paid hours each quarter, separate from mandatory training. The next quarter will replace mixed observation and practice sessions with internal simulations because client schedules are less predictable. Pay, working time and all contractual entitlements remain unchanged by stipulation. The case does not ask students to interpret employment law or decide whether a real promise is legally enforceable.

HR must choose how to announce the format change. A sends a clear factual notice: the twelve hours remain, simulations will practise useful skills, and staff can ask practical questions. B first uses a funded thirty-minute group discussion to ask what staff thought the programme offered, compare that with managers’ intentions, and then explain the change and limits. The discussion fits existing paid team-meeting time and does not reduce the twelve development hours. B delays the final programme notice by two working days, still before enrolment opens. Neither route may promise client access that the firm cannot deliver.

The programme’s earlier invitation described an opportunity to prepare for more client-facing work. A can avoid unnecessary uncertainty and quickly communicate what is funded. B may uncover concerns that the notice alone misses, but hearing them does not oblige HR to grant every request. No participant has been promised a promotion, a client assignment or a particular number of observation sessions in a written guarantee.

Both routes preserve twelve paid development hours next quarter and all stipulated formal entitlements. A: announce the new format and invite practical questions. B: thirty-minute expectation discussion first, then notice two working days later, still before enrolment. Discussion is separately funded in existing meeting time. No unverified promise of client access or promotion is permitted.

Your decision

Would you choose A or B? Explain whether preserving the hours is enough to establish that the programme still delivers what employees reasonably understood it to offer.

Round 2 · More information

Private preparatory conversations reveal different interpretations. Participants understood the earlier invitation as a route to observing real client work: several describe their regular practice effort as their side of a reciprocal development arrangement. Managers understood it as general skill preparation, with occasional observation only when a suitable client agreed. Neither interpretation was recorded as a guaranteed entitlement. Two observation sessions happened last quarter, making the participants’ interpretation understandable without establishing that anyone deliberately deceived them.

The next quarter has no confirmed client-observation places. The development lead can deliver twelve paid hours of simulation with feedback using anonymised past briefs, but cannot claim it is the same experience as live client contact. A future observation opportunity would depend on client permission, suitable work and capacity. HR can commit now to documenting the programme’s purpose, actual provision, conditional opportunities and review date, and to reporting at the six-week review whether any observation places have become feasible. That review is not a promise that places will exist.

Staff disagree about the substitute: some value feedback practice; others mainly wanted exposure to client conversations. There is no evidence that everyone feels betrayed or that the change will cause resignations. The available decision is how to acknowledge the ambiguity and set a credible arrangement for the next quarter, not whether to invent an entitlement or force employees to accept HR’s interpretation as their own.

Your decision

How would you communicate and follow through now? Distinguish what you would acknowledge, what you can commit to, and what must remain conditional.

Answer the first decision: B before equivalence

I would choose B. The small, funded discussion can reveal what the hours meant to people before HR declares continuity. Unchanged hours establish continuity of time, not equivalence of developmental experience. A remains defensible if it explicitly invites substantive concerns and corrects the message promptly, but a notice restricted to logistics is unlikely to resolve the differing interpretations here. Discussion is not an unlimited promise-making exercise.

Answer the revealed decision: acknowledge without adjudicating rights

Say that the earlier wording and actual observation sessions understandably led some participants to expect client exposure, while managers intended broader preparation. Acknowledge the disappointment and the organisation’s part in leaving this unclear. Do not call employees mistaken, assert deliberate deception, or convert an unwritten expectation into a legal entitlement. The case stipulates formal terms; a real legal question would need separate advice.

Commit to delivery, not reassuring ambiguity

Confirm twelve paid hours of simulations and feedback, honestly distinguishing them from live client work. Explain the scheduling constraint and publish purpose, provision, conditions and a six-week review date. Commit to checking feasible observation opportunities and reporting the result at that review, even if none exist. Client permission, suitable work and capacity remain conditions, not concealed guarantees. Record differing priorities and invite corrections to the summary rather than demanding agreement that the substitute is equivalent.

Repair is tested through follow-through

Track whether the promised sessions and feedback occur, whether the review happens on time and whether staff understand what is fixed versus conditional. Ask whether the delivered development is useful, not merely whether the message sounded reassuring. An apology or explanation alone is not a proven cure for lost trust. Revisit feasible programme choices if the substitute fails its intended purpose, without making unsupported commitments.

Instructor note

Distinguish a wish from a perceived reciprocal obligation: ask what communication, past conduct and employee contribution support the latter interpretation. Neither a disappointed feeling nor an absent written guarantee settles that question. Sources include theory, meta-analysis and studies challenging common measurements; overlapping evidence is not five independent trials of this communication choice. No precise retention or trust benefit is forecast. This is post-change expectation reconciliation with existing employees, not recruitment messaging.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

Every firm, programme, hour, conversation, past session and review date is fictional. Both paths retain 12 paid development hours; the 30-minute discussion uses separately funded existing meeting time, not 11.5 remaining development hours. B delays notice by two working days but still precedes enrolment. Two past observation sessions do not establish a recurring guarantee. The six-week review guarantees a report, not observation places. Formal entitlements are unchanged by scenario stipulation, not inferred from absence of writing. Psychological-contract language is analytical, not a legal ruling. Different reactions and valuable delivered provision matter. Meta-analytic associations do not prove the proposed conversation causes trust repair or lower turnover, and the experimental social-account study did not establish apology superiority.

  1. Rousseau: Psychological and Implied Contracts in Organizations, 1989. Original conceptual paper distinguishes individual beliefs in reciprocal obligations from shared relational interpretations. Legal discussion is not imported into the fictional case.
  2. Rees, Brownell and Kickul: Beyond Exchange and Affect, 2026. Original open-access meta-analysis discusses heterogeneous responses and expectation clarification; correlational evidence cannot establish this intervention’s effect.
  3. To Deny, to Justify, or to Apologize, 2021. Original experiment examines social accounts after breach; results do not support promising that apology alone repairs stress or trust. Student setting limits transfer.
  4. Zhao and colleagues: Psychological Contract Breach and Work-Related Outcomes, 2007. Original meta-analysis links perceived breach with several outcomes while noting self-report, causality and evidence limitations. No turnover forecast is taken from it.
  5. Montes and Zweig: Do Promises Matter?, 2009. Original experimental and longitudinal studies question common breach measures and emphasise delivered inducements, providing a critical counterweight to promise-only explanations.

MKT-03 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Co-branding · Brand attribution

They remember the character. Do they remember us?

Hong Kong in the news · March 2025

On 23 March 2025, Chow Tai Fook launched a Chiikawa jewellery collection at Hong Kong’s iSQUARE store and Macau’s MGM store. Trade reporting the next day described the initial batch selling out quickly. In an April statement, the company placed collaborations alongside heritage, craftsmanship and reaching younger consumers.

A fast launch shows attention and initial demand, not automatically long-term brand understanding or repeat purchases. There is no claim here that the real collaboration damaged the company’s identity. The following stationery shop, illustrated character and research findings are fictional.

Your classroom decision

You manage a fictional heritage Hong Kong stationery shop known for carefully bound, refillable notebooks. An authorized collaboration adds a removable sleeve featuring an invented woodland-owl character to the same notebook. The launch batch of 100 notebooks sold out over the weekend, with enthusiastic queues and photographs.

Your stated objective is to introduce new notebook users to the shop’s craftsmanship and refill system, while earning sales. The merchandising team proposes increasing the collaboration from 20% to 70% of next quarter’s display space. Total space is fixed, so core notebooks and refills would fall from 80% to 30%. Both ranges remain available; prices and product quality would not change.

The partner permits either display approach and a redesign linking the character to the notebook’s use. Licensing obligations are already covered; no new minimum order is forced by this decision. Launch sell-through is known, but later use, repeat purchases, category margins and sales displaced from the core range are not yet measured.

Your decision

Would you allocate 70% of the display to the collaboration, keep it at 20%, or choose another balance? What would count as success for the shop’s stated objective beyond a launch sellout?

Round 2 · One fact changes

Twenty launch buyers voluntarily join a short follow-up interview. Without prompts, 18 can name the character, four can name the stationery shop, and three can explain the refill system. These answers can overlap: they are not three separate groups. Several say the sleeve was their main reason for buying.

This small volunteer group is diagnostic, not a representative estimate of all buyers. Nobody has yet been tracked long enough to establish repeat purchasing. The findings do not show brand damage, prove that collectors are poor customers, or test whether a redesigned display would perform better. The first 100 sales remain real sales in this fictional scenario.

Your decision

Would you expand the character-first display unchanged, redesign it, or stop the collaboration? Explain how your choice connects new attention to the shop’s own product, and what evidence could make you reconsider.

Debrief · Attention to whose brand?

The collaboration has sold products and attracted attention. Those are genuine outcomes. But the stated objective also requires customers to connect the appealing character with this shop and its refillable notebook. Remembering the partner is not the same as remembering the maker or understanding the benefit.

The follow-up raises a question about that connection, not a verdict on every customer. Collector demand may be commercially valuable in its own right. If management changes the primary goal to selling collectibles, it should say so and evaluate margins and displaced sales rather than quietly treating launch excitement as evidence of future core-notebook demand.

Build a bridge to the product

A defensible response is to retain a bounded collaboration while redesigning the presentation: make the shop’s identity visible, show the binding and refill mechanism, and connect the owl’s artwork to writing or keeping a journal. Keep core notebooks and refills easy to find. The partner permits this in the scenario; in practice the scope of authorized use must be checked.

This is a hypothesis, not a guaranteed fix. A character-first display might still sell more, while a balanced display might teach the product better. Compare those outcomes with the shop’s objective and the value of the space each occupies. Ending every collaboration because of one small follow-up would discard useful possibilities without testing them.

Measure the intended result

Test a clearly described revised display against the current one in comparable periods or locations. Track product sales, contribution after relevant costs, unprompted shop recall, understanding of the refill system and observed use separately. Later, measure actual refill or notebook purchases with appropriate consent rather than treating purchase intention as retention.

Include non-buyers and core-range customers when investigating the display trade-off. Account for event timing, availability and visitor mix. Define what evidence would support 70%, 20% or a different allocation before choosing the results you like. Do not force every customer to value craftsmanship; determine whether enough of the intended audience learns why this notebook is useful.

Back to the evidence

The real jewellery launch illustrates collaboration as an audience-building strategy; the company’s April statement is management’s account, not a causal study. Hong Kong industry interviews emphasize fit and coherent storytelling, but cannot establish what will happen in this fictional stationery shop.

Brand-extension research suggests that parent-brand strength and perceived fit matter, and that fit can come from capabilities, image or complementary use rather than identical product categories. Those studies do not say that every character collaboration succeeds or fails, and their results are not numerical forecasts of this shop’s retention.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–3: choose display space and define success in one sentence. Minutes 3–7: reveal the interviews; distinguish partner recall, shop recall and product understanding. Minutes 7–12: sketch a display message that connects character and notebook, then name one outcome that could disconfirm its value.

Challenge “sold out means the strategy worked” by asking which goal was measured. Challenge “collectors are the wrong customers” by asking whether the shop has evidence of that or merely a preference. Challenge “just put our logo bigger” with whether customers understand the product benefit. Do not add the interview counts together or project them to all launch buyers.

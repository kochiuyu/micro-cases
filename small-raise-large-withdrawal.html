SUS-23 · Hong Kong · 15 minutes · Benefit cliffs · Withdrawal rates · Distribution and budget

Just above the threshold. No help at all?

Design the transition, not only the threshold

Research and official benefit guidance distinguish abrupt eligibility losses from gradual withdrawal. Smoothing a transition changes who receives support and how much funding is needed. The community fund below is entirely fictional: its schedules are not Hong Kong government benefits, legal entitlements or advice about a real applicant.

Your classroom decision

You help design a proposed monthly discretionary community grant in Hong Kong. The planning cohort contains 80 fictional adult applicants with otherwise comparable circumstances for this exercise. A separate, fully funded service protects urgent needs and existing commitments; none of that support can be removed. No actual eligibility or payment decision is being made.

The fund has HK$60,000 for the month’s grants. Administration is separately funded and assumed equal under both schedules. H pays HK$1,000 when assessed monthly earnings are HK$10,000 or less, and zero above that. T pays HK$900 up to HK$8,000, then reduces the grant by HK$0.10 for each HK$1 earned above HK$8,000, reaching zero at HK$17,000; it never pays a negative grant. The relevant earnings are after compulsory payroll deductions. For the classroom calculation, taxes, other benefits and necessary expenses do not change between schedules or with the small earnings change later.

At the recorded earnings, both schedules use the entire grant budget. H concentrates larger grants below its threshold. T gives less to those three groups while also supporting the group above that threshold. The fund’s stated aims are to help people with fewer resources and to avoid making an earnings increase leave someone with less. These aims may compete under a fixed budget. Recommend a schedule before any terms are announced; no recipient has an existing promise under either proposed rule.

Round 2 · More information

Before the rules are adopted, one applicant in the HK$10,000 group receives a confirmed HK$100 increase in assessed monthly earnings, to HK$10,100, without changing hours or necessary expenses. All other earnings and circumstances remain unchanged. Apply each proposed schedule to the updated earnings in the same month. There is no grace period in either draft; any transition protection would need an explicit, costed revision, not an invented exception.

The committee asks for the applicant’s earnings plus grant before and after the raise, and the revised total grant cost. These resources exclude the unchanged other items, so their change is comparable within this exercise. The applicant decides their own employment choices; the fund must not advise refusing a raise, conceal earnings or change already promised support.

Your decision

After the HK$100 earnings increase, would you retain your recommendation or redesign it before launch? Calculate the applicant’s resource change and the fund’s revised cost, then explain who would finance any protection you add.

Follow resources through the threshold

Under H, the applicant initially has HK$10,000 + HK$1,000 = HK$11,000. After the raise, the grant is zero and resources are HK$10,100. Earnings rose HK$100 but these resources fell HK$900. The lost grant of HK$1,000 is ten times the increase. This discontinuity is a benefit cliff.

Under T, the original grant is HK$900 − 0.10 × (HK$10,000 − HK$8,000) = HK$700. At HK$10,100 it is HK$690. Resources rise from HK$10,700 to HK$10,790, a gain of HK$90. The taper avoids this cliff in the simplified isolated schedule, but gives a smaller initial grant.

A withdrawal rate is not an average tax rate

For this HK$100 change, H withdraws HK$1,000 ÷ HK$100 = 1,000% of the additional earnings. That is an effective withdrawal rate over the stated interval crossing a discontinuity, not a literal income-tax rate or a smooth derivative at the cutoff. T withdraws HK$10 ÷ HK$100 = 10%, leaving 90% of the increase.

This is a schedule calculation, not proof anyone will change hours, refuse advancement or become better off over a lifetime. Real resources depend on other programmes, taxes, work costs, household circumstances and time horizons. A smooth single grant does not guarantee a smooth combined benefit system.

Equal starting budgets do not mean equal budgets forever

Only one H payment disappears, so the revised H total is HK$60,000 − HK$1,000 = HK$59,000. Under T one grant falls by HK$10, so the total is HK$59,990. Both fit the original ceiling. Do not silently redistribute the remainder; state a rule for it.

A one-month protection retaining that person’s original HK$1,000 H grant would add HK$1,000 to the revised H total, bringing it back to HK$60,000. That is affordable in this one supplied scenario, but is a different schedule, delays rather than necessarily removes the later cliff, and is not proof that all future transitions can be funded. New applicants or lower earnings can change costs.

A taper has a distributional price

At the original earnings, T reduces each grant by HK$100, HK$200 and HK$300 in the first three groups. Their combined reduction is 20 × (100 + 200 + 300) = HK$12,000. The fourth group receives 20 × HK$600 = HK$12,000. No additional money has appeared.

T is a strong option if avoiding the illustrated cliff warrants that redistribution. H prioritises larger support below the cutoff but must acknowledge its discontinuity. A redesigned taper or transition rule should show its recipients, costs and limits before adoption. Consult people affected, use understandable notices, protect data, provide accessible correction and review, and never treat income alone as a complete description of need.

Instructor note

0–4 minutes: vote on H versus T and identify the distributional sacrifice. 4–8 minutes: reveal the raise and calculate the applicant’s resources. 8–12 minutes: compare the finite-change withdrawal rates and both programme budgets. 12–15 minutes: test a costed transition proposal and explain why it may only defer the cliff.

Students design a fictional rule, not administer eligibility to real people. Do not make moral claims about willingness to work or suggest concealing earnings. Equal opening spending is cohort-specific. Keep the HK$100 earnings increase separate from the HK$1,000 grant loss, and distinguish the fund’s budget from an applicant’s resources. This is a benefit-schedule case, not allocating one indivisible facility or choosing cash versus goods.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All applicants, earnings, grant rules, money and programme protections are original fiction. No actual government benefit or legal entitlement is described. Assessed earnings are after compulsory payroll deductions; other taxes, benefits and necessary expenses are stipulated unchanged for the comparison. Administration is separately and equally funded. H includes exactly HK$10,000. T is max(0, 900 − 0.10 × max(0, earnings − 8,000)). Starting costs are both HK$60,000; one earnings change gives H HK$59,000 and T HK$59,990. Resources change −HK$900 and +HK$90. One-month H protection can return the single scenario to HK$60,000 but is not a permanent cliff cure or universally affordable rule. All urgent obligations and existing commitments are protected. Sources read 16 November 2026 provide concepts and foreign examples, not evidence about Hong Kong eligibility or applicants. No behavioural, poverty-reduction or welfare effect is estimated.

PPL-26 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Attribution · Task conditions · Development feedback

A slower colleague. Or a harder queue?

A result needs an explanation, not just a label

Attribution research examines how people explain another person’s behaviour using both personal and situational factors. A visible outcome does not reveal its cause on its own. Research also cautions against assuming that finding a situational influence means personal differences cannot matter.

The service team and all observations below are fictional. The decision concerns paid developmental support, not a rating, pay, promotion or disciplinary action. Public guidance supplies ideas for evidence-based feedback; foreign public-sector procedures are not rules for this fictional Hong Kong employer.

Your classroom decision

You supervise a business-services helpdesk. Last week, adviser Mei completed 36 cases while adviser Pat completed 48. Each had 30 productive casework hours, net of breaks, meetings and other duties. Both had enough assigned work to remain occupied. The same closure rules applied, including required documentation; quality checks found no recorded failure. Completion count is a signal to investigate, not an agreed standalone target.

The dashboard does not show assignment difficulty, waiting dependencies or the steps each adviser used. You have two paid supervisor hours available tomorrow. Use them either for A, a focused routine-work coaching session with Mei, or B, a short assignment-and-process review with both advisers before choosing any targeted coaching. Both routes begin with a respectful conversation and permit correction of records. The other activity can happen in next week’s existing development time, so this is an order-of-work choice, not a permanent ban on either support.

Mei has not been asked to explain the difference. You must tell Mei what the first session is for without calling anyone lazy or incapable. No imminent customer deadline or known safety problem requires skipping the review. Coaching could uncover useful practices quickly, but selecting it as a remedy for low effort or poor skill would assume a cause not yet established.

Round 2 · More information

The assignment log shows that 20 of Mei’s 36 completed cases were exceptions requiring external verification; 8 of Pat’s 48 were exceptions. Routine completions were therefore 16 and 40. Mei had been routed more exceptions because of prior experience, not because of an error by either adviser. Exception cases generally need additional coordination, but the records do not quantify a reliable time premium or fully describe unfinished work. Do not convert the counts into an exact adjustment.

A separate check of ten routine cases per adviser from that week matches the documented request type, completeness of input, software version and required checks. Mean active handling time is 31 minutes for Mei and 29 for Pat; all twenty cases pass the same quality check. These are selected observational cases, not a randomised test. Interruptions, individual case nuances and variation around the means are not fully recorded. No specific ineffective step has yet been identified. Mei welcomes discussing the workflow, but says the exception-heavy queue leaves the raw completion comparison feeling misleading.

Your decision

How should the new evidence change your explanation and next support step? Interpret the two-minute routine-case gap without dismissing it or calling it proof of a skill problem, and design a fair follow-up comparison.

What the first count establishes

Mei completed 12 fewer cases, or 25% fewer than Pat’s 48. That is an output difference over equal recorded casework hours, not a measured difference in motivation or ability. Without difficulty, workflow and unfinished-work information, neither the fraction attributable to assignment mix nor the fraction attributable to individual practice can be calculated.

Context changes the inference

Exceptions are 20/36, about 55.6%, of Mei’s completions and 8/48, about 16.7%, of Pat’s. The differing composition provides a plausible situational explanation for part of the aggregate gap. It does not establish exactly how much, because exception duration and work in progress are missing. Prior experience explains the routing decision but does not prove current superiority at every task.

Do not erase the remaining signal

The selected routine-case comparison shows a two-minute mean difference: 31 − 29. It is about 6.9% of Pat’s 29-minute mean. Matching several conditions makes this more informative than raw total closures, but it is not a causal estimate or a statistically established persistent gap. Equal quality removes one observed trade-off, not every explanation. The finding warrants a proportionate conversation about steps and conditions, not a character label or automatic exoneration.

Choose support that can distinguish explanations

Prefer the assignment-and-process review first here. Ask both advisers to explain the workflow and correct the log; inspect a small agreed set of routine cases for avoidable steps, interruptions and input differences. If a specific practice is missing, offer targeted coaching with paid practice time. If routing or a tool creates delay, fix that condition. Coaching is supportive when linked to an observed need, not a punishment for occupying the harder queue.

Make the next comparison fairer

Over the next two weeks, compare routine cases of the same defined types with similar input completeness and the same required quality checks. Agree the sampling rule before seeing outcomes, record relevant interruptions and unfinished cases, and avoid cherry-picking only fast completions. Where service needs permit, balance comparable assignments without withholding necessary exception expertise. Compare quality and handling-time distributions, not just one mean. This remains a bounded learning exercise, not covert surveillance or proof that all unobserved differences have vanished.

Instructor note

Allow 3 minutes for the opening sentence, 5 for the reveal and arithmetic, and 7 for a follow-up plan. A sound initial coaching choice must frame coaching as exploration rather than a proven remedy. After the reveal, reward answers that retain both the assignment explanation and the unresolved routine signal. Do not demand a numerical decomposition unsupported by the records.

This is not recency in annual appraisal and not a Simpson reversal: no within-group ranking reverses the overall ranking. The central question is what a manager may reasonably infer and do under incomplete evidence. A useful sentence is: “The log shows fewer completions; I want to understand the work mix and steps before deciding what support would help.” Do not diagnose personality, health or effort. No legal standard, formal sanction or real employee action is being recommended.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All advisers, records, hours, counts and handling times are original fiction. Completion counts are not a standalone performance standard. Equal productive hours do not imply equal assignment conditions. Exception-time premiums and unfinished work are unquantified. The matched routine sample contains ten selected cases per adviser, not random assignment; the two-minute mean difference has no supplied uncertainty estimate or established cause. Quality checks passed in the supplied records, not a guarantee of flawless work. Both review and coaching use paid time; this exercise makes no pay, promotion, dismissal or disciplinary decision. Foreign guidance is conceptual context, not Hong Kong employment law.

STR-21 · Hong Kong classroom scenario; Vancouver historical context · 15 minutes · Emergent strategy · Strategic learning

Follow the plan. Recognise a better pattern?

From a game to a communication tool

In November 2012, Vancouver game developer Tiny Speck decided to close Glitch. An internal communication system had become useful to its own team; that experience helped motivate the move toward Slack. This was not simply executing the original game plan. Johnny Rodgers later described the decision, and investor John O’Farrell recalled backing the change despite the game’s failure.

Outside demand still had to be learned. In a February 2015 interview, Stewart Butterfield described inviting other companies to try Slack and discovering problems as team sizes grew. The lesson is not that every side project deserves a pivot. An unexpected pattern can deserve examination without yet proving a new strategy.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom case. You run Harbour Studio, a small Hong Kong design service. Its approved annual direction is to produce retail catalogues. Over eight weeks, a junior team has accepted six unsolicited training-pack jobs from four unrelated training providers. Two providers returned for another job. All six paid the same quoted price, with no discounts. The team wants training providers recognised as a second target market.

Existing customer contracts are fully staffed separately. For the next four weeks you have exactly forty discretionary staff-hours. Staff time is interchangeable for this exercise; no extra hiring or overtime is allowed. The two proposed programmes are indivisible except for reducing the number of trial jobs. No signed commitment requires either programme, and the two retail pitches can be postponed without penalty.

Choose one use of the hours. A trial would authorise only this month’s work, not a permanent change of target market. Both programmes must finish within the same forty-hour limit. Do not combine them or assume extra resources.

Round 2 · More information

New evidence, same decision and capacity. A workload check finds that every training job used two additional senior-review hours charged to a general-help code. The demand is genuine: all four providers are unrelated, and the repeat orders were separately paid. The missing hours, however, recur on every job. The senior reviewer is included in the same forty-hour pool, not a free resource.

For this month, no safe way to remove that review has been demonstrated. Six jobs would therefore require 4 + 6 × 8 = 52 hours, exceeding the limit. Four jobs would require 4 + 4 × 8 = 36 hours, leaving four hours of slack. Those four hours cannot fund the indivisible retail campaign. Enough new training requests exist for either trial size; there is no evidence of later demand.

All other facts stay fixed. The feasible choices are the full retail campaign or a smaller four-job training trial. The hidden work does not erase the customer pattern, but it changes what the company can responsibly authorise.

Your decision

With the full workload visible, return the forty hours to retail or authorise only four training jobs? Choose one and name a condition for continuing beyond this month.

What changed?

The reveal separates two claims that were bundled together. Repeated paid orders support the claim that the team found a real use for its service. They do not support the claim that six jobs fit the month’s capacity. Neither the original plan nor the sales pattern settles the allocation automatically.

From local pattern to deliberate commitment

Intended strategy is the direction managers mean to pursue: retail catalogues. Realised strategy is the pattern visible in actual activity: retail work plus repeated training jobs. A persistent pattern not originally intended can be described as emergent strategy. Six jobs are a signal worth examining, not conclusive proof of a durable market position.

Recognition is different from endorsement. Management can deliberately authorise a bounded continuation, reject it, or later revise the target market. Deliberate and emergent elements can coexist; a strategy need not be entirely planned or entirely accidental.

Two defensible decisions

Return to retail: protect attention for the existing direction and avoid formalising a service whose full workload was hidden. This sacrifices the chance to learn from actual training demand this month. A good defence must explain why two uncertain retail pitches deserve the scarce block of hours, not merely say that a plan must never change.

Authorise four training jobs: buy bounded evidence about an already observed pattern while recording all hours. This sacrifices the retail campaign and cannot establish long-run demand in one month. A good defence must accept that opportunity cost, name a responsible owner, and refuse unapproved fifth and sixth jobs.

A review rule before the work begins

If choosing the trial, specify what would justify continuation: for example, each job must finish within eight fully recorded hours, customers must accept the normal scope and price, and the next decision must consider demand beyond these four requests. A workload overrun pauses further acceptance and triggers a new resource decision; it does not justify hidden overtime. Meeting the rule earns a review, not automatic expansion.

If choosing retail, keep the evidence of training demand rather than relabelling it as failure. State what future evidence would reopen the strategic question and what work would have to be displaced then. Waiting is a choice with a cost, not a free option.

Instructor note

Allow fifteen minutes: two for the reported event and fictional role, three for the first vote, three for the reveal and revote, four for rival defences, and three for the strategy distinction. Ask retail supporters what would make them revise the plan. Ask trial supporters exactly which opportunity they are giving up and when they would stop.

Do not award a single numerical winner. The workload calculation rules out six jobs, not the entire training direction. Insist on a choice between the two feasible programmes. Avoid turning the discussion into activity-system fit: the focal question is how an observed local pattern earns, or fails to earn, management authorisation. The Slack history illustrates recognition and subsequent learning, not a prediction that Harbour Studio will succeed.

PPL-23 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Collective effort · Task interdependence · Contribution visibility

One team bonus. Whose contribution is visible?

A shared result can hide the work behind it

Research on collective effort distinguishes motivation from the ability to identify individual contributions. Team-process research also treats coordination and helping as real work, not just the finished items that visitors see. Those ideas raise a practical question: what evidence would help this team without turning preparation into paperwork?

The exhibition, bonus, people and hours below are invented. A delayed output is not a diagnosis of low effort.

Your classroom decision

Four employees at a fictional Hong Kong design studio voluntarily join an optional exhibition project during paid working time. They have a fixed remaining allowance of 24 person-hours across the team. One person working an hour uses one person-hour; a 15-minute meeting attended by all four uses one person-hour. Normal duties, breaks and employment entitlements are protected; unpaid overtime is not an option.

The current estimate assigns 18 person-hours to essential building and content work, leaving six for rehearsal and contribution review. Everyone knows the readiness checklist. If the team meets it, the pre-agreed HK$4,000 exhibition bonus is shared equally, HK$1,000 each. Neither plan changes the checklist, pay, entitlements or bonus rule. This is not a decision to retrospectively withhold anyone’s pay.

Plan A uses a half-person-hour final-output review, with members briefly explaining their work at completion. It preserves more rehearsal time but relies on recollection and finished results. Plan B uses a simple responsibility-and-dependency log plus short check-ins: two person-hours in total, including one person-hour of individual updates and one person-hour for a four-person 15-minute meeting. The two hours include all setup and review for this plan.

The log records agreed owner, needed input, handoff, help supplied and unresolved block, not keystrokes, private messages or minute-by-minute surveillance. Entries are visible to the team and correctable. Both plans are feasible. No reliable probability of passing the checklist under either plan is available.

Your decision

Would you choose the lightweight output review or the responsibility-and-dependency log? Explain what you gain, what preparation time you give up and how the shared bonus affects the question.

Round 2 · More information

Before the 24-hour preparation allowance is spent, the team reviews an earlier prototype. Its work was completed in a separate, already-finished allocation; none of those hours is part of the current budget. The following observations are verified, but they do not provide an individual effort ranking.

Prototype observationVerified context
Mei: no finished display panelHer layout awaited approved copy; she also checked the shared image files
Jun: three finished display panelsThese used already-approved copy; the panels differ in size and difficulty
Merged demonstration: inconsistent versionsNobody was assigned to reconcile versions and confirm handoffs

The team confirms that merging versions and checking handoffs is necessary for this exhibition, requires two additional person-hours, and was omitted from the original 18-hour estimate. That task is now mandatory under either plan. The team can assign a competent willing owner and a backup within the revised work allocation; all their execution time is included in the two hours. No external delay, training or extra funding is needed.

At least two person-hours of rehearsal are required by the readiness checklist. Extra rehearsal remains useful but its effect is not quantified. You may select A or B, assign the missing work and spend the remaining allowance on rehearsal. Do not assume the log itself completes the merging work, saves a measured number of hours or proves who tried hardest. Bonus terms remain unchanged.

Your decision

After the dependency problem appears, which review approach would you use within the unchanged allowance? Assign the missing work, show the time budget and explain what you would not infer from visible outputs.

Separate an incentive risk from a diagnosis

When effort disappears into a joint result, people may doubt whether their own contribution matters or will be recognised. This can create a motivation risk. It does not establish that anyone in this team reduced effort. A missed handoff, missing skill or absent input can also explain delayed output. The prototype identifies a coordination gap, not a culprit.

Read the dependency, not just the count

Mei’s zero finished panels does not mean zero contribution: approved copy was unavailable and she supported shared files. Jun’s three panels are genuine output, but unequal difficulty and input readiness prevent treating three versus zero as a fair effort score. Neither observation proves equal effort either. Ask what was expected, what inputs were available, what support occurred and what evidence remains missing.

Repair the work allocation first

Give the version merge an agreed owner, backup, input list and handoff point. Confirm that the people supplying copy and panels know what the owner needs and when. Recognise quiet checking and helping alongside visible production. Both A and B must solve this known gap; a log cannot substitute for someone doing the work.

Price the review in person-hours

Revised essential work is 18 + 2 = 20 person-hours. A leaves 24 − 20 − 0.5 = 3.5 person-hours for rehearsal. B leaves 24 − 20 − 2 = 2 person-hours. Both meet the stated rehearsal minimum. B uses 1.5 more person-hours than A, exactly the extra rehearsal A preserves.

The difference is not two clock-hours for each employee and not a cash saving: paid capacity is fixed. Do not add the four-person meeting again; it is already inside B’s two-hour total. No expected bonus or productivity gain can be calculated without additional evidence.

Choose proportionate visibility

B is defensible when ongoing dependencies make early detection and a shared record worth sacrificing the extra rehearsal. Use it to surface blocks and helping, not rank staff by number of updates. A remains defensible if the newly assigned handoff is straightforward and the team values the additional rehearsal; document that assignment and acknowledge the limitations of a retrospective review.

A recommendation should specify what would trigger a change next time: repeated unclear ownership, unresolved inputs, avoidable rework, or documentation that consumes its allowance without improving decisions. More visible activity is not automatically more useful contribution. Do not reward log verbosity, force identical output counts or change a promised bonus after seeing the result.

Instructor note

Ask learners to distinguish person-hours from meeting duration before voting. Reveal the prototype and require a revised 24-hour budget. Reward plans that assign the missing coordination under either option and retain the fixed bonus rule. Discuss the difference between identifying contribution and blaming someone for low visible output. Evidence can improve coordination without proving motivation; this paper exercise is not an employee assessment or employment-law advice.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All names, work, prototype, hours and bonus terms are fictional. The project is optional, paid and feasible; mandatory employment protections are unaffected. Both approaches include a review and can assign the known missing task. Two-person-hour coordination execution is separate from two-person-hour logging. No individual diagnosis, surveillance or retrospective pay change is authorised. Sources optional.

  1. Karau and Williams · Social Loafing: A Meta-Analytic Review and Theoretical Integration · 1993. Original meta-analysis discusses evaluability, meaningfulness and collective effort. Supports a possible motivation mechanism, not a diagnosis of any named fictional member or guaranteed benefit from logging.
  2. CIPD/CEBMa · High-performing teams: Scientific summary · May 2023. Evidence review distinguishes performance behaviours/outcomes, team processes and contextual limits. No published effect size is applied to the fictional hours or bonus.
  3. UK HSE · Management Standards: Role. Official practical guidance concerns clear compatible responsibilities and routes to raise uncertainty. Comparative management guidance, not a Hong Kong legal rule.
  4. Atlassian Team Playbook · Roles and Responsibilities. Original practitioner process identifies disagreements and unassigned responsibilities. Used as a practical example, not independent proof of performance gains; no vendor statistics or tool purchase needed.
  5. Marks, Mathieu and Zaccaro · A Temporally Based Framework and Taxonomy of Team Processes · 2001. Accessible reproduction of the original theoretical article; coordination and backup sections read. Publisher page was inaccessible. Conceptual taxonomy, not causal proof that this log improves output.

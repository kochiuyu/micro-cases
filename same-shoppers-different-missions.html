MKT-30 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Occasion segmentation · Shopping missions · Unit of analysis

One customer profile. Two shopping missions?

The reporting question

Marketing research separates shopping circumstances from enduring person characteristics. This fictional pilot makes that distinction visible without assuming a demographic group has one fixed need.

Your classroom decision

At fictional Hong Kong grocer Lane Basket, Mei must brief two equally sized communication panels for adult shoppers aged 30–49 in the same neighbourhood. Option G repeats “Groceries for local adults aged 30–49” on both panels. Option M uses one panel for an emergency top-up and one for a planned weekly shop. Either option costs HK$2,000 in total. Prices, assortment, product quality and service capacity stay identical; no discount or faster-service promise is authorised.

A voluntary, consented diary pilot supplies 12 purchase-occasion records: six emergency top-ups and six planned weekly shops. On each top-up, the shopper reports needing a forgotten ingredient for tonight and wanting to locate it quickly. On each weekly shop, the shopper reports using a list and wanting to compare unit prices across that list. These are stated purposes, not inferred from age, basket size or time of day. At this stage, the records are not linked by person. The manager wants useful communication, not a claim that one option has already increased sales.

Available featureWhat can honestly be communicated
Top-up supportAn aisle finder for locating an item; no guarantee of stock or queue time.
Weekly-shop supportA list planner displaying current unit prices; no promise of lowest prices.
Access and privacyBoth panels appear together in store and on an open webpage. Anyone may choose either tool on each visit. No individual tracking or sensitive profiling is required.
Evidence boundaryTwelve selected occasions in a small pilot; no representative sample, message experiment or sales-effect estimate.
Your decision

Should the grocer use one demographic message or two mission-based messages? Specify the benefit and call to action for each proposed communication.

Round 2 · More information

The research lead now links the records using consented pseudonymous diary IDs. Adults A, B, C, D, E and F each recorded exactly one emergency top-up on one day and one planned weekly shop on another. Thus there are six people and 12 occasions, not 12 different customers. Every person appears in both missions; their demographic group and buyer/user roles stay unchanged.

A proposed database rule would label A–C “top-up customers” and D–F “weekly customers” and show each person only that assigned message thereafter. The research lead asks Mei to approve or replace the rule. This pilot contains no other missions and gives no evidence about the proportion of future visits in either mission. Any new research must be voluntary and avoid collecting sensitive traits.

Your decision

Do the linked diaries justify two permanent customer segments? Revise the targeting rule and propose one test that would distinguish relevance from a sales claim.

A mission is not a demographic

Choose M as the better-supported design hypothesis: its two benefits match the supplied purposes, whereas the age-based message does not explain either task. This is not proof of superior conversion. Suitable copy is “Forgot an ingredient for tonight? Use the aisle finder” and “Planning your weekly shop? Compare unit prices with the list planner.” Both are available to everyone, without assuming that all top-ups ignore price or all weekly shoppers have ample time.

What the linkage changes

The six people generate six records in each mission. Each individual belongs to both occasion categories, so a mutually exclusive permanent partition is unsupported. Under the proposed A–C/D–F rule, one of each person’s two recorded missions would receive the mismatched message: six of 12 occasions. That count diagnoses this supplied history, not a future error rate. Keep the occasion as the unit of segmentation and let shoppers select their current task anew.

A modest, discriminating test

In a voluntary follow-up, ask adults their current mission before showing randomly assigned generic or mission-matched copy. Balance allocation within each stated mission; keep prices, availability and tools constant. Predefine comprehension and successful tool selection as relevance measures. Compare results within missions and record repeat participation appropriately. If sales uplift is claimed, measure incremental purchases in an adequately designed experiment rather than treating clicks or diary statements as sales. Allow other missions and “neither”; do not force all visits into these two categories.

Limits and transfer

Situational research and shopper-mission methods motivate examining occasions. They do not supply this pilot, validate its proposed messages or establish local effect sizes. Some published satisfaction findings have small effects and use repeated cross-sections rather than following the same people. Here, the fictional linked diaries themselves establish switching. This exercise holds the people and their roles fixed; it changes the purpose of each visit.

Instructor note

Give the opening and exhibit first. Ask groups to draft two truthful messages without adding discounts or service guarantees. Reveal the person links only after Q1, then ask Q2 before discussing either answer. Debrief the unit of analysis: six people, 12 occasions, six historical mismatches under the arbitrary permanent rule. Accept a cautious generic pilot proposal if students explicitly distinguish insufficient causal evidence from the clear mission differences. Close by separating useful relevance tests from unsupported revenue promises.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

Fictional teaching case. All people, messages, diary records, features and costs are invented. Sources inform concepts and limitations, not claims about a real grocer or causal effects.

  1. Russell W. Belk, Situational Variables and Consumer Behavior (1975). Distinguishes enduring person characteristics from situation and task; conceptual and empirical background, not these diary observations.
  2. Sonja Wendel, Situation Variation in Consumer Benefit Salience (2007), Chapter 2. Original research on situation-dependent information benefits and channel consideration. Different context; no grocery-message uplift is imported.
  3. Hunneman, Verhoef and Sloot, The moderating role of shopping trip type in store satisfaction formation (2017). Original Dutch repeated-cross-section study; its small interaction effects and measurement limitations caution against universal mission stereotypes.
  4. Knox, Bell and Corsten, Situational Determinants of Unplanned Buying in Emerging and Developed Markets, MSI Report 11-114. Original trip-level diary research and explicit mission questions. Background for the occasion unit, not permission to predict or stimulate this pilot’s unplanned purchases.
  5. YouGov, Shopping Missions. Provider’s own explanation of classifying purchase occasions through basket composition and circumstances. Commercial methodology description, not independent proof of message effectiveness.

HSP-06 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Hospitality · Table mix · Capacity planning

Same seats. Same capacity?

In the news · Tables matter as well as seats

Hong Kong announced a change to its per-table dining limit on 30 September 2022. That historical policy is not a constraint in this fictional case, which explores how ordinary table sizes fit different groups.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A small Hong Kong restaurant is choosing between two layouts for its next trial dinner seating. Layout A has six four-person tables. Layout B has four four-person tables and four two-person tables. Both have 24 chairs and the same trial cost.

For this exercise, both options have passed the necessary professional checks, preserve accessible circulation and exits, and can receive equally adequate kitchen and staff service. These are classroom assumptions, not claims of real approval.

All customer requests will be for one 90-minute seating, with no table turns. Tables are fixed for this trial: no combining tables, adding chairs, splitting parties or seating unrelated parties together. These are operating assumptions, not rules attributed to law. The owner says either layout should work because both have 24 seats. The party-size mix is not yet known.

Round 2 · One fact changes

The host receives requests from four parties of four and four parties of two: 24 diners in eight parties. These are requests, not confirmed bookings. Everyone wants the same 90-minute seating.

The original constraints still apply: each party needs its own table, with no splitting, sharing, combining, extra chairs or table turns. No spending or margin information is supplied.

Your decision

Can either layout accommodate all eight requested parties together? Which would you choose for this seating, and what would you check before adopting it for every service?

Layout A: empty chairs do not make another whole table

Place the four parties of four at four tables. The two remaining tables can each accommodate one party of two. That seats 16 + 4 = 20 requested diners in six parties.

Four chairs remain unoccupied at tables already used by the two smaller parties. They cannot accommodate the remaining parties under the no-sharing rule. Twenty is the maximum number of requested diners that A can seat in this mix, not a claim that the room physically has only 20 chairs.

This calculation is a capacity ceiling, not a rule about which requests deserve priority. How the restaurant handles unaccommodated requests is a separate service decision.

Layout B: the table sizes fit these requests

Its four four-person tables accommodate the four larger parties; its four two-person tables accommodate the four smaller parties. All eight parties and 24 diners fit simultaneously.

B therefore fits this seating’s requests better under the stated constraints. No higher revenue or profit follows automatically: customer spending and margins are unknown, and the objective here is accommodating the requested parties.

Change the party mix, change the result

Suppose instead that six parties of four request the same seating. A accommodates all six parties and 24 diners. B accommodates only four parties and 16 diners: its two-person tables cannot combine, and parties cannot split.

B is not universally superior. A larger number of small tables is useful for one demand mix and unsuitable for another. Neither illustration establishes the restaurant’s typical demand.

Trial a layout against realistic demand

Review party-size patterns by day and service period, along with arrival times and dining durations. In a real restaurant, assess whether table combinations are practical rather than assuming either that all tables combine or that none do.

Use a reversible trial of a feasible layout and monitor accommodated requests, waiting or declined requests and service quality. Preserve accessibility, circulation and exits. Other layouts may be possible; the classroom comparison deliberately limits the choice to two feasible options.

Instructor note

Ask students to place whole parties, not simply count people. In A, two couples can sit at four-person tables, but the remaining chairs do not become two additional tables. Make the no-sharing and no-combining assumptions visible before the reveal.

Then introduce six parties of four to challenge the idea that B is always better. The lesson is resource fit, not a universal layout, spending forecast or fairness rule.

All layouts, requests and operating constraints are invented. Professional feasibility is assumed for the exercise; this is not a licence, safety assessment or legal instruction for a real restaurant.

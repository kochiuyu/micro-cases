STR-04 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Strategy · International business · Product adaptation

Export the product, or export the promise?

In the news · What travels with the brand?

In a 4 February 2026 announcement, Jollibee Group described Hong Kong as Tim Ho Wan’s benchmark market for operating standards supporting expansion. It pointed to procedures, chef capabilities and quality controls. These are the company’s own statements, not independent evidence that every overseas format succeeds.

The strategic question is broader than copying a product: which capabilities and promises should stay consistent, and which features can change? Our organiser maker below is fictional and unrelated to Tim Ho Wan.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. You run a Hong Kong maker of modular wooden desktop organisers. Your domestic bestseller is 40 cm deep. Customers value durable construction and removable dividers that let them rearrange their workspace.

A retailer in a new overseas market invites you to propose a launch. Exporting the existing design would avoid new tooling and keep one product specification. A shallower version would add development work and a second specification to support.

Your brand promise is durable, adaptable organisation, not a particular depth. You will not weaken materials or abandon removable dividers. The retailer’s precise fit requirement and commercial terms have not yet arrived. You have not signed or started production.

Keep consistentPotentially adaptable

A domestic bestseller is evidence about existing customers, not proof of fit in this new channel.

Your decision

Would you offer the existing organiser unchanged, develop a shallower version, or seek a different retail channel? What must stay the same for the brand promise to remain credible?

Round 2 · One fact changes

The retailer confirms that this launch requires an organiser no more than 30 cm deep, to fit its display and the desks targeted by this particular range. The 40 cm version cannot qualify. This is a channel-specific commercial requirement, not a law or a claim about everyone in that country.

A compliant version preserving quality and removable dividers is technically feasible. Its one-off development and tooling cost is HK$120,000. At the proposed selling price, contribution after all recurring variable costs, including export fulfilment and the expected cost of returns, is HK$60 per unit.

The retailer offers a firm order of 1,000 units conditional on delivery of that compliant version. For this exercise assume those units are delivered and paid for if you proceed. Further demand is uncommitted. No other incremental fixed costs or residual tooling value are assumed in this simplified calculation. You have enough cash to fund development, but spending it still has an opportunity cost.

Your decision

Would you commit to the shallower version on these terms? Calculate what the first order covers, then identify the evidence or commitment you would need before investing.

Separate the promise from the specification

Reducing depth need not weaken the brand if durability and useful modularity survive. Test the shallower version with the intended users rather than assuming that a physically compliant product is useful.

Keeping the existing design remains a possible strategy, but not for this retailer’s stated launch. You would need a different channel or segment with suitable space and demand. Neither “copy everything” nor “adapt everything” is a universal rule.

What does the first order cover?

1,000 × HK$60 = HK$60,000 contribution. Against HK$120,000 one-off cost, the first order leaves HK$60,000 unrecovered.

HK$120,000 ÷ HK$60 = 2,000 units would cover the stated development and tooling cost at unchanged unit contribution. That means 1,000 additional units beyond the first order, not a forecast that they will sell.

This is a narrow incremental cost-coverage calculation, not whole-company profit, a cash-flow timetable or a risk-adjusted investment valuation. More sales at a lower contribution would change the threshold.

Choose a conditional next move

You could seek a larger binding commitment or a development contribution, validate additional channels, or decline this launch and preserve the existing design. A deliberately funded learning investment is also possible, but explain why its uncertain future value justifies the unrecovered cost.

Before proceeding, specify who will test the adapted product, the quality standard, responsibility for supporting two versions and a limit on further spending. Do not mistake a retailer’s interest for committed repeat orders.

Key idea: preserve the customer promise, adapt only what the chosen market needs, and test whether that adaptation earns its cost.

Instructor note

Allow 2 minutes to distinguish core promise from specification, 3 for the fit reveal and 5–7 for cost coverage and a conditional recommendation. Ask whether students mean this channel or an entire country. Accept an evidence-based investment, renegotiation or alternative-channel strategy; do not make automatic adaptation the model answer. Compare the threshold with committed demand, not with an invented probability.

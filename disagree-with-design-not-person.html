PPL-37 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Task and relationship conflict · Facilitated reset · Constructive dissent

A useful disagreement. An escalating quarrel.

Preserve the disagreement; change how it is handled

Disagreement about a proposal is not the same as disagreement about a colleague’s character. Research distinguishes task, process and relationship conflict, with outcomes that depend on context. This fictional meeting asks how to preserve useful challenge when discussion becomes personal. It does not diagnose employees or promise that a particular intervention improves performance.

Your classroom decision

You lead fictional Harbour Loop, a Hong Kong product-design team launching a small exhibition guide next month. Two feasible designs meet the same safety, accessibility, privacy, cost and deadline requirements. No client commitment has been made about either design. Four adult colleagues must compare visitor clarity and flexibility before you make the authorised final choice.

Design A uses a fixed sequence of sections, making the intended reading order explicit. Design B allows visitors to choose a section first, making different interests easier to accommodate. Both also provide a complete index. User testing has not established an overall winner. Everyone already has the same evidence; no hidden annex or disputed handoff is involved.

Route D uses tomorrow’s paid forty-minute slot for structured debate: compare each design against the two agreed criteria, challenge assumptions and record unresolved evidence. Route R first uses that slot for a facilitated reset of how colleagues discuss the issue, then uses a separately funded forty-minute slot the next day for design evaluation.

Both routes fit the decision deadline and normal paid hours. A trained neutral internal facilitator, not either designer’s supervisor, is available without extra fee. R displaces optional planning work to an already-funded slot next week. It consumes more scheduled team time, not free capacity. Neither route guarantees consensus; you remain responsible for the design decision.

Round 2 · More information

The agreed meeting record begins with: “A gives readers a clear order”; “B supports visitors who arrive with one question.” Later someone asks who will prepare the comparison and whether both proposals get equal speaking time. Finally come: “You always need to win”; “You dismiss anything that is not yours.” These fictional remarks are not verified facts about either person.

After those personal remarks, interruptions increase and neither proposal receives a complete response. In brief separate check-ins, both designers say they still want a useful guide and want both designs assessed. They agree voluntarily to the facilitated reset and to sharing that willingness. They have not agreed on a design or resolved their disagreement.

The facilitator proposes agreeing on speaking turns, separating evidence from assumptions about motives, and converting personal accusations into specific observable concerns. The designers may decline a proposed agreement or stop voluntary facilitation. The leader must still require respectful conduct and provide a workable route to the decision, rather than force mediation or demand friendship.

The supplied facts describe an ordinary deteriorating discussion, with no allegation requiring a safeguarding or misconduct investigation. If such a concern emerges, protect people and use the appropriate formal process; a reset cannot replace it or waive rights. Agree confidentiality and permitted reporting in advance rather than promising absolute secrecy.

Your decision

Would you keep or revise your route? Classify the three kinds of disagreement in the record, propose one immediate intervention and one rule for the later design debate. Explain what the facilitator may agree with colleagues and what decision remains the leader’s responsibility.

Debrief · Classify the issue, not the employee

The first remarks concern design merits and are task disagreement. Questions about preparation and speaking time concern the process of doing the work. The later remarks attribute enduring motives or faults to people, adding a relationship dimension. More than one type can coexist; a question about fairness is not automatically a personal attack.

Do not label someone a difficult personality or infer intent from a short record. Use the observed interruptions and incomplete responses to explain why the current discussion is failing. The shared objective remains, but agreement on that objective does not remove the need to repair how competing views are considered.

Debrief · Pause the pattern, not the challenge

Choose R on the revealed facts. Stop the personal exchange and preserve both proposals for later evaluation. The reason is not that dissent must disappear: the discussion is no longer giving either design a fair hearing. The voluntary reset is available, bounded and compatible with the deadline.

A possible opening is: “We still need to test both designs. The last comments were about motives, and we have stopped answering the design questions. Let us pause that exchange and agree how each concern can be heard.” Address observable conduct without deciding which person is generally right or demanding a public apology.

Debrief · Reframe without erasing a concern

Ask what specific action led to a concern and what practical change is sought. “You always need to win” might become a request to let a response finish before rebutting it, but do not supply that interpretation as fact. Let the speaker explain and the other person respond. Agreement on a rule is not agreement with an accusation.

The facilitator helps colleagues find acceptable working arrangements; they do not choose A or B, adjudicate misconduct or compel admissions. Report only agreed operating arrangements and necessary concerns within the stated confidentiality limits. The leader retains responsibility for conduct, resources and the final authorised design decision.

Debrief · Return to the task

For the design debate, give each proposal equal uninterrupted presentation time, then compare its clarity and flexibility against the same existing criteria. Ask for evidence, uncertainties and a response to the strongest objection. Record unresolved differences and the leader’s reasons. Do not turn speaking-time equality into an assumption that both designs have equal merit.

If the reset is declined or fails, use a respectful managed process: obtain written design arguments, allow responses, and make a reasoned decision by the deadline. Keep a review or concern route open. This fallback is management responsibility, not a mediated agreement or punishment for declining voluntary facilitation.

Debrief · Time is a cost, not proof of return

D schedules forty minutes of design discussion. R schedules forty minutes of reset plus forty minutes of design discussion, eighty minutes altogether. It uses forty additional team-session minutes. Those are not forty total person-minutes, and no wage rates or complete individual attendance schedule are supplied to calculate a cash return.

Check whether later meetings allow complete responses, specific evidence and respectful disagreement, not whether all objections vanish. A calmer meeting alone does not prove the chosen design works better for visitors. Research does not establish that this particular reset will succeed, or that all task conflict is useful and all process questions should be suppressed.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–5: choose a route and name the missing behavioural evidence. Minutes 5–10: reveal the record and classify each shift. Minutes 10–15: practise a neutral interruption and preserve a substantive design objection. Finish by separating a voluntary working agreement from the leader’s decision authority.

PPL-09 repairs an ongoing operational handoff; PPL-17 pools previously unshared evidence. This case holds evidence and the shared objective constant while the form of discussion changes. It is a paper exercise, not a personnel diagnosis, disciplinary finding, real mediation or instruction to ignore formal protection routes.

Faster at both. Do both?

In the news · Doing some things well is not doing everything

On 13 February 2026, Hong Kong’s Census and Statistics Department released services-trade figures for 2024. Hong Kong both exported and imported substantial services.

These figures provide context for asking why a capable producer might obtain some work from others. They do not prove that comparative advantage caused particular trade flows. The small fictional team below illustrates one principle, not a complete explanation of Hong Kong trade.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A small Hong Kong retailer needs 32 customer parcels packed. Founder Mei can pack 12 parcels an hour; colleague Sam can pack eight. Both meet the same required quality. No work has started.

Each has four uninterrupted hours before the common deadline. For the opening decision, consider assigning this packing job to one person. The rest of the work brief has not yet arrived, so you do not know what other tasks might compete for their time.

Assume constant rates, sufficient supplies and separate equipment, with no setup, switching, training or coordination time. Either person can do a task, but not two tasks simultaneously. There is no overtime or outsourcing. Staff costs for this four-hour block are fixed across allocations.

Mei12 parcels/hour2 hours 40 minutes
Sam8 parcels/hour4 hours
Your decision

If you assign the 32-parcel packing job to one person, who finishes sooner? What missing information could change whom you should assign?

Round 2 · One fact changes

Before either person starts, the full brief arrives: 24 finished catalogue-product photos are also required by the end of the same four-hour block. The photo items are separate from the customer parcels, so photography does not have to wait for packing, or vice versa.

PersonPacking ratePhotography rate
Mei12 parcels/hour6 finished photos/hour
Sam8 parcels/hour2 finished photos/hour

Both meet the required quality for either task. All original time, equipment and cost assumptions still apply. The targets are 32 parcels and 24 photos, not a combined total of interchangeable items.

Your decision

How would you allocate their four hours to finish both 32 parcels and 24 photos? Does being faster at both mean Mei should do each job? What does Mei give up by spending an hour packing?

Finish both jobs, not just the first one

Assign Mei to photography for four hours: 4 × 6 = 24 photos. Assign Sam to packing for four hours: 4 × 8 = 32 parcels. Both targets are met exactly under the stated assumptions.

Mei is indeed the faster individual packer. That opening answer is not wrong; it answers a narrower question. Once photography also matters, using her time for packing displaces another output the team needs.

Check the tempting alternative

If Mei first packs all 32 parcels, she uses 2 hours 40 minutes and has 1 hour 20 minutes left. At six photos an hour, that remaining time produces eight photos. Even if Sam takes photos throughout his four hours, he adds only eight more.

That plan completes the parcels but produces 16 photos, eight short of the required 24. Do not count spare parcels as compensation for missing photos, or sum the two units into a single productivity score.

Compare the output each task displaces

One hour of Mei’s packing produces 12 parcels instead of six photos. Each parcel therefore costs her 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5 photo of forgone output. One hour of Sam’s packing produces eight parcels instead of two photos: 2 ÷ 8 = 0.25 photo per parcel.

Sam gives up fewer photos per parcel, so he has a comparative advantage in packing. Conversely, one photo costs Mei two parcels and Sam four parcels. Mei has a comparative advantage in photography. These are opportunity costs in alternative output, not wage costs or monetary prices.

Faster at both is not the same as best allocated to both

Mei has an absolute productivity advantage in both tasks: more output per hour at the same quality. But her relative advantage is larger in photography. The allocation uses that difference rather than treating Sam’s lower rates as a reason to leave him without useful work.

This is not a rule to give every task to the slower person. Different targets, deadlines, quality requirements, setup costs or equipment constraints could change the plan. Nor is this a recommendation for permanent job specialisation, pay differences or an assessment of anyone’s development potential.

Instructor note

Keep the opening question explicitly about assigning packing to one person. With both people packing together, the job would take 1 hour 36 minutes under the same rate assumptions, so Mei alone is not the fastest possible team packing plan. The reveal asks a different question: meeting two outputs by one deadline.

Ask students to show both targets and their time allocations before naming comparative advantage. Then have them explain the units in the opportunity-cost comparison. The answer is about what time displaces, not who is more valuable as a person.

All rates and constraints are invented. The trade-news hook is context, not empirical support for these numbers or a claim that this two-task model explains every business or international trade decision.

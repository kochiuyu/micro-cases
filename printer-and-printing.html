A cheaper printer. More expensive printing?

A historical Hong Kong example

On 13 April 2006, Hong Kong’s Consumer Council reported that tested printers differed substantially in consumable and electricity costs, and that its overall performance rating did not include machine or consumable prices. This historical example motivates checking the whole printing package; it is not a current product comparison.

The machines, suppliers, contracts and office workloads below are original fiction. No real brand is recommended, and no historical price or yield is used in the calculation.

Your classroom decision

A fictional Hong Kong professional-services business needs a printer for a small office. The decision covers one year of necessary black-and-white document printing. Its preliminary plan is 4,000 printed sides during that year. The manager must choose between A, with an HK$800 purchase price and HK$0.30 of compatible consumables per printed side, and B, with an HK$2,000 purchase price and HK$0.10 per side. A printed side is one page of output; a sheet printed on both sides counts as two.

Both meet the same required quality, speed, capacity, security and service standards. Both are affordable. The stated consumable rates are guaranteed in the fictional one-year supply agreement, including replenishment and cleaning/setup consumption, billed on actual printed sides. There are no included free sides, minimum quantities, whole-cartridge purchase rounding, subscription fees or cancellation penalties. Rates do not change with volume during the year. This deliberate metered arrangement makes the arithmetic exact; it is not an estimate from a manufacturer’s cartridge yield.

Paper, electricity, staff time, maintenance and all other costs are equal under the two options. Residual values after the year are equal; no financing, tax or discounting difference is relevant. Neither machine creates extra demand for documents, and unnecessary printing is not a benefit. The manager compares only the costs that differ: machine plus compatible consumables. Nothing has been purchased, and there is no sunk switching or migration cost.

Round 2 · More information

Before ordering, the business confirms that two separate offices each need their own printer. Neither has an existing usable machine, and they cannot share one. The same prices, rates and conditions apply independently to each. There is no bulk discount, pooled page allowance, training benefit or extra cost from selecting different packages.

The first office’s approved document plan now requires 2,000 printed sides for the year. The second office’s plan requires 10,000 sides. These are supplied planning quantities, not a claim that last year’s average predicts future demand. Both packages can comfortably handle either workload. There is enough budget to choose either for each office. No purchase has occurred and the manager can choose A for one and B for the other.

The owner asks for a recommendation by office and a rule for reviewing it if planned output changes before the orders are placed. Do not average the two offices into a single machine or print extra pages to justify a higher entry price. After purchase, a replacement decision would require a fresh comparison of avoidable costs; the rule here is for the initial choice.

Your decision

Which package would you choose for each office now? Find the printed-side quantity at which the two initial-purchase packages cost the same, and explain what a change in expected use does to your recommendation.

Buy the working system, not its cheapest part

The machine and its compatible consumables are complementary inputs: the office needs both to obtain printed output. A cheaper machine can therefore be paired with a more expensive flow of supplies. This does not by itself show deceptive pricing, market power or excess profit. Supplier costs and margins are not given; the terms are explicit.

Start with the preliminary workload

At 4,000 sides, A costs HK$800 + HK$0.30 × 4,000 = HK$2,000. B costs HK$2,000 + HK$0.10 × 4,000 = HK$2,400. A is HK$400 cheaper on the supplied one-year assumptions. B’s lower per-side cost has not yet recovered its HK$1,200 higher machine price.

Different users can prefer different packages

At 2,000 sides, A costs HK$1,400 and B costs HK$2,200: choose A, saving HK$800. At 10,000 sides, A costs HK$3,800 and B costs HK$3,000: choose B, saving HK$800. The mixed choice costs HK$4,400 across the two offices. Buying A for both costs HK$5,200; buying B for both also costs HK$5,200. Equal totals for the uniform choices do not make them as cheap as matching the package to each office.

Find the crossing point, not a universal winner

B costs HK$1,200 more initially but saves HK$0.20 per printed side. HK$1,200 ÷ HK$0.20 = 6,000 sides. At that quantity each costs HK$2,600. Below 6,000 choose A on these assumptions; above 6,000 choose B; exactly at 6,000 they tie. This is a one-year initial-purchase cost threshold, not a break-even profit or a recommendation to increase output.

What linked demand means here

Demand for compatible consumables follows use of the machine. The expected price of those supplies also affects which machine buyers want before purchase. A change in planned printing can therefore change the preferred entry product even when both machine prices stay fixed. Real procurement must also check yield, included supplies, maintenance, quality, useful life and residual value. Our stipulated equal costs and metered rates isolate the complement-pricing mechanism; they do not describe every actual printer.

Instructor note

Let students calculate the 4,000-side comparison before revealing the two-office quantities. Ask why pooling or averaging is inappropriate when each office needs a separate machine and packages can differ without cost. Require the 6,000-side threshold and distinguish initial purchase from replacement after a sunk machine cost. Do not infer demand elasticity, manufacturer profit, predation, lock-in or a causal increase in printing from these two quotes. No probability distribution or future workload guarantee is supplied. If quantities become uncertain, test credible scenarios around the crossing point rather than invent expected probabilities.

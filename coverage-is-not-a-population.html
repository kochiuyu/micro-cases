ACC-17 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Audit selection · Population definition · Evidence limits

Most of the value. Enough of the evidence?

Evidence quality, not just effort

On 29 July 2025, Hong Kong’s AFRC released inspection and investigation reports describing progress alongside recurring audit deficiencies. HKICPA’s training resources separately identify inadequate sampling coverage and unsupported sample sizes as inspection concerns. The distributor and audit-planning exercise below are fictional, not findings about a real firm.

Your classroom decision

An audit team is planning an initial evidence-gathering batch for a fictional Hong Kong distributor. The supplied list contains 20 larger customer balances of HK$40,000 each and 100 smaller balances of HK$2,000 each: HK$1,000,000 in total. The list has been reconciled and checked complete for this exercise. The procedure and assertion stay fixed: test whether the recorded year-end receivables exist, using a designed confirmation procedure with suitable follow-up. Valuation and other audit work are separate.

The next work block has 20 effort units. One larger balance takes one unit and a smaller balance takes half a unit, including the planned follow-up workload. A tests all 20 larger balances. B tests ten specified larger balances and 20 smaller balances selected by the auditor through simple random selection without replacement from the complete smaller-balance list. Every smaller balance has a chance of selection. The ten larger items in B are specifically selected, not a random sample of that group.

This is the sequencing of an initial batch, not a fixed cap on a completed audit. No individually mandatory item is being omitted, and further necessary work will be resourced before an opinion. Neither proposed count is supplied as a professionally adequate sample size. Risk assessment, tolerable misstatement, expected error and final evaluation remain necessary. Choose the initial emphasis: more recorded value now, or evidence from both value groups.

Your decision

Which initial batch would you prioritise, A or B, and why? Calculate its value coverage and identify the balances about which its evidence would remain limited.

Round 2 · More information

Before selection begins, a process walkthrough establishes that all larger balances came through an established manually reviewed billing route. All 100 smaller balances came through a separate automated subscription-billing import introduced this year. The reconciled list already includes both routes; nothing was missing. The new route is not proven faulty, but clean results from the manual route cannot establish that its entries are valid.

The manager says: “If A finds no exceptions, we have checked 80% of the money, so we can call the whole balance clean.” No tests have yet produced results; this is a proposed future conclusion, not evidence. Both initial batches remain feasible at the same effort. B would provide observations from the smaller population, but 20 random selections alone do not establish acceptable sampling risk or a clean remaining population.

The supervisor can revise the sequence and authorise additional testing after the risk and sample-design review. The same receivables-existence objective remains. Do not substitute a general review of IT controls for the specified test or infer a valuation allowance from the mere presence of an import process. Any exceptions need investigation and evaluation before a population conclusion.

Your decision

Would you revise the initial batch after learning about the two billing routes? Correct the manager’s proposed conclusion and specify what further design and evaluation are needed before drawing a population conclusion.

Value coverage describes what was touched

A covers 20 × HK$40,000 = HK$800,000, or 80% of recorded value. B covers ten × HK$40,000 plus 20 × HK$2,000 = HK$440,000, or 44%. Both use 20 effort units. A offers more direct examination of larger balances; B includes smaller-route observations. Coverage is not a confidence level or a percentage of assurance.

The untested group has its own process

A’s clean results, if obtained, would concern the tested larger balances for this procedure. They would not establish existence of the smaller balances generated through another route. The difference motivates reassessing risk and obtaining suitable evidence from that population; it does not prove error or fraud. B is a defensible initial priority after the reveal without being a complete audit plan.

Selection and sufficient evidence are different questions

The smaller items in B are randomly selected from their defined list, so their selection is not confined to convenient files. That is not enough to declare the count adequate. The team must justify sample design and size, perform the procedure and appropriate follow-up, investigate exceptions, evaluate sampling risk and determine whether the results support the intended conclusion. No numerical error projection is possible because no test results are supplied.

Keep the populations visible

Evaluate the smaller-population sample in relation to that population. Do not project specifically selected larger-item results onto untested larger balances or combine unlike selections into one clean-rate calculation. The final response may include additional or different testing. The initial work block cannot override the need for sufficient appropriate evidence or justify signing prematurely.

Instructor note

First compare effort and value coverage; then reveal the different billing routes. Ask exactly what a clean result would support. Accept A as an initial high-value sequencing choice if the student explicitly reserves the remainder for necessary work; after the reveal require a credible response to the separate population. B is not automatically adequate because its selection is random. This is population and selection logic, not an audit sample-size formula, actual opinion or professional advice.

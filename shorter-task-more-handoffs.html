OPS-39 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Service process design · Touch time and lead time · Handoffs

A shorter task. More handoffs.

Follow the order, not only the task

Process-mapping guidance separates work on an order from the time it waits between activities. Research on business-process logs also distinguishes several causes of waiting; a time gap alone does not identify the cause. The fictional print-service comparison below follows the same order boundary through two feasible arrangements.

Your classroom decision

You manage fictional Harbour Print’s order-checking service in Hong Kong. Before a standard print order can reach production, staff verify the supplied file and specification, check the standard quotation, and confirm the customer’s recorded approval. These are required controls, not optional paperwork. Production itself is outside this comparison.

Three existing paid employees are trained and authorised for all three checks on standard orders. Route S assigns each check to a specialist desk and transfers the order twice. Route C assigns the complete standard order to one coordinator, with the three employees handling different orders. Staffing, pay, hours, workload and release policy stay unchanged.

A timed work study reports six minutes for S’s three checks combined, versus ten minutes for C’s checks. The S figure excludes transferring and re-entering order details. Management wants a monitored standard-order trial that shortens receipt-to-production-ready time without weakening accuracy, required approvals or staff conditions. It has not promised customers a new turnaround time.

Both routes are affordable and already supported by existing systems and training. Required records remain available and auditable. These standard orders need no separate independent sign-off; complex orders and approval exceptions retain their existing escalation route. No worker loses a job or rest break in this decision.

Round 2 · More information

A controlled diagnostic replay uses the same complete standard-order information under each route. The clock starts at receipt, 09:00, and ends when all checks and the production-ready record are complete. Both routes wait five minutes before first work. The listed intervals are sequential, complete and non-overlapping; no unrecorded work or customer-response delay occurs.

S checks the specification from 09:05 to 09:07, then waits until 09:27. Desk two re-enters details from 09:27 to 09:28 and checks the quotation until 09:30. The order then waits until 09:45. Desk three re-enters details until 09:46 and confirms approval until 09:48.

C’s coordinator performs all required checks and completes the record continuously from 09:05 to 09:15. Its ten minutes include all information handling. S’s two extra entry minutes copy unchanged identifiers into separate desk screens; they add no verification. C uses the existing shared record without removing any required evidence.

Observation confirms that S’s two transfer queues arise while the next desk serves previously accepted work. C’s coordinator has a continuous work slot in this replay. Both outputs pass the same audit. This diagnostic is not a representative sample or a sustained-capacity test; it does not establish future average waiting, error rates or throughput.

The operating target for the proposed trial is production-ready within thirty minutes of receipt, with all controls intact. Management can trial either route under the unchanged workload and observe results before making a service promise. No new software, extra staff, priority jumping or hidden queue is available.

Your decision

Calculate total touch time, waiting time and receipt-to-ready time for each replay. Which route would you trial, and what would you measure before claiming a sustained improvement? Explain why removing duplicate entry is not permission to remove verification.

Debrief · Reconcile every minute

S uses six minutes of required checks plus two minutes of duplicate entry: eight touch minutes. Waiting is 5 + 20 + 15 = 40 minutes. Total receipt-to-ready time is 8 + 40 = 48 minutes, consistent with the 09:48 finish.

C uses ten touch minutes and five waiting minutes: fifteen minutes overall, finishing at 09:15. In this replay C takes two more active minutes but finishes thirty-three minutes earlier. Both have the same five-minute initial wait; the difference comes from thirty-five extra transfer-wait minutes offset by S’s two-minute touch advantage.

Debrief · Choose a trial, not a universal claim

Trial C for standard orders, preserving the stated controls and escalation route. It meets the thirty-minute target in this diagnostic; S misses it by eighteen minutes. The evidence supports testing an end-to-end arrangement, not advertising a fifteen-minute guarantee or assuming every future coordinator will have an uninterrupted slot.

The six-versus-ten comparison omitted duplicate entry. Even the complete eight-versus-ten touch comparison does not rank customer lead time correctly here. A shorter task is useful only within the whole process; the customer cannot use an order that is still waiting between desks.

Debrief · Keep work and waiting distinct

S’s waiting is elapsed time for this order, not forty minutes of paid staff doing nothing. Other orders occupy the receiving desks. Do not multiply this order’s time saving by wages and call it cash saved, or divide a working day by fifteen minutes and call that sustainable output.

The two entry minutes are active work, even though they add no new check. A ratio of touch time to lead time would be 8/48 for S and 10/15 for C. That ratio is not staff utilisation, proof of value creation, or a quality score.

Debrief · Preserve the control purpose

Remove redundant copying only after confirming which fields and controls it serves. The case establishes that the shared record preserves the required evidence. In another process, a second person’s independent check may be essential; calling it a handoff does not make it waste.

Keep exceptions visible and route them to the existing authorised reviewer. Do not quietly extend C to work outside staff competence or remove customer approval. A coordinator owns the standard order’s progress, not unlimited authority to change commercial terms or bypass controls.

Debrief · Test the whole service

During the trial, retain receipt timestamps and record starts, finishes, transfer waits, rework, missed targets and good completions for all eligible orders, including unfinished ones. Watch other queues, workload balance and staff experience. Do not make the trial look faster by excluding difficult orders or moving their receipt time.

Compare like workloads and order types over enough operating periods to see variability. If C creates long coordinator queues or quality problems, revise the design rather than defend the label. A shared record could also improve S; that alternative needs its own tested workflow, not an assumed zero-wait future.

Instructor note

Have students draw S as 5 wait, 2 check, 20 wait, 1 copy, 2 check, 15 wait, 1 copy, 2 check. Draw C as 5 wait and 10 work. Require both timelines to reconcile to their finish times before discussing redesign.

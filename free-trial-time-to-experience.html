MKT-33 · Singapore · 15–20 minutes · Trial design · Benefit-realisation cycle · Experience versus access

A free trial. Enough time to experience the benefit?

Access is not yet experience

Free-trial research examines learning through use, not merely receiving access. Results from software studies do not establish one universally best duration. A trial should give customers a fair opportunity to evaluate the relevant experience while making its limits and any later purchase clear. The language service below is fictional.

Your classroom decision

You manage fictional Singapore language-practice service Clear Phrase. Choose a trial for the same 100 consenting adult newcomers. The objective is an informed assessment of the practice-and-feedback cycle, not maximum registrations or pressured subscriptions. No trial has been advertised or accepted.

The sample contains three 20-minute sessions: attempt a short dialogue, practise with feedback, then retry and review the comparison. Sessions must occur on separate available days. Completing all three exposes the complete sample cycle; it does not establish fluency, a learning gain or satisfaction. Setup screens and account logins are not sessions.

A offers up to three sessions, expiring at the end of calendar day 7 after signup. B ends after the third completed session, including its feedback, or at the end of day 21, whichever comes first. Signup is day 1. Under B, unused sessions can expire at the cap; it is not unlimited access.

Both use identical sessions, support, optional reminders and accessibility arrangements. A complete fixed S$2,000 quote funds either design for these 100 people, including setup, delivery and support through day 21 and read-only feedback access through day 28. Capacity covers three sessions each. Longer access creates no unpriced support bill in this bounded comparison.

No card or payment is required. Both trials stop without billing or automatic renewal. Any later paid plan requires a separate informed opt-in at the same clearly displayed price and terms. Customers may leave at any time. The full timetable, session limit and expiry rule appear prominently before signup.

The manager favours A because a seven-day message is simple and reaches a decision sooner. The onboarding team has not yet supplied newcomers’ setup and availability checks. Before launch you can inspect them. Do not assume all 100 begin practising on signup day or that a longer window guarantees attendance.

Which trial would you provisionally choose, and what timing evidence would you request before calling seven days enough? Distinguish account access from an opportunity to experience the complete three-session cycle.

Round 2 · More information

A pre-launch walkthrough records these 100 newcomers’ setup requirements and stated availability. Forty can attend on days 2, 4 and 6; forty on days 6, 8 and 10; twenty on days 15, 17 and 19. These are their only feasible session dates through day 21; both designs can supply every listed slot.

Later starts reflect account setup and users’ own schedules, not a difference in teaching or support quality. The supplied timetable is a feasibility scenario, not observed attendance or a randomised test. For counting opportunities, assume each person attends every feasible session that remains within the offer; actual people may skip or leave.

Do not accelerate sessions into setup time or invent extra availability. Both designs keep the same three-session content and fixed quote. Finished-session feedback remains available read-only until day 28 under either design. No payment, advertising-effect, satisfaction or learning-outcome data have been collected.

Your decision

Which design better supports evaluation under this timetable? Count people who could finish all three sessions under each, explain the remaining uncertainty, and propose measures that distinguish opportunity, actual experience and a voluntary purchase.

Debrief · Start the clock at the right event

B is a sensible provisional preference if setup consumes much of the first week, but the opening alone does not establish that it does. Request setup completion, first feasible practice date, later availability and the full trial-cycle requirements. Seven calendar days of access are not seven days of meaningful use.

The important milestone is experiencing the complete sample cycle, not merely installing the service or opening a lesson. Three sessions are this product’s supplied demonstration structure, not a universal threshold for language learning or a claim that everyone must buy after the third session.

Debrief · Trace A’s opportunities

Under A, the first 40 can attend on days 2, 4 and 6 and finish the cycle before day 7 ends. The next 40 can attend only day 6; days 8 and 10 fall outside the trial. The final 20 cannot reach even their first feasible session before expiry.

Thus A permits 40 complete cycles out of 100, or 40%, in the attendance-if-feasible scenario. Available session opportunities total 40 × 3 + 40 × 1 + 20 × 0 = 160. Sixty people lack enough time to sample the entire cycle, even if motivated to attend every feasible slot.

Debrief · Trace B’s opportunities

Under B, the three groups can finish on days 6, 10 and 19, all before the day-21 cap. The scenario permits 100 complete cycles, or 100%, and 300 session opportunities. Compared with A, that is 60 more people with a full-cycle opportunity and 140 more usable session slots.

These are conditional opportunity counts, not 100 completed trials or 60 extra customers. B also ends earlier than day 21 for someone completing the third session. Its usage limit and calendar cap work together: absent or very late users still may not finish. Do not advertise three guaranteed completed sessions.

Debrief · Choose an evaluation opportunity

Choose B for this cohort and stated objective: it removes A’s timing barrier without changing content, quoted cost or payment protections. The first 40 gain no extra required sessions; the other 60 gain room to evaluate. B does not establish better teaching, higher conversion or greater profit.

A remains simpler and could suit a different cohort able to complete the cycle in its first week. Shorter trials may prompt earlier decisions; longer trials can delay them or substitute for paid use. Neither effect is quantified here. Evidence about other software products does not settle this service’s sales outcome.

Debrief · Measure the stages separately

Track signup and setup completion, session dates, full-cycle completion and voluntary dropouts separately. Ask those completing the cycle whether they could assess the feedback and whether it suits their needs, allowing a negative answer. A completed third session is not proof of benefit or willingness to pay.

Track later paid opt-ins, refunds and continued use separately, with informed measurement consent. Use a common observation horizon, such as day 28 after signup, when comparing cohorts; do not compare A’s day-7 purchases with B’s day-21 purchases as if exposure time were equal. Record actual support cost too.

To estimate the causal effect of design, use a prospectively planned, consented comparison with common eligibility, content and outcome definitions, sufficient follow-up and appropriate analysis. This timetable calculation is not that experiment. Do not call purchases among completers the conversion rate for all signups or interpret noncompletion as lack of interest alone.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–4: choose provisionally and identify the missing clock. Minutes 4–9: draw the three availability timelines and both expiry rules. Minutes 9–14: reconcile 160 versus 300 session opportunities and 40 versus 100 complete-cycle opportunities. Minutes 14–18: design a stage-specific scorecard and an honest trial description.

MKT-28 separates confidence about setup from physical fit before purchase. This case concerns time to sample actual service delivery. MKT-06 separates contacts from qualified leads; here participants are already enrolled prospects and expiry limits evaluation. Neither a login nor a completed sample is a demonstrated learning gain or sale.

  1. Takeaki Sunada · Customer Learning and Revenue-Maximizing Trial Design · 2 October 2018 working paper. Original SaaS field-experiment analysis compares seven-, fourteen- and thirty-day trials and finds the shortest best among uniform policies in its setting. Its card-linked automatic conversion differs from this case’s separate opt-in; findings are not language-learning evidence.

PPL-41 · Hong Kong · 15 minutes · Work design · Qualified relief · Procedural fairness · Safety voice

One warning. Two workplaces.

A territory-wide alert is not a site assessment

In an October 2026 Legislative Council reply, Hong Kong’s Government explained that the territory-wide Heat Stress at Work Warning does not replace assessment of individual workplaces. Employers must respond to local conditions, and can seek occupational-health expertise where the warning does not adequately reflect the exposure. The reply also describes additional warning triggers introduced in August 2026. The contractor below is fictional.

Your classroom decision

You manage a fictional Hong Kong building-maintenance contractor. Next week it must complete rooftop and sheltered-ground tasks. A competent occupational-health assessment has specified the controls, paid rest arrangements and conditions for stopping or reassessing each task. Neither staffing package below can relax those requirements. Both have been reviewed as feasible only while those assessed conditions remain valid. In this contractor’s procedure, anyone can stop work on their task and flag a changed condition. The designated supervisor can approve a restart only after competent reassessment.

Choose a staffing package before work starts. Package A transfers two qualified colleagues from a sheltered team to provide rooftop relief. Package B buys a temporary qualified relief team from an approved provider. Training, supervision, employment protections and agreed pay are included. Nobody loses wages for taking required rest or reporting a changed condition. No employee health information is supplied or requested.

All figures in the exhibit are hypothetical planning estimates for the same week, after the required controls and paid rest. A capacity unit is one standard completed task, not an hour of safe exposure. Rooftop tasks require the stated qualification; sheltered tasks cannot be substituted. Normal baseline staffing already provides 28 rooftop units and 44 sheltered units. The client needs 32 rooftop and 40 sheltered units. The additional staffing budget is HK$6,000.

Your decision

Which staffing package would you book, and why? Compare cost and spare capacity, then explain how you would allocate relief fairly without treating identical rest arrangements as the definition of fairness.

Round 2 · More information

Before booking, the client confirms that an access change moves four sheltered tasks to a later week but brings four qualified rooftop tasks into this week. Demand is now 36 rooftop units and 36 sheltered units. Capacity and prices in the exhibit are unchanged. This is a scheduling change, not evidence that rooftop work has become safer.

A supervisor proposes keeping Package A and asking colleagues to shorten the assessed rest arrangements to make up the two missing rooftop units. Another proposes buying Package B. You may instead negotiate a revised completion date; no penalty price for delay is supplied. Any actual deterioration in conditions still triggers the assessed stop-and-review procedure, regardless of the package purchased.

Your decision

What would you authorise now: A, B, or a negotiated delay? State the capacity shortfall, the extra cost of B relative to A, and who may stop work when local conditions change. What would you tell the sheltered team about the different relief arrangements?

Debrief · Capacity is not permission to expose people

Initially both packages meet demand within budget. A leaves two rooftop units and no sheltered units spare; B leaves six rooftop and four sheltered units spare. B costs HK$2,400 more. A is defensible if the lower cost and smaller buffer suit the work; B is defensible if the additional flexibility is worth that price. The exhibit does not assign a monetary value to illness or prove that either package prevents it.

Debrief · Qualifications limit reassignment

After the change, A is two rooftop units short even though it has four sheltered units spare. Adding the two capacities hides the skill constraint. B provides two spare rooftop units and eight spare sheltered units, within the HK$6,000 budget. B is a feasible on-time choice under the stated conditions. A with a negotiated delay may also be defensible, but its commercial consequences have not been priced. Shortening the assessed rest arrangements is not an available capacity solution.

Debrief · Equal respect need not mean identical arrangements

Explain the task-based assessment and the relief allocation before work begins. Different exposures and duties can require different controls; sheltered colleagues should not inherit an unmanageable backlog. Publish qualified-cover responsibilities and consult both teams on the rotation, while protecting individual confidentiality. Required breaks and good-faith safety reports must not reduce the stipulated pay or become evidence of poor commitment.

Debrief · Give the reporting route an operational response

The designated supervisor follows the assessed stop-and-review procedure when conditions change; reporting does not depend on winning a budget argument. Name a backup decision-maker, an escalation route and the person responsible for obtaining competent reassessment before work resumes where required. Track whether planned relief was actually delivered, uncovered qualified work and response time to concerns. Do not use zero reports as proof of safety or compare raw output without accounting for task conditions.

Instructor note

Allow 15 minutes: three to read, four for the first decision, four for the demand change and four to design the supervisor and relief arrangements. Ask why four spare sheltered units do not solve two missing rooftop units, and why the territory-wide warning cannot certify one roof.

This is a staffing and fairness exercise, not a heat-risk assessment, legal opinion or medical protocol. Real controls and work-rest arrangements require current guidance and competent assessment; no safe temperature or universal break duration is invented here. The fictional packages assume assessed conditions remain valid and must be reconsidered if they do not.

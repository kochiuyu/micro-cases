Learn from one experiment. Keep the learning comparable?

An experiment should answer a stated question

Entrepreneurship research studies how explicit hypotheses and decision rules change venture learning. Experimental-design guidance also distinguishes choosing among offers from identifying individual effects. This fictional venture must decide which kind of evidence is worth its time.

Your classroom decision

Two founders in Hong Kong sell editable price-sign template packs to small retailers. The packs contain ordinary shop signs, not regulated labels. Two earlier paid pilots produced different take-up rates, and the team is considering a lower price plus assisted setup. The immediate purpose is to decide what merits one further funded pilot, not to launch at scale or claim a profitable business.

They have HK$20,000 for one learning cycle, including recruitment, paid delivery work and evaluation. A tests the redesigned package against the original offer concurrently and produces an overall package decision in two weeks. B first compares prices with delivery held fixed, then, only if its rule permits, compares delivery at the selected price. B takes up to four weeks and needs up to twice as many new prospects. Both fit the funded allowance and service capacity. The founders can afford the extra time, but it delays an overall package decision by up to two weeks.

For either plan, each comparison recruits forty new eligible single-shop owners from the same recruitment channel, randomly assigning twenty to each truthful offer during the same period. One owner represents each independent business and sees one offer; there are no repeat participants or shared purchasing decisions. Eligibility, pack content and the seven-day purchase window are fixed. All offered orders can be fulfilled on promised terms. Consent and ordinary commercial protections are met; no deceptive purchase buttons or unpaid labour are involved. Detailed historical conditions and proposed rules are about to be reviewed.

Your decision

There is also a service gate: all orders in a version must meet its promised delivery terms before that version can continue. A version failing this gate cannot be selected under the count rule. If N qualifies on counts but fails service, E can continue only if E meets both its own eight-order minimum and its service gate; otherwise stop. Counts retain every assigned prospect, including non-buyers. Record realised delivery cost separately; affordability and sustainable margin still require later evaluation. No interim stopping for a good-looking score is allowed. Your decision

Which design would you fund now, and what would count as a stop or a change? Apply the written rules to an illustrative valid comparison with E = 6 and N = 9 paid orders, then explain what this result would and would not establish.

Answer the first question: package or component?

The old pilots show 5/20 = 25% and 10/20 = 50% take-up, a 25-percentage-point difference. Price, buyer group, delivery and period all changed, so no isolated price effect follows. A can compare the two complete offers for the common next target, even though two features differ; that is not a flawed design if the question is which package attracts orders. B sacrifices speed to distinguish price at self-service and then delivery at the selected price. It does not fully estimate every interaction between price and delivery.

A defensible choice for this venture

Choose B if the founders need to know what to retain or revise before spending again and can use the four-week allowance. It tests the hypothesis that lowering price improves take-up under the current delivery method, then whether assistance adds enough response at the selected price. Choose A if a quick, bounded decision about the whole package is more valuable than component attribution. State that limitation before starting; do not later describe a package win as proof that a lower price caused it.

Answer the second question: use the rule before the story

If the illustrative comparison follows the design and both versions pass the service gate, N = 9 meets the eight-order minimum and exceeds E = 6 by three, so continue N to a further pilot. That is 45% versus 30%, a 15-percentage-point observed difference. It passes the team’s screening rule, not a statistical significance test or proof of future profitability. Under A it supports the package; under B stage one it supports further investigation of the lower price with self-service. Stage-two effects would apply at that selected price only.

Let a negative result change the next action

If N records seven and E six, stop that offer path: neither minimum is met, despite N looking better. If N records nine and E eight, keep E because N’s one-order advantage misses the three-order rule and E meets its minimum, provided E passes service. Failure of service blocks continuation of that version regardless of demand. Record why the theory or offer changes, then specify a fresh test using new observations. Unexpected feedback can generate a hypothesis, but it should not be relabelled as a predeclared confirmation.

Instructor note

These small comparisons are decision screens, not powered demonstrations of a universal market effect. The thresholds are fictional management choices for authorising further learning; they are not supplied by the cited studies. Random assignment and common conditions improve interpretability but do not remove sampling uncertainty or establish market-wide demand. Do not insist that changing multiple features is always unscientific: a bundle can be the treatment of interest. B’s conditional second stage adds time and limits generalisation across prices. This case concerns identification and predeclared revision, not selecting the experimental unit or mapping which assumption to test first.

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All venture details, offers, budgets, numbers and thresholds are original fiction. No actual purchases or experiments are requested. Past rates are 25% and 50%, a 25-percentage-point difference; price, buyer group, delivery and period changed together. Next comparisons randomly allocate forty new independent single-shop owners concurrently, twenty per offer, with full denominators and no repeated participants. A tests a package; B tests price under self-service then delivery at the selected price, not all interactions. Continue N only with at least eight orders and at least three more than E, plus service fulfilment. Otherwise E needs eight orders and service fulfilment; otherwise stop. E = 6 and N = 9 implies 30% versus 45%, a 15-point difference, meeting the count rule but not establishing statistical proof or profit. The scientific-entrepreneurship replication includes the original trial; they are not independent samples.

  1. Camuffo, Cordova, Gambardella and Spina: A Scientific Approach to Entrepreneurial Decision Making. Full original article describes hypothesis formulation, explicit decision thresholds and tests with comparable alternatives; published online in 2019.
  2. Camuffo and colleagues, 2024: Large-scale replication and extension. Four-trial study examines termination, focused revision and performance; its sample includes the original trial, so it is not wholly independent evidence.
  3. Koning, Hasan and Chatterji, 2022: Experimentation and Start-up Performance. Original research examines A/B testing as part of broader learning strategies, including substantial product changes; not a guaranteed return from this small pilot.
  4. NIST/SEMATECH handbook: What are the objectives?. Authoritative design guidance distinguishes comparing alternatives from screening individual factors and recommends writing objectives first.
  5. Center for Open Science: Preregistration. Explains advance planning, transparent deviations and the distinction between exploratory and confirmatory work. The fictional founders use internal predeclared rules, not a claimed public registration.

ENT-29 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Consensual corporate spinouts · Organisational boundaries · Transition dependencies

An internal idea. An independent venture?

Independence still needs interfaces

Research on corporate spinouts examines the tension between freedom to pursue new markets and access to a parent’s capabilities. Public transition-service agreements also show that separation can involve defined, temporary support. These sources frame the fictional discussion below; they do not prescribe a legal structure or predict commercial success.

Your classroom decision

Two employees of a Hong Kong commercial-signage firm have developed a booking tool for community workshops. The employer owns the prototype, made during paid work. The tool is noncore: the firm sells signs, not subscription software. Employer and employees are jointly considering how to validate the idea over the next six months. Nobody has resigned or formed a company, and no agreement will be signed in this classroom decision.

A keeps the project inside the employer, with its existing commercial approval process and access to technical staff. B pursues a consensual independent venture led by the two employees, with authority over its own customer and product decisions. For this case, assume both sides accept in principle the same six-month funded validation envelope, a voluntary staff transition and permission to use specified prototype code. These are scenario conditions, not completed legal arrangements. B is a venture offering a service to customers, not a choice to sell licences to the employer.

Under B, the employer offers a named technical specialist for twelve paid support hours a month for six months, restricted to maintaining the prototype and transferring operating knowledge. No extension or free emergency coverage is promised. Code-use permission excludes the employer’s customer records and unrelated systems; new customer relationships need their own arrangements. Both routes must validate customer demand and a sustainable operating model before any scale-up. The task is to recommend which route to develop, not approve an investment, employment change or contract.

A: internal ownership, normal commercial approvals, continuing access to established technical capability. B: jointly agreed route toward a separate venture, independent customer/product decisions, specified code permission, twelve support hours monthly for six months. Both have the same funded six-month validation envelope. B must replace temporary support; neither route has validated demand or long-run costs.

Your decision

Would you develop A or B with the employees? Explain what decision freedom is worth and which inherited capability must not disappear when the boundary changes.

Round 2 · More information

Interviews reveal that workshop organisers need small changes to booking rules and prices between weekly events. The employer’s normal commercial committee meets quarterly and prioritises signage accounts; it has not agreed an exception for this project. The mismatch is with the approval system, not evidence that managers or employees are incapable. A could be redesigned with delegated authority, but that change is not yet secured. Demand remains exploratory rather than contracted revenue.

The prototype still depends on the employer’s specialist for deployment and recovery procedures. The agreed twelve hours monthly are sufficient for the planned small validation pilot, including knowledge transfer, but not for an unrestricted public launch. An independent provider has supplied a preliminary transition plan: document and practise the procedures during months one to three, then seek acceptance of the replacement capability at the end of month four. If accepted, months five and six allow two months of overlap. Preparation and overlap fit the fictional six-month budget; ongoing prices after month six remain unverified.

Acceptance requires the replacement operator to demonstrate deployment and recovery on a test system without the employer doing the work. The employees will own the checklist and evidence; the employer’s specialist will witness the tests within the support allowance. If readiness fails, the parties must reduce or pause the pilot before support expires, or explicitly negotiate and fund a different plan. B cannot assume indefinite rescue. These facts support a conditional route choice, not immediate separation or a conclusion that future revenues cover independent operating costs.

Your decision

Would you keep or change your recommendation? Specify the decision authority, temporary support boundary and exit test needed before independence could be responsibly pursued.

Answer the first decision: separate freedom from self-sufficiency

A retains capability and reduces transition work, but its processes may privilege the established business. B can let the team address a different customer system, yet incorporation alone does not create technical competence or viable economics. I would initially explore B only with an explicit dependency map. Identify code rights, operating knowledge, decision authority, service scope and customer responsibilities rather than treating employee enthusiasm as sufficient evidence.

Answer the revealed decision: conditional B

I favour developing B because weekly customer adaptation conflicts with the unchanged quarterly approval process. Give the proposed venture genuine authority over its customer and product decisions within its funded scope. Make this recommendation conditional on confirmed permissions and a workable capability transition before separation or customer commitments. A with genuinely delegated authority remains a defensible fallback. Do not mistake the interviews for paying demand or infer that independence causes success.

Make support bounded and replaceable

Specify the named specialist, twelve hours monthly, maintenance and knowledge-transfer scope, six-month end date and escalation for requests outside scope. The total allowance is 72 hours, not unlimited support. Document procedures during months one to three; test independent deployment and recovery at the end of month four. Passing then leaves months five and six for overlap. Log readiness failures and reduce or pause the pilot before expiry if replacement is not feasible. Any extension requires a fresh, funded agreement, not an assumption.

Keep the commercial and rights gates separate

Operational readiness is necessary but insufficient. Obtain a credible post-support cost estimate and compare it with evidence of customer willingness to pay before a continuing business commitment. Scoped permission to use code is not ownership of every employer asset or permission to export customer data. In a real venture, appropriately qualified advisers would resolve employment, intellectual-property, customer and funding arrangements. This case makes no jurisdiction-specific claim and executes none of them.

Instructor note

Ask students to name a capability that survives the boundary as a managed dependency and an authority that must change. Contrast a separate legal venture with an internally differentiated unit: B crosses an ownership/organisational boundary, whereas a separate internal team remains under the employer. Sources include a historical corporate study, a real transition agreement and institutional guidance. University examples are analogies, not corporate laws; none validates the invented timetable or a causal performance advantage.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

The firm, tool, interviews, budget feasibility, support and transition plan are entirely fictional. The recommendation concerns which route to develop, not signing or submitting a legal agreement, making an investment or terminating employment. Twelve hours multiplied by six months equals 72 support hours. Acceptance at the end of month four leaves months five and six, two months, for overlap; no earlier acceptance is assumed. The twelve-hour allowance includes knowledge transfer and witness testing and is sufficient only for the stipulated small pilot. Same funded six-month envelope does not establish equivalent long-run cost or commercial viability. Ongoing independent prices and demand remain unverified. A delegated internal alternative is possible but unapproved. The two WIPO guidance pages overlap and are not independent empirical tests; academic spinout guidance is contextual, not Hong Kong corporate advice. The Xerox study is observational and the SEC agreement illustrates bounded support, not recommended clauses.

  1. Chesbrough: The Governance and Performance of Xerox Technology Spin-off Companies, 2003. Original observational study of corporate spinouts; examines parent governance, search freedom and complementary capabilities. One-company evidence does not establish a universal causal advantage.
  2. 3M and Solventum: Transition Services Agreement, 31 March 2024. Primary public agreement illustrates scoped transitional services, time limits, migration and consent requirements. Not a contract template or claim about the fictional venture.
  3. WIPO: IP Assignment and Licensing. Primary institutional explanation distinguishes transferring ownership from permission to use intellectual property. Supports identifying the relevant rights rather than assuming all assets transfer.
  4. Independent Review of University Spin-out Companies, 2023. Original government-commissioned review discusses commercial capabilities and access to support and infrastructure. Academic setting is an analogy, not evidence about this corporate venture.
  5. WIPO: Technology Transfer Agreements. Primary guidance discusses specified assets, access rights, knowledge transfer and obligations. Emphasises adapting arrangements to circumstances; not jurisdiction-specific legal advice.

ACC-32 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Normal and abnormal loss · Process costing · Inventory cost

Rejects. Which loss belongs in stock?

A reject count is not a cost classification

Production rejects do not all receive the same cost treatment. Professional process-costing guidance distinguishes expected process loss from excess loss; inventory standards exclude abnormal production waste from inventory cost.

Harbour Clips, its production records and every amount below are fictional. The exercise supplies a simplified costing policy, not a technical reject benchmark or a determination of any real company’s reporting obligations.

Your classroom decision

You are controller of fictional Harbour Clips, a Hong Kong maker of identical plastic stationery clips. One run has finished. All 1,000 units received the full production process before final inspection: 900 are good and unsold; 100 are valueless rejects. Nothing is missing or unfinished.

The run cost HK$19,000. This is the complete properly measured production cost pool, including all necessary disposal costs, with no separate abnormal disposal charge. There are no scrap receipts, rework, opening inventory, work in progress or unallocated overheads. The good units’ net realisable value exceeds their cost.

The draft report assigns all HK$19,000 to the 900 good units because they are the only saleable output. You have time to obtain the approved normal-loss allowance and inspection report before signing. Neither document has yet reached you; do not invent a loss percentage.

Use this classroom policy: normal loss is the expected loss under usual conditions. Its cost is absorbed in the process rate. Abnormal loss is the excess over that allowance and is expensed now. With all units fully processed and no scrap value, divide total process cost by input units minus normal-loss units; apply that rate to good output and abnormal loss.

Round 2 · More information

The approved allowance, supported by normal operating records, is 5% of input units. Inspection confirms 50 normal rejects and another 50 attributable to a one-off equipment-setting incident. For this exercise those additional rejects are abnormal. All rejects were identified only after receiving the full process.

The allowance remains appropriate for this run; do not reset it to the observed 10% to absorb the incident. Costs, physical quantities and the absence of scrap value remain unchanged. No further cash payment is created by correcting the allocation.

Your decision

What inventory amount and abnormal-loss expense would you now sign? Show normal loss, expected output, the cost rate and a reconciliation to HK$19,000.

Debrief · A count is not a classification

Obtain the missing evidence before signing. The count establishes 900 good units and 100 rejects, not how much loss is normal. Neither capitalising every cost nor expensing all rejects is justified merely by that count. The supplied policy makes the normal allowance consequential.

Debrief · Find the denominator

Normal loss is 1,000 × 5% = 50 units. Expected good output is 1,000 − 50 = 950 units. Actual good output is 900, so abnormal loss is 950 − 900 = 50 units. The cost rate is HK$19,000 ÷ 950 = HK$20.

Expected output is a costing denominator, not a claim that 950 good clips physically exist. The 50 abnormal rejects receive the same full-process rate as good output because both received all production work. Partial losses or unfinished units would require additional analysis.

Debrief · Reconcile the report

Finished inventory is 900 × HK$20 = HK$18,000. Abnormal-loss expense is 50 × HK$20 = HK$1,000. Costs reconcile: HK$18,000 + HK$1,000 = HK$19,000. Units reconcile separately: 900 good + 50 normal rejects + 50 abnormal rejects = 1,000.

The normal rejects carry no separate inventory balance or loss expense here; their production cost has not disappeared. It is absorbed through the expected-output rate. The abnormal allocation is removed from inventory rather than being spread over saleable clips.

Debrief · Avoid three tempting shortcuts

Dividing HK$19,000 by 900 and capitalising everything hides the HK$1,000 abnormal loss in inventory. Dividing by 1,000 gives HK$19 but fails to absorb normal loss correctly. Expensing all 100 rejects at that rate gives HK$1,900, wrongly treating normal and abnormal loss alike.

Relative to the draft, inventory and this period’s pre-tax profit each fall HK$1,000, all else equal. This is not a cash saving or a new payment. Nor does a correct calculation prove the allowance or incident classification: those require supporting operational evidence.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–4: vote before revealing the allowance. Ask what information is missing. Minutes 4–9: reveal the report and calculate in pairs. Minutes 9–14: reconcile quantities and costs separately. Minutes 14–18: compare the three shortcut denominators and ask what evidence supports normal operating conditions.

Keep the boundary clear: ACC-31 measures unfinished work; this case has fully processed output and separates normal from abnormal loss. ACC-04 concerns stock location and net realisable value; neither is disputed here. ACC-21 concerns sell-or-process-further choices, not loss classification. Do not infer worker misconduct from an incident label.

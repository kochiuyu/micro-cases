PPL-48 · Hong Kong · 15 minutes · Team role design · Human verification · Protected review time

The AI drafted it. Who has time to check it?

Human review still takes work

Hong Kong’s Privacy Commissioner recommends that organisations make employee responsibilities as human reviewers explicit when they use generative AI. The fictional store group below is drafting practice exercises from a supplied public product guide. The issue is how to assign and budget checking work, not choosing a model or uploading records.

Your classroom decision

A Hong Kong stationery group wants twelve short practice cards to help new colleagues identify ordinary display products. An approved tool drafts from a public reference guide, with no personnel, customer or sales data. The cards are fictional training material, not customer messages, safety instructions, ratings, or evidence about employee performance.

In M, the trainer checks every card against the guide. In P, a qualified peer who did not draft the card checks it against the guide and records the evidence; the trainer gives a final scope-and-evidence check. Neither design publishes an unchecked card. Unresolved items wait; people do not make up review time after work.

Paid capacity available this week is 120 trainer minutes and 160 peer minutes. The times in the exhibit are exercise estimates for routine cards. The two allocations are separate: spare peer time does not qualify someone to take over the trainer’s role.

How many routine cards can each plan clear this week? Which design would you provisionally choose, and what kind of uncertainty should a peer escalate rather than score as correct?

Round 2 · More information

A rehearsal finds two of the twelve drafts describe a product pair whose distinction is documented only in a specialist appendix. Both were detected during the ordinary peer check. For each of these cards, an extra twelve peer minutes would suffice with training the peers do not yet have, or the already-qualified trainer can spend an extra twenty minutes on an authoritative check.

All cards still need the baseline check and sign-off under P, including the two escalations. The other ten are unchanged. The manager can reserve some trainer minutes now or defer any card that cannot be resolved. The appendix is already available and authorised for this internal check; nothing new goes into the tool.

Your decision

Design a feasible review plan for the twelve cards using current qualifications. Show the time required in each role, and explain what the team should record before using the cards in training.

Debrief · Drafts are not reviewed output

M can clear floor(120/18) = 6 routine cards, leaving twelve trainer minutes. P needs 12 × 12 = 144 peer minutes and 12 × 3 = 36 trainer minutes for all twelve routine cards, both within capacity. That does not establish quality or a permanent workload saving.

Debrief · Escalation takes capacity too

Under P, sending two extras to peers would require 144 + 2 × 12 = 168 peer minutes, over the 160-minute allocation, and peers are not yet qualified for that check. Escalate them to the trainer instead: peer time stays 144 and trainer time becomes 36 + 2 × 20 = 76. Sixteen peer minutes and forty-four trainer minutes remain. Defer an unresolved card rather than treating this calculation as approval.

Debrief · Build expertise, not a rubber stamp

Use a named checker per card, a guide citation and version, the issue escalated and evidence of resolution. A second person can audit a small rotating sample against the source and teach the rationale for tricky pairs during paid time. Compare missed errors and total review minutes before changing the workflow; avoid ranking staff by flagged drafts.

Instructor note

Assign separate budget coins to each role and ask whether exchanging them would change what the team is qualified to check. Contrast tool adoption, training design and verification capacity. Nothing here authorises use of personal data or replacement of expert checks.

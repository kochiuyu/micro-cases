FIN-24 · Hong Kong · fictional business · 15–20 minutes · Insurance deductibles · Loss frequency · Retained risk

A lower premium. How much risk stays here?

A deductible is not necessarily an annual ceiling

Hong Kong insurance-education guidance distinguishes the part of a covered loss retained by a policyholder from the insurer’s benefit limit. Commercial wording may apply a deductible separately to each occurrence. The precise contract matters; a single annual loss total can conceal repeated retained amounts.

The workshop, quotes and losses below are original fiction. No named insurer offers these terms. This is a comparison exercise, not advice to buy, cancel or claim under an actual policy.

Your classroom decision

You manage a small Hong Kong display-material workshop choosing next year’s optional stock-and-equipment property cover. Mandatory cover, contractual minimums and required prevention measures are already satisfied and stay in place. Both fictional quotes cover exactly the same events and property. Insurer reliability, settlement timing and service are identical. Neither quote has been accepted.

For each separate incident, subtract the deductible from its covered loss, but never below zero; the insurer pays that result up to its per-incident payout limit. The workshop retains the rest. There is no annual cap on deductibles, no annual aggregate payout limit, no coinsurance or underinsurance adjustment, no additional sublimit and no excess recovery from another policy. These are complete simplified classroom conditions, not universal policy rules.

Compare three hypothetical one-year scenarios: no loss; one covered incident costing HK$24,000; and one covered incident costing HK$60,000. No probabilities are supplied. The finance sheet also records HK$24,000 total covered property loss in a prior year, but you have not yet checked the incident-level record. Do not assume that total came from one event.

Premiums include all charges and are paid once for the full year. Compare annual premium plus retained property loss, ignoring tax and discounting. Settlement bridging funds and operating commitments are separately protected; both quotes are feasible for the supplied scenarios. Prevention and the underlying loss process do not change with the quote. You must explain the risk retained, not only the premium saved.

Round 2 · More information

The prior-year incident log arrives. Its HK$24,000 total came from four distinct, unrelated covered incidents on separate dates, each costing HK$6,000. The fictional terms treat each as a separate occurrence. Both insurers confirm that the deductible would apply four times, not once to the annual total.

Use the log to back-test both quotes on the same losses. This is information before choosing next year’s cover, not permission to change insurance retrospectively. The premium quotes remain valid and unchanged. The record supplies no reliable frequency forecast or probability for the coming year; one large loss, several smaller losses and no loss remain possibilities. Neither a favourable replay nor the cheapest no-loss premium establishes the best future choice.

Your decision

Would the incident-level record change your recommendation? Recalculate both quotes for the four losses, explain why the annual total was insufficient, and state what you still need before choosing.

Debrief · Apply the contract to each incident

For a covered loss L, insurer payment = min(limit, max(0, L − deductible)). Retained loss = L − payment. Add retained amounts across incidents, then add the annual premium once. Do not subtract a deductible twice, and do not add the full loss again after counting the retained portion.

Debrief · Three opening scenarios

With no loss, annual cost is A HK$9,000 versus B HK$4,000. For one HK$24,000 loss, A pays HK$22,000 and retains HK$2,000: cost HK$11,000. B pays HK$18,000 and retains HK$6,000: cost HK$10,000. B is HK$1,000 cheaper in that scenario.

For one HK$60,000 loss, A’s payout stops at HK$30,000, leaving HK$30,000 retained and total cost HK$39,000. B pays its HK$50,000 limit, leaving HK$10,000 retained and total cost HK$14,000. Retention can exceed the deductible when the payout limit binds.

Debrief · The same total, four deductibles

For each HK$6,000 incident, A pays HK$4,000 and retains HK$2,000. Four incidents produce HK$16,000 insurer payment and HK$8,000 retained loss; add the HK$9,000 premium for HK$17,000 total cost. B pays zero on each incident because each loss equals its deductible. It retains HK$24,000 and adds HK$4,000 premium: HK$28,000 total cost.

A is HK$11,000 cheaper in this replay. The annual gross loss is still HK$24,000, but applying B’s deductible only once would incorrectly produce HK$10,000 total cost. The number and size of incidents, and the contract’s occurrence definition, determine how much stays with the firm.

Debrief · Defend a recommendation without inventing probabilities

A can be defended when concern about repeated medium-sized incidents outweighs its higher fixed premium and lower payout limit. B can be defended when premium savings and protection against a larger single loss matter more, provided the firm accepts repeated deductibles. Neither dominates every supplied scenario. No expected annual cost can be calculated from unweighted scenarios.

Before signing, seek a longer, relevant incident history, plausible loss sizes and frequencies, and the firm’s tolerance and funding for retained losses. Confirm actual wording, valuations, exclusions, occurrence grouping, limits and settlement needs with a qualified adviser. A deductible is not an annual maximum loss, and the largest supplied scenario is not a worst-case bound. Do not weaken prevention or combine unrelated incidents into a claim.

Instructor note

Give pairs three minutes to compare the opening scenarios and cast a provisional vote. Reveal the incident log only after they state an assumption about loss frequency. Allow four minutes to calculate each incident’s insurer payment and retained loss, then sum and add one premium. Spend the final five minutes comparing the large-loss and repeated-loss arguments.

Require a conditional recommendation, not a claimed expected-value optimum. Watch for applying one annual deductible, confusing payout limits with gross-loss caps, treating the deductible as the maximum retained loss, or treating a prior year as a forecast. Do not ask students to disclose personal insurance or financial details. All arithmetic and necessary terms are provided; sources are optional.

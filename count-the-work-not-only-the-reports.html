PPL-18 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Span of control · Delegation · Managerial capacity

More people to lead. The same manager’s day?

Structure and decision rights change together

In an interview published on 26 April 2023, DBS’s human-resources leader described streamlining its structure and delegating decisions to lower organisational levels. This is a leader’s account, not proof that fewer layers work everywhere. Our fictional design studio asks a smaller question: which work should sit with which manager?

Your classroom decision

A fictional Hong Kong design studio has grown to 14 designers reporting to one studio manager. Two of the designers are experienced, willing and assessed as ready for bounded supervisory roles. They remain part of the 14; do not count them as new employees. All role changes require staff consultation and agreed paid preparation before this six-week trial. No redundancy, pay cut, unpaid management work or cancelled coaching is proposed.

A creates two teams. Each senior designer becomes a lead with six designer reports; both leads report to the studio manager. B creates one routine-work team: one lead supervises eight designers, while the other five designers report directly to the studio manager. Thus the studio manager has two direct reports under A and six under B, including B’s one lead. No employee has two line managers.

The same funded management envelope covers either arrangement, including allowances, paid preparation and design-work backfill. A protects ten supervisory hours weekly for each lead and twenty management hours for the studio manager. B protects ten hours for its one lead and thirty for the studio manager by buying more backfill of the manager’s individual design work. These are management hours, not whole working weeks; production duties fit the remaining contracted time. Neither choice promises a productivity gain.

Your decision

Which structure would you trial, and why is the number of direct reports insufficient to decide whether its managers have enough time?

Round 2 · More information

A consent-based work diary and project review distinguish routine approvals from interdependent work. Eight designers mostly produce repeat-format jobs within clear specifications. Five work on two connected bespoke projects requiring the studio manager’s cross-project judgement. The remaining designer is the experienced person who would lead the routine team under B. Under A, the second lead comes from the five bespoke designers; funded backfill preserves that person’s project output.

Eight weekly hours of the manager’s current work are routine approvals that an assessed lead can handle within written limits. Delegation does not remove those hours from the organisation; the estimates below include them in the leads’ workloads. The two bespoke projects still need eight manager-hours of coordination under either structure. Splitting their people between A’s teams does not make that work disappear. Quality, safety, client commitments, confidential personnel matters and escalation routes stay protected.

A joint planning estimate, including all routine decisions, individual coaching, handoffs and cross-project coordination, gives A’s studio manager 18 hours weekly and each lead nine hours. B gives the studio manager 22 hours and its lead ten hours. These are workload estimates, not measured future outcomes. Both fit their stated protected time. A distributes close coaching across smaller teams but adds two team interfaces; B keeps complex work directly connected to the studio manager but leaves no estimated spare supervisory time for its routine-team lead. No extra unpaid hours are available.

Your decision

Which trial would you choose now? Compare the remaining time at every management level and specify a review trigger that would change the design rather than simply asking people to work longer.

Debrief: count the whole structure

A’s 14 designers comprise two leads and twelve other designers. The manager’s span is two, and each lead’s span is six. B comprises one lead, eight designers reporting to that lead and five reporting directly to the manager. The manager’s span is six, not five; the lead’s is eight. Neither chart has removed the need for coaching or made the lead a free extra worker.

Time does not follow headcount automatically

A leaves 20 − 18 = 2 hours for the manager and 10 − 9 = 1 hour for each lead. B leaves 30 − 22 = 8 hours for the manager but 10 − 10 = 0 for the lead. Both allocate forty protected management hours in total; their estimated use is 36 under A and 32 under B. Spare time is attached to particular roles and cannot automatically cover another person’s confidential, skilled or time-critical responsibilities.

Choose a design, then watch its weak point

B may fit the diary’s work split and preserve direct cross-project coordination, but its routine lead is fully loaded even before unexpected coaching or exceptions. A provides more distributed coaching capacity and two developed leads, but the manager retains coordination across teams and has less spare time. Either can be defended with a workable review plan. Monitor actual coaching access, decision waiting time, rework and contracted-hour pressure by role; revise scope, backfill or reporting allocation if the estimates fail. Do not treat a universal “ideal” span as a substitute for those observations.

Instructor note

Ask students to sketch both charts before doing the time arithmetic. A headcount recommendation without task, authority and time allocation is incomplete. The diary does not establish causal productivity effects. No individual is being selected on sensitive data, and the exercise changes neither employment contracts nor professional safety authority. PPL-08 designs an employee’s job; ENT-09 transfers founder authority; this case designs management capacity across a growing team.

A promise of quality. Costly for whom?

A historical Hong Kong warranty example

On 15 January 2018, Hong Kong’s Consumer Council reported restrictive terms and limited transparency in a survey of private-car warranties and maintenance services. Its advice included understanding the scope and restrictions of a warranty.

That historical survey does not establish the quality of any repair business below. The original fictional case asks what makes a seller’s promise informative, rather than merely impressive.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A new Hong Kong bag-repair service believes its workmanship is reliable, but customers cannot easily assess it before buying. For its next 100 comparable repair jobs, it can advertise a truthful description of its checking process, or add a clearly written workmanship guarantee. Both routes keep all existing customer rights and ordinary remedies.

A uses the process description without an extra promise. B adds a 60-day promise: if the same repaired fault recurs and is verified, the service arranges collection and redelivery and provides a HK$200 inconvenience credit, in addition to the normal repair remedy. The written scope, claim route and contact details are available before purchase. The service has capacity and funding to honour it.

B needs HK$1,000 of additional administration for the 100 jobs, plus the cost of valid claims. The advertising cost is otherwise the same. No sales increase has been established. The owner is choosing a promise to customers, not permission to ignore faulty work under A.

RouteWhat customers seeAdded commitment
ATruthful description of checking processExisting rights and ordinary remedies retained
BSame description plus written 60-day promiseCollection/redelivery and HK$200 credit for a verified recurrence of the same repaired fault
Your decision

Would you introduce B’s guarantee now or use A while gathering evidence? Explain why a customer should believe the promise and what cost information you need before offering it.

Round 2 · More information

Use this deliberately simplified model to test the signal. There are two fixed workmanship types: reliable providers have a 2% valid-claim probability per job; less reliable providers have 20%. Each provider knows its own type; customers do not. Our owner is stipulated to be the reliable type. Offering the guarantee does not change workmanship in this model.

For either type, each job can generate at most one valid claim in the 60 days. The extra collection, redelivery and credit cost totals HK$400 per valid claim, above ordinary remedies already included under both routes. Administration remains HK$1,000. Assume all valid claims are honoured, the firm remains able to pay, and there are no other incremental costs.

For the same 100 jobs, suppose being recognised as a guarantee-offering provider brings HK$6,000 of extra contribution before these guarantee costs. This hypothetical benefit applies equally to either type if it copies B; it is not a measured demand forecast. Compare B with each type’s own A baseline. Customers can check the terms and claim arrangements, but cannot directly observe the provider’s type.

Assume providers choose the option with the higher expected incremental contribution, with no separate risk-aversion penalty.

Your decision

Under these assumptions, would both types want to offer B, or only one? Calculate each type’s expected added cost, then name one condition that could make the promise stop being a credible quality signal.

The same promise has different expected costs

For the reliable type, expected claims are 100 × 2% = 2. Added cost is HK$1,000 + 2 × HK$400 = HK$1,800. The hypothetical HK$6,000 benefit exceeds that by HK$4,200. For the less reliable type, expected claims are 20 and added cost is HK$9,000, leaving HK$3,000 less than its A baseline.

These are expected incremental comparisons, not guaranteed claim counts or total profit. Independence between jobs is not needed for the expected number, although dependence affects the risk of many claims arriving together.

Why the signal might separate types

Both types can print the same promise. They do not face the same expected cost of keeping it. In this model the reliable type gains by offering B while the less reliable type loses by copying it. That difference can support a separating signal, provided customers understand and trust the commitment.

The cheap description alone does not create this type-dependent obligation. It can still help customers and must remain truthful. A guarantee is not a substitute for evidence behind claims about workmanship, nor does it prove that an individual provider is reliable.

Test what would break the reasoning

If providers can evade valid claims or disappear, the apparent obligation may cost little and become easy to copy. If the extra benefit were HK$12,000 rather than HK$6,000, both types would gain from B under the same cost assumptions. The guarantee would no longer separate them through this simple incentive comparison.

A real decision needs evidence on claims, customer response, administration, collection costs and ability to fulfil the promise. Clear verification handles genuine uncertainty without treating every claimant as opportunistic. Preserve fair review and all existing rights; do not hide exclusions to make the promise artificially cheap.

Instructor note

Let the first vote remain provisional: claim probabilities and customer response have not yet been supplied. In round two, ask whether the less reliable type would imitate, not merely whether our reliable owner can afford B. Require both expected-cost calculations and the honouring assumption.

Quality is fixed here. This is different from ECO-10, which asks how coverage changes prevention behaviour. It also differs from a buyer checking a supplier’s quality: the seller chooses a potentially informative commitment. The arithmetic supports incentive compatibility within this restricted model, not a complete market equilibrium, welfare optimum or legal guarantee design. Foreign regulatory guidance is background, not Hong Kong advice.

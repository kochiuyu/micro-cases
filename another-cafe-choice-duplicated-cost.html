ECO-37 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Differentiated entry · Business stealing · Net social benefit

One more café. More choice or duplicated cost?

New sales are not all new value

An additional café can create real value by serving a more convenient location, even when its customers previously bought elsewhere. But an entrant’s receipts do not measure the whole social gain. A complete comparison counts changes in visitors’ convenience, both businesses’ results and the resources required to operate another outlet.

Your classroom decision

For one fictional Hong Kong promenade trading day, independent café A operates at the west end. Café B could open at the east end or remain closed. Both sites are permitted, accessible and safely staffed. Existing employment and contractual obligations are protected. This is an economic comparison, not a licensing decision or advice to exclude a competitor.

B would sell forty identical cups that day at HK$30 each. Each cup uses HK$10 of ingredients, labour and other variable resources. Opening B adds HK$700 of fixed operating resource cost for that day. All inputs include their opportunity costs; no unpaid work, tax, subsidy, rent transfer or uncounted setup cost is hidden.

Both cafés independently charge the supplied HK$30 price in every scenario, with identical drinks and service quality. Neither coordinates prices or contacts its rival. Prices and locations are exogenous classroom assumptions, not an equilibrium to solve. Each café has safe capacity for all the customers assigned to it.

The forty cups are a stipulated scenario, not an estimate from real demand research. The initial sheet does not say where those customers would otherwise buy, whether total visits increase or how walking effort changes. A positive B profit alone therefore cannot establish whether total visitor-and-business benefit rises.

Evaluate B’s private daily profit separately from total model benefit. Total model benefit equals all visitors’ willingness to pay, minus walking disutility and all variable and fixed operating resource costs. Equivalently, add visitor surplus and both firms’ profits after fixed costs. Visitor surplus is willingness to pay minus the cup price and walking disutility.

Round 2 · More information

The complete scenario contains exactly one hundred visitors: sixty at the west end and forty at the east end. Each values one cup at HK$50 before price and walking effort; extra cups have zero value. With or without B, each buys exactly one cup. There are no new promenade visits, lost visits or other customers.

Buying at one’s own end has zero walking disutility. Walking to the opposite end costs the visitor HK$10 in money-equivalent effort, including the entire return journey. This is a welfare cost, not a cash payment to a transport operator. There are no other travel costs or benefits.

Without B, A sells all one hundred cups: the forty eastern visitors walk west. With B, sixty western visitors remain at A and all forty eastern visitors buy from B. Nobody walks between ends. Customers independently choose the nearer otherwise-identical café. A remains open in both scenarios, with no change in service quality.

A also uses HK$10 of variable resources per cup and incurs HK$800 fixed daily operating resource cost whether B opens or not. Both firms’ fixed costs are real resources on this day, not transfers between participants. No congestion, environmental effects, other amenity benefits, future innovation or distributional weights are included.

First evaluate B at its supplied HK$700 fixed cost. Then ask what would happen if a separately feasible design reduced B’s fixed resource cost to HK$250 without changing capacity, wages, safety, quality, prices, walking or demand. This is a distinct hypothetical, not a free saving already available in the original proposal.

Your decision

Calculate visitor surplus, both cafés’ profits and total model benefit without and with B. Explain the difference between displaced sales and saved walking. Find the fixed-cost boundary for a strictly positive total benefit from entry, and assess the separate HK$250 design.

Debrief · Private entry can pay

B’s revenue is 40 × HK$30 = HK$1,200. Variable cost is 40 × HK$10 = HK$400. After its HK$700 fixed cost, B earns HK$100. That supports entry on B’s private profit criterion within this one-day scenario; it does not count the effect on A or value visitors’ shorter walks.

Before entry, A earns 100 × (HK$30 − HK$10) − HK$800 = HK$1,200. After entry, A earns 60 × HK$20 − HK$800 = HK$400. It remains profitable and open. A loses HK$800 while B gains HK$100, reducing combined business profit by HK$700.

Debrief · Convenience is not imaginary

Without B, western visitor surplus is 60 × (HK$50 − HK$30) = HK$1,200. Eastern visitor surplus is 40 × (HK$50 − HK$30 − HK$10) = HK$400. Total visitor surplus is HK$1,600. All visitors prefer buying to the zero-surplus no-purchase option.

With B, all one hundred visitors buy locally. Visitor surplus is 100 × HK$20 = HK$2,000, an increase of HK$400. The same forty people switch sellers, but they gain genuine convenience. Calling their purchases displaced does not make the walking benefit disappear.

Debrief · Reconcile the whole change

Without B, total model benefit is visitor surplus HK$1,600 plus A profit HK$1,200 = HK$2,800. With B, it is HK$2,000 + HK$400 + HK$100 = HK$2,500. Entry reduces this measure by HK$300, despite B’s positive HK$100 profit and visitors’ positive HK$400 gain.

The direct resource check gives the same result. Gross cup value stays 100 × HK$50 = HK$5,000 and variable cost stays HK$1,000. Initially subtract walking HK$400 and A fixed cost HK$800: HK$2,800. With entry subtract fixed costs HK$800 + HK$700 and no walking: HK$2,500.

Debrief · Do not count transfers as new gains

Total cup revenue remains HK$3,000; HK$1,200 merely changes recipient from A to B. The matching variable resources for forty cups also move between providers. The remaining HK$800 contribution shifts between firms before B’s fixed cost. Revenue transferred between buyers and sellers is not an extra social benefit.

Consumer surplus plus conventional producer surplus excludes fixed costs. This case instead adds profits after fixed costs, so it captures the extra outlet’s resource burden. Do not use the terms interchangeably or subtract B’s HK$700 twice. The comparison is a specified net-benefit measure, not an unlimited claim about societal welfare.

Debrief · Find the entry boundary

With all other inputs unchanged, the net benefit of B is HK$400 of saved walking minus B’s fixed resource cost F. Entry strictly improves total model benefit when F is below HK$400; at HK$400 the comparison ties. B’s private profit is HK$800 − F, positive whenever F is below HK$800.

At F = HK$250, B earns HK$550, combined profit becomes HK$950, and total model benefit is HK$2,950. Entry now adds HK$150 relative to HK$2,800. Between HK$400 and HK$800, entry can be privately profitable but socially negative in this model; endpoints require distinguishing a tie from a strict gain.

Debrief · Keep the conclusion bounded

Under the original HK$700 cost, decline entry on the supplied total-benefit criterion, while recognising why B would privately want to enter. The alternative HK$250 design passes both criteria. Location variety is valuable; whether its value exceeds duplicated resources depends on the actual numbers and counterfactual.

This calculation does not justify banning cafés, coordinating prices or granting incumbent protection. Real entry can change prices, quality, participation and future competition, all held fixed here. A broader policy decision needs those effects and distributional considerations, plus applicable legal processes; no public action is authorised.

Instructor note

Draw the two locations and place sixty visitors west and forty east. Require separate columns for visitor surplus, A profit and B profit. After computing the HK$300 loss, ask why the forty switchers still benefit. Finish by varying F while preserving safety and service assumptions.

ECO-20 studies lost independent alternatives after a proposed merger, without a complete numerical welfare estimate. ECO-36 studies an entrant’s recoverable investment and private incentive. Here a fully specified differentiated-location entry changes walking costs and fixed resource use, revealing why private profitability and net social gain can diverge.

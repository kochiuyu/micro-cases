PPL-39 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Team formation · Shared mental models · Team charter

Excellent alone. Ready to work together?

Individual skill is not a shared workflow

A group of capable people still needs compatible expectations about the joint work. A team charter is a short account of how members will coordinate, decide and check progress. It is useful only if understood and used. The consulting team and all timings below are fictional, not evidence that one meeting guarantees performance.

Your classroom decision

Five experienced adults form a new project team at fictional Harbour Advisory in Hong Kong: a project lead, analyst, researcher, designer and reviewer. Each has demonstrated the required individual skills on comparable assignments. None has worked in this exact team before. Their assignment is one integrated client briefing, not five independent deliverables.

All five have one protected 90-minute paid block before production begins. A uses 15 minutes for a task handoff and 75 for optional visual-template refinement. B uses 45 minutes for a focused team launch and 45 for that refinement. All preparation and recording fit inside these times; no extra meeting, overtime or unpaid work is available.

The agreed client requirements, quality checks, confidentiality and delivery deadline are unchanged under either route. Necessary production and review time is already funded separately. The template is usable now: extra refinement is optional, not an accessibility or quality requirement. B therefore displaces optional work rather than reducing mandatory checks.

The lead can clarify internal roles, handoffs and decision rules within the approved brief, and retains final release authority. The team cannot change client scope, waive checks or create new spending authority. A charter here is an internal working aid, not a new employment contract, disciplinary agreement or authority to send work to the client.

Round 2 · More information

A short written check, already completed within normal planning, reveals different meanings of “finished”. The analyst means calculations completed; the researcher means sources verified; the designer means pages formatted; the reviewer means checks signed off; the lead means ready for authorised client release. These are useful stages, but the team has not agreed how they connect.

The analyst assumes the reviewer can directly alter figures. The reviewer expects to flag questions for the analyst to resolve. The designer assumes formatted pages will not change. No common revision cutoff, source-of-truth file or disagreement route has been specified. No client work has yet been released, and no error or misconduct is established.

All five are available and willing to compare assumptions. The lead can settle internal workflow questions during B, with everyone able to raise concerns. A still remains available, but a brief task list would have to address these same mismatches to be adequate. There is no evidence that the team needs a repeat technical-skills course.

Your decision

Keep or change the launch choice. Propose a short charter covering a shared outcome, stage-specific completion, review authority, handoff and disagreement. Calculate B’s extra coordination time in person-hours, and specify how the team will test understanding without assuming that signing a page proves alignment.

Debrief · Competence is not common understanding

B is the stronger choice after the reveal. The issue is not whether the analyst can calculate or the designer can format; it is whether their assumptions fit together. Different meanings of “finished” can coexist if stages and handoffs are explicit. Do not label the members incompetent or difficult.

A can be reasonable when shared expectations are already established or can be checked briefly. Here several connected mismatches remain unresolved. The justification for B is focused coordination before interdependent work, not a universal rule that every new team needs a long meeting.

Debrief · Make the trade-off explicit

Both routes use 90 × 5 = 450 person-minutes, or 7.5 person-hours. A uses 1.25 person-hours for coordination and 6.25 for refinement. B uses 3.75 for coordination and 3.75 for refinement. B reallocates 2.5 person-hours; it does not add 2.5 hours to anyone’s working day.

The optional template refinement falls by 30 elapsed minutes, from 75 to 45. Necessary production, accessible presentation and required review remain funded. No evidence values the displaced refinement in dollars or proves that B will save more time later. Its proposed benefit is a better-specified joint workflow.

Debrief · State the joint outcome

A possible charter begins: “Produce one briefing that meets the approved client requirements, has traceable evidence and checked calculations, and is released only by the lead.” All five should be able to explain that outcome. It does not mean five polished sections independently count as a completed briefing.

Define stages separately: analyst calculation draft; researcher evidence verification; designer assembled layout; reviewer documented checks; lead release decision. Record the input each stage needs and its named owner. Existing quality requirements still apply; stage labels must not substitute for doing the checks.

Debrief · Assign authority and revisions

One defensible rule is that the reviewer records questions and proposed corrections; the analyst owns numerical changes, with the researcher checking affected evidence. The designer updates the assembled version after accepted changes. The reviewer rechecks affected material, and the lead decides release. Other rules can work if responsibility and rechecking are equally clear.

Use one agreed working file and a visible change log. Set internal handoff cutoffs within the existing project schedule. A late change must be flagged, assessed and rechecked, not silently inserted or forbidden regardless of importance. The lead resolves workflow disagreements; client-scope changes still require the existing authorised route.

Debrief · Check the mental model

Ask each member to explain the next handoff in their own words. Walk through a hypothetical corrected figure arriving after layout: who changes it, who is told, what is rechecked and who may release? If answers conflict, revise the working aid before treating the charter as usable.

Alignment does not require identical expertise or suppressing disagreement. Members should understand the shared workflow while retaining their specialist judgments and the ability to raise concerns. A signature, unanimous nod or completed template alone does not demonstrate that this understanding exists.

Debrief · Keep it live

At the already funded first production checkpoint, compare actual handoffs with the charter. Record unclear responsibilities, unresolved changes and checks awaiting completion; adjust the working aid through the lead’s existing authority. Do not create an unfunded standing meeting or claim that a charter prevents every delay or disagreement.

Instructor note

Use four minutes for the opening choice, four for the conflicting assumptions and six for a compact charter and walk-through. Ask which part clarifies joint work rather than retrains an individual. PPL-04 concerns novice readiness, PPL-25 incompatible managerial instructions and PPL-29 reflection after delivery; this case forms a shared workflow before execution.

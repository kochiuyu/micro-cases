ENT-23 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Entrepreneurial bricolage · Resource recombination · Repeatable routines

Use what is available. Build what is repeatable?

Resourcefulness is more than buying cheaply

Entrepreneurship research describes bricolage as recombining available resources for a new use. It can make a service possible without acquiring an entirely purpose-built system. The same research also examines limits when repeated improvisation becomes the normal operating pattern. These are contextual findings, not a rule that borrowed equipment fails or new equipment guarantees growth.

The venture, equipment, prices, tests and customer opportunity below are invented. Safety, permission and paid work are requirements, not constraints to evade.

Your classroom decision

A fictional Hong Kong founder is starting a tabletop product-photography service for small craft sellers. She owns a suitable camera and has HK$30,000 available for choosing a setup. Required operating costs, insurance and paid working time are funded separately; this HK$30,000 is not needed to meet those commitments. She wants to retain a cash cushion while learning what customers need.

R combines permitted spare display boards, her camera, professionally checked borrowed lights and a documented shared-room booking. A display board originally used for exhibitions becomes a background; the combination can accommodate unusual product shapes. The HK$3,000 package includes agreed borrowing compensation, safe accessories and all access charges for this period. Nothing is taken without permission, and no unpaid help is assumed.

S purchases a compatible standard tabletop kit for HK$12,000, using the same room and camera. Repeatable positions and a documented setup sequence may reduce rebuilding, but the founder commits more cash before knowing the job mix. Both options have passed the same basic image-quality and safety checks. Neither has yet been timed across repeat jobs. All setup payments remain uncommitted; no customer delivery has been promised.

The initial choice is provisional. Both are affordable, have the same customer permissions and deliver the specified image quality. Future demand, resale values and long-run profits are not supplied. Do not assume that either equipment price establishes its speed.

Recognise the recombination

R is not merely a cheap version of S. Existing camera, display materials, lights and room access are combined to supply a new service. Its flexibility for unusual shapes is useful. Borrowing still has a price, a permission boundary and a time cost; the apparent resource is not free simply because someone already owns it.

Count repeat work, not the first success

Eight repeat jobs require R: 8 × 6 = 48 hours, S: 8 × 3 = 24 hours, or H: 8 × 4 = 32 hours. R cannot honestly promise all eight within 32 hours. It can accept at most five whole jobs, using 30 hours and leaving two. S can accept all eight with eight hours of slack; H can accept all eight with no slack beyond the stated job allowances.

Keep cash and capacity separate

R leaves HK$27,000 after setup, S HK$18,000 and H HK$23,000. H preserves HK$5,000 more than S but uses eight more delivery hours for the eight jobs. R preserves HK$4,000 more than H but cannot serve three of the offered jobs. These balances are not profit: customer payments, operating economics and future asset values are not fully modelled. Do not count the separately funded operating budget again or pretend spare time is cash.

Standardise a part, not every possibility

H shows that retaining resourcefulness and building a repeatable core need not be opposites. Fix the positions, reset sequence, checks and handoff for the repeat package; retain adaptable boards for unusual work only when it is safely scoped and timed. A checklist is useful only if someone can execute it with the available equipment. Recombination outside that core remains possible without rebuilding every routine for every job.

Make a bounded commitment

H is defensible if serving eight jobs while preserving more cash is the priority and its full-use schedule is acceptable. S is defensible if the eight-hour cushion and simpler routine justify committing HK$5,000 more. R is defensible if retaining cash matters more than taking every offered job, with acceptance capped at five. None requires declaring that bricolage is bad or that standardisation always wins.

Before accepting future work, check reset time, variation, permissions and quality under the actual job mix. A successful unusual job is evidence of useful capability, not proof of repeat capacity. The eight jobs do not establish future demand or justify indefinite expansion.

Instructor note

Vote on the provisional setup before revealing timings. Ask learners to identify the resource recombination, then calculate feasible whole jobs without inventing overtime. Require a concrete choice between cash cushion, accepted work and time slack. Distinguish selective upgrading here from a grant comparison or transporting a selling routine from a previous business. Research about bending conventions is not permission to bypass safety, employment, ownership or customer obligations.

PPL-13 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Procedural fairness · Leave planning · Employee voice

The same leave entitlement. The same fair process?

In the news

A Hong Kong Family and Women Portal article dated 21 August 2024 explained family-status discrimination protections, including their employment context. It points employers towards fair treatment rather than assumptions about people with caring responsibilities.

The fictional allocation below is not a reported dispute or a legal ruling. It asks how to design a fair process while preserving statutory and contractual rights.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. Eight employees in a Hong Kong repair workshop want the same five-working-day break three months from now. Each has enough paid annual leave. A checked roster needs five qualified colleagues at work throughout, so at most three can take that whole break. All eight can cover the relevant work; no extra qualified cover is available for this exercise.

No request window has opened, no dates have been approved and no application-order promise has been made. The manager is consulting everyone before announcing the process. All annual leave can still be taken within the required period; alternative dates are available. Entitlement, pay, rest days, statutory holidays and any specific legal or contractual protections are not being voted away.

Option A records requests in arrival order after an announced opening time. It is simple and gives early certainty. Option B collects requests over a week, then gives priority to colleagues who did not receive this popular break last year, with a disclosed lottery for equal priority. It includes a confidential route to consider exceptional, fixed-date needs. B shares opportunity over time but requires records and explanations.

Your decision

Which process would you recommend before requests open: A or B? Explain what makes it fair to someone who does not receive the dates they prefer, and identify one fact you would check before announcing it.

Round 2 · More information

The proposed portal opens at 9am. Three colleagues work at desks then; five are assigned off-site work and cannot use the work-only portal until 3pm. Staff are not expected to apply during unpaid time or use personal devices. A shared opening time would therefore give unequal practical access.

The leave history has been checked with all eight: the same three received this popular break last year; the other five did not. Separately, two colleagues voluntarily give the manager limited, confidential evidence of fixed-date commitments: one has an unavoidable caring arrangement and one an externally scheduled personal milestone. The case does not ask the class to compare their private lives or declare either a legal priority.

Both proposals can be revised before launch. Paid application access can be arranged for everyone, and a confidential, consistently applied exception-and-review route can accompany either rule. Any specific statutory, contractual or anti-discrimination requirement must be assessed before ordinary preferences are allocated; unresolved obligations go to appropriate advice, not a team vote. The three-place ordinary planning limit is not permission to override those duties. No decisions have yet been made.

Your decision

What process would you announce now? Specify fair access, the ordinary priority rule, how time-specific requests will be considered privately, and how a colleague can correct an error without having to disclose personal details to the team.

Equal entitlement does not settle equal opportunity

The number of leave days and the allocation of one popular period are different questions. A timestamp can look neutral while rewarding a work assignment that supplies earlier access. Fixing access is necessary before relying on arrival order; telling off-site colleagues to hurry is not a remedy.

A revised A can retain quick, clear ordering if access is genuinely comparable. B avoids a race within its application week and recognises previous opportunities, but can still be unfair if records, tie-breaking or exceptions are arbitrary.

Hear the relevant need, not the most public story

Invite everyone to comment before the rule is settled. State what information is relevant to fixed-date constraints and who may see it. Request only what is needed; explain the process to colleagues without sharing another person’s evidence.

Check applicable obligations first. For remaining discretionary requests, define the exception criterion consistently and explain how it interacts with rotation or arrival order. Do not automatically rank parents above non-parents, or dismiss a caring need because the published rule looks equal. If exceptional needs exceed available cover, escalate and explore lawful arrangements rather than inventing a priority from this case.

Explain and allow correction

Announce the ordinary rule, tie-break, application window, response date, confidential review contact and how prior-year records will be checked. Give an unsuccessful applicant the reason relevant to their own request and help plan other dates without reducing their entitlement.

A reviewer not involved in the initial decision can check an incorrect record or inconsistent application. Review is not a promise that every disappointed person will win. Keep future rules predictable, but correct errors rather than repeating them in the name of consistency.

Instructor note

Vote on the process, not on which named person deserves leave. Reveal unequal portal access and ask students to distinguish a shared written rule from a fair opportunity to use it. Ask what rotation solves, and what it does not solve.

A strong answer may recommend B with verified history and a private exception route, or a substantially repaired A with equal paid access and the same protections. Neither process guarantees satisfaction or legal compliance. Do not calculate a lottery until protected obligations and exceptions are resolved: three ordinary places are not necessarily three lottery places. Unlike PPL-06, the issue is legitimacy of allocating scarce preferred dates, not coordinating a task dependency. Do not stereotype employee preferences using the research source’s cultural categories.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All workshop, roster, history, proposed rules, access restrictions and private commitments are invented. This is prospective process design: no application queue, approved leave or established promise is reversed. All eight have sufficient entitlement, alternative leave dates and interchangeable relevant qualifications. Five-person coverage is a stipulated ordinary planning constraint, not an excuse to disregard legal or contractual rights. The case does not adjudicate any real employee, determine legal priority or ask students to publish private information. Specific obligations must be examined before discretionary allocation. Neither the caring arrangement nor personal milestone is assigned a legal classification by the draft. Voluntary limited evidence goes only to the manager/reviewer; the team sees reasons and rules without others’ personal details. Source-derived general process principles are not a compliance checklist. Hong Kong Labour Department guidance supplies local consultation/notice context, while Acas and Business Gateway are UK/Scottish background, not Hong Kong law. Business Gateway contains dated legal material elsewhere; only its general holiday-process discussion is relied on, not current statutory figures. The research paper’s conceptual discussion was read; no national-cultural stereotype, effect size or claim that this rule improves retention is adopted. Portal article date is its displayed 21 August 2024, not an inferred date from its URL.

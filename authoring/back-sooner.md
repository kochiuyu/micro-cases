# Back sooner. Worth more?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 86 -->

FIN-08 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Finance · Investment appraisal · Payback and present value

![A training-studio owner considers two unlabelled room-layout boards beside a tradesperson gesturing toward the sunlit spare room.](../back-sooner-scene.jpg)

## In the news

On 8 July 2026, InvestHK announced a works contract for an HKUST building retrofit. Schneider Electric and Veolia would finance the upfront investment and recover it through verified energy savings over 15 years.

This announced arrangement, not a report of realised savings, highlights the importance of an investment’s time horizon. The self-funded studio and estimates below are fictional and unrelated to that project.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong training studio has one spare room and two alternative fit-out plans. A supports an established short-course format with stronger early demand. B supports a programme expected to build over time.

Either costs HK$100,000 today and runs for the same four-year remaining lease. Only one can occupy the room. Doing neither is also possible. Cash and operating reserves are sufficient for either plan; existing classes remain funded.

The owner’s summary says A pays back at the end of year 2, B at the end of year 3. “Why wait an extra year?” she asks. The full four-year estimates have not yet been compared.

## Evidence

| Initial summary · HK$ | Plan A | Plan B |
|---|---:|---:|
| All-in outlay today | 100,000 | 100,000 |
| Year 1 net receipt | 60,000 | 20,000 |
| Year 2 net receipt | 40,000 | 40,000 |
| Reported simple payback | End of year 2 | End of year 3 |

Net receipts mean incremental cash after all relevant operating costs, maintenance, working-capital movements and tax, not sales or accounting profit. Model all receipts at year-end. No disposal proceeds or cash flows after year 4.

**Student question:** Which plan would you provisionally favour? Is the shorter payback enough to commit, and what is missing from this comparison?

## Round two

The accountant completes the full-life review. A is forecast to bring in HK$10,000 in year 3 and HK$10,000 in year 4. B is forecast to bring in HK$60,000 in each of those years. The earlier figures are unchanged. The review also confirms that the studio can use the room for all four years and fund either timing profile.

| Complete forecast · HK$ | Plan A | Plan B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Outlay today | 100,000 | 100,000 |
| Year 1 net receipt | 60,000 | 20,000 |
| Year 2 net receipt | 40,000 | 40,000 |
| Year 3 net receipt | 10,000 | 60,000 |
| Year 4 net receipt | 10,000 | 60,000 |

For this exercise, the plans have comparable forecast risk and the supplied required return is 10% a year. Converting every year’s net receipt to its value today gives HK$101,947 for A and HK$137,299 for B, rounded. Subtracting each HK$100,000 outlay gives net present value, or NPV, of HK$1,947 and HK$37,299.

These are reviewed planning estimates, not guaranteed enrolments or cash already earned. The 10% is a classroom assumption, not a loan quote.

**Student question:** Keep A for its faster recovery, choose B for its higher modeled NPV, or decline both? Explain your recommendation and one assumption that could change it.

## Recovery answers one question

A’s first two receipts total HK$100,000, recovering the initial outlay at the end of year 2. B has recovered HK$60,000 by then; its year-3 receipt takes the total to HK$120,000, so its payback is at the end of year 3.

Payback asks when the investment is recovered. It does not count the full value of receipts after that point, and simple payback does not adjust for time value. Earlier recovery can still matter when cash is scarce or the usable horizon is short.

## Put the years on the same footing

At a 10% required return, HK$1,100 received one year from now has a present value of HK$1,000. To compare plans today, divide a year-1 receipt by 1.10, a year-2 receipt by 1.10², and so on.

Adding all four discounted receipts gives about HK$101,947 for A and HK$137,299 for B. After their equal initial outlays, NPV is about HK$1,947 and HK$37,299. These amounts include the time-value adjustment; the undiscounted surpluses of HK$20,000 and HK$80,000 are different measures.

## Choose the stronger full-life plan on these facts

B is the stronger choice under the supplied assumptions. Both have positive modeled NPV, but only one fits the room, and B contributes more value at the required return. It wins despite later recovery, not because later cash is inherently better.

A remains a feasible alternative, but its faster payback is not enough to outweigh the full-life comparison here: funding and four-year use are explicitly available. Declining both would need a reason beyond these forecasts and the supplied required return.

## Keep the forecast honest

Higher NPV is not a promise that the bank balance will rise by that amount today, nor is it reported accounting profit. It is the value implied by the chosen cash-flow estimates, timing and required return.

If later enrolments disappoint, the room becomes unavailable sooner, risks differ materially or reserves are no longer sufficient, revisit the comparison. A real liquidity constraint can make earlier receipts valuable; it should be examined explicitly, not assumed away or quietly invented after the calculation.

## Instructor note

Take a provisional vote before revealing the later receipts and supplied present-value totals. Students can explain the choice without calculating powers or reading outside sources. Ask what simple payback measures and what it omits.

For an optional calculation, use NPV = −100,000 + C₁/1.10 + C₂/1.10² + C₃/1.10³ + C₄/1.10⁴. Unrounded results are HK$1,946.59 and HK$37,299.36. Because cash arrives at year-end, do not interpolate B’s payback to 2.67 years.

All relevant project cash movements are already included; do not deduct depreciation or add loan interest to this self-funded project comparison. The supplied discount rate represents the required return. This case extends beyond Case 7’s one-year valuation and differs from Case 62’s loan repayment comparison. Do not turn it into an IRR or tax exercise.

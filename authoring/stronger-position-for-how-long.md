# A stronger position. For how long?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 354 -->

STR-30 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Dynamic capabilities · Channel migration · Learning lead times

![A channel manager compares an established ordering desk with an emerging digital-order workflow.](../stronger-position-for-how-long-scene.jpg)

## Today’s performance and tomorrow’s adaptability

Dynamic-capabilities research distinguishes operating effectively now from sensing change, committing resources and reconfiguring how a firm works. It also warns that maintaining change capabilities is costly and does not guarantee advantage. The channel signals, resources and timing below are fictional decision inputs, not a prediction about any industry.

## Opening decision

A Hong Kong trade-supplies distributor is profitable through account managers who collect customer orders and coordinate fulfilment. Some customers are exploring structured digital purchase orders that connect their procurement systems directly to suppliers. The distributor has a website, but cannot yet reliably validate incoming order fields, reconcile exceptions and transfer confirmed orders into fulfilment without duplicate entry. Buying a connector alone would not establish that operating capability.

The firm has twenty paid improvement hours each week for the next twelve weeks, separate from the staffing required to honour existing orders. A assigns all twenty hours to streamlining the current account-manager channel. B assigns eight hours to that work and twelve to learning the digital-order workflow with synthetic test orders, staff practice and documented exception handling. Both use the same funded hours and protect service standards, privacy and existing obligations. No live migration, customer commitment, new vendor contract or further spending is approved by this choice.

A targets a proven profitable channel and avoids building an unused capability. B gives up some current-channel improvement to develop a potential transition path. Current sales have not fallen. The decision is whether to begin the learning work now, not close the existing channel or establish a separate innovation unit. Customer demand, the eventual economics and the amount of later investment remain uncertain.

## Evidence

12 weeks × 20 funded improvement hours = 240 hours. A: 240 current-channel improvement. B: 96 current improvement + 144 digital-workflow learning. Existing order-service capacity is separately protected. Neither route has a quantified return forecast.

**Student question:** Would you choose A or B for the next twelve weeks? Distinguish improving the present channel from developing the ability to change channels.

## Round two

An exploratory review of twenty existing customers finds that eight intend to require connected purchase orders for at least some buying around twelve months from now; twelve report no current timetable. These are stated intentions, not signed changes or a representative market-share forecast. Existing revenue is still stable, and customers may retain both channels. The uneven signal argues against abandoning account managers, but does not establish that waiting is costless.

A technical and operations assessment estimates nine elapsed months from the start of a sustained learning programme to demonstrated readiness: data-field mapping, exception practice, staff learning and supervised reconciliation tests. The proposed twelve weeks approximate the first three months for this planning comparison. It is the first phase, not a complete capability. The later six months would require separately approved resources and continued access to the relevant expertise; neither is guaranteed. Simply buying software later would not remove the assessed learning sequence.

If the full programme starts now and proceeds as assessed, readiness is around month nine, three months before the customers’ possible month-twelve change. Waiting six months for clearer sales evidence and then completing the same nine-month programme gives readiness around month fifteen, three months after it. These conditional dates are not probabilities or promises. At the end of the first phase, managers can assess customer intentions, demonstrated workflow learning, revised remaining costs and protected service performance before authorising continuation. They can stop if the transition case weakens.

**Student question:** Would you keep or change your allocation? Specify what to sense, what to commit now, and what evidence would justify reconfiguring operations later rather than simply buying more technology.

## Answer the first decision: distinguish two capabilities

A improves an ordinary capability: efficiently taking and fulfilling current orders. B begins a repeatable process for changing how orders are received, checked and fulfilled. That is the relevant dynamic-capability task, not the mere ownership of a connector. I would initially consider B as a bounded learning commitment while preserving today’s delivery; A remains defensible if expected transition value cannot justify displaced improvement work.

## Answer the revealed decision: B with conditional continuation

I favour B’s first phase because a nine-month learning lead time makes a six-month wait strategically consequential even without current sales decline. Allocate the 144 transition hours and retain 96 hours of current improvement. Do not authorise the full programme implicitly. Reassess at the twelve-week gate using remaining cost, opportunity cost, customer evidence and actual learning. If the case no longer justifies continuation, stop or redesign rather than treating initial effort as a reason to spend more.

## Sense, commit, reconfigure

Sensing means regularly verifying which customers intend to change, which order types are involved, their timing and what would cause plans to slip. Seizing means reserving the bounded learning time and assigning responsibility for evidence and decisions. Reconfiguring means eventually changing data ownership, exception-handling routines, staff skills and handoffs to fulfilment after supervised tests establish readiness. No live channel switch follows from a successful software demonstration alone.

## Keep the clock conditional

Starting now plus nine months yields month-nine readiness; six months of delay plus nine yields month fifteen. Relative to a possible month-twelve requirement, that is three months early versus three months late. It is a timing comparison, not a forecast of lost sales. Uneven migration and stable present revenue support keeping the existing channel available. Later continuation requires funded resources, feasible costs and validated readiness, not optimism about every customer moving together.

## Instructor note

Ask for observable routines and decision gates rather than the slogan “be agile”. A digital connector is an input; repeatable learning and reconfiguration are capabilities. Theory sources disagree about how capabilities create enduring advantage and explicitly recognise maintenance costs. None estimates the fictional nine-month lead time, migration probability or return. The case concerns timing under ambiguous signals, not harvesting a declining division or choosing an organisational chart.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

All customer counts, hours, channels and lead times are original fiction. A: 20×12=240 current-improvement hours. B: 8×12=96 current plus 12×12=144 transition, total 240. Required service staffing is separate, so no customer obligation is sacrificed. For planning only twelve weeks approximate three months; later six months need separate approval. Nine-month readiness assumes sustained resources and successful learning, not merely first-phase authorisation. Start 0+9=9; wait 6+9=15; possible requirement 12 yields buffers +3 and −3 months. Eight of 20 is 40% of interviewed customers, not 40% revenue, a population estimate or a migration probability. No quantified return or economic optimal it y is claimed. Five original conceptual sources overlap; theoretical arguments are not five independent empirical confirmations.

- [Teece: Explicating Dynamic Capabilities, 2007](https://www.academia.edu/28916243/EXPLICATING_DYNAMIC_CAPABILITIES_THE_NATURE_AND_MICROFOUNDATIONS_OF_SUSTAINABLE_ENTERPRISE_PERFORMANCE). Original framework develops sensing, seizing and reconfiguring; capability development differs from operational efficiency.
- [Eisenhardt and Martin: Dynamic Capabilities: What Are They?, 2000](https://www.theisrm.org/documents/eisenhardt-martin-2000-dynamic-capabilities-what-are-they.pdf). Original theory article treats capabilities as identifiable processes and cautions that they are not sufficient guarantees of sustained advantage.
- [Winter: Understanding Dynamic Capabilities, 2003](https://web.mit.edu/rgibbons/www/nano2004/lecture2/Understanding_dynamic_capabilities_winter.pdf). Original conceptual note distinguishes ordinary capabilities, change routines and ad hoc problem solving, including the costs of maintaining change capability.
- [Zollo and Winter: Deliberate Learning and the Evolution of Dynamic Capabilities, 2002](https://www.academia.edu/50525970/Deliberate_Learning_and_the_Evolution_of_Dynamic_Capabilities). Original theoretical analysis examines experience, articulation and codification in developing routines; no universal learning timetable is prescribed.
- [Teece, Pisano and Shuen: Dynamic Capabilities and Strategic Management, 1997](https://www.ahri.com.au/wp-content/uploads/Teece-Pisano-and-Shuen-1997.pdf). Foundational original framework connects coordination, learning and reconfiguration with paths and assets; change is costly and context-dependent.

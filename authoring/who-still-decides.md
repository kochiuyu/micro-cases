# A new leader. Who still decides?

ENT-09 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Entrepreneurship · Family succession · Management authority

## In the news

A CUHK Business School feature posted on 7 April 2016 discussed family-business succession and the relationships built around a founder.

The page says the story first appeared in November 2014. It is historical context, not a prediction about the fictional family below.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. Mrs Chan, founder of a Hong Kong stationery wholesaler, wants to move from full-time management to two scheduled advisory days a week over the next six months. She will keep her shares and remain on the board.

The family and board have already selected Alex, an experienced adult family member, as the next general manager. Alex wants the role, knows the operation and has demonstrated competence in its ordinary work. The decision is how to transfer management, not whom to appoint.

Both proposed arrangements are affordable and workable. Ownership, pay, staffing rights and customer commitments stay unchanged. Existing quality, payment and approval controls remain. The class is designing a transition, not signing or changing legal documents.

## Evidence

| Transition | Continuing founder involvement |
|---|---|
| A · Six months of co-management | Alex leads daily work; routine quotes and supplier orders need joint approval. Mrs Chan provides a daily remote approval window outside her two advisory days. |
| B · Dated management handover | After agreed preparation, Alex decides routine matters within board-approved policies and budget. Mrs Chan advises on scheduled days; the board monitors and retains defined major decisions. |

A preserves frequent access to the founder’s judgement but retains a regular management obligation. B gives clearer operating accountability but requires boundaries, reporting and willingness to let Alex decide.

Neither arrangement is established as cheaper or more profitable. Formal authority and documentation would require appropriate professional advice before implementation.

**Student question:** Would you choose six-month co-management or a dated handover with bounded authority? What knowledge or risk would justify keeping the founder involved in routine approvals?

## Round two

In a facilitated planning discussion, Mrs Chan identifies her main concerns: new bank borrowing and a change to the family trading name. She does not need to approve every ordinary quote that follows the agreed policies. Once the reporting line is agreed, she is willing to stop giving staff separate instructions.

Alex accepts reserving those two major matters for the board under properly documented arrangements, with monthly reports on cash, overdue customer accounts, order errors and policy exceptions.

Ordinary orders already have price limits, approved suppliers and a second-person payment check. Alex can work within these rules; the payment check stays. The transition plan will name an escalation route for exceptional terms.

The preparation period includes agreed introductions to key customers and suppliers and a short list of topics for the founder’s advice. These are agreed planning intentions, not evidence that relationships or authority have already transferred successfully.

**Student question:** Which transition would you now choose? Propose a handover or review date, a visible way to transfer key relationships, the founder’s continuing advisory role and a route for disagreements without two competing bosses.

## Keep ownership, oversight and management distinct

Mrs Chan’s shares are not being transferred. Her continued ownership therefore does not answer who should approve tomorrow’s ordinary quotation. She also has a board role and a proposed advisory role; those are different from giving daily instructions.

Under B’s proposed design, the board oversees the business and retains the specified major decisions. Alex runs ordinary work inside the agreed boundaries. Actual powers must be checked in the company’s arrangements, not inferred from a title.

## Agree the founder–successor transition compact

Introductions let Alex learn how important customer and supplier relationships work. Scheduled advice gives Mrs Chan a continuing contribution without silently restoring approval of every ordinary order.

A monthly report should make problems visible. If a serious concern needs board intervention, use the formal agreed route. Quietly sending a different instruction to staff would leave them unsure whether to follow Alex or the founder.

A short transition compact could set the chosen handover or review date, schedule joint introductions where Mrs Chan visibly supports Alex’s role, identify the advice she will continue to provide and direct disagreements to an agreed discussion or board route rather than rival staff instructions. The pair must agree its workable details; a nameplate alone cannot transfer those relationships.

## Check the compact against three decisions

Ordinary quote inside approved limits: under B, Alex decides and records it. Consult through the named exception route if the terms fall outside policy. Existing checks remain; relevant exceptions enter the monthly report.

Proposed new loan: Alex prepares information; the board considers the reserved decision, including Mrs Chan in her board role and appropriate professional review before any commitment. Record the decision through the agreed governance process.

Proposed trading-name change: bring the proposal to the board rather than announcing it as a routine management decision. Record its consideration and communicate any properly authorised outcome through one agreed route.

These are the fictional proposal’s boundaries, not statements that every company must allocate legal powers identically. If choosing A, routine quotes still need the agreed joint approval; name who conveys that decision to staff.

## Choose a real transition, not a new nameplate

B is now more credible: Mrs Chan’s major concerns can be addressed without every routine decision returning to her. It still needs completed boundaries, introductions, documentation and a clear handover date.

A remains defensible if important practical knowledge still needs repeated joint decisions or boundaries remain unfinished. It requires the founder’s daily time commitment, one route for communicating decisions and an end or review date. There is no cost-free option that simultaneously removes daily involvement and retains every joint approval.

After an agreed initial period, review unresolved escalations, duplicate instructions, policy exceptions and whether advisory time matches the plan. Those signals invite a documented adjustment, not an informal return to two bosses.

## Instructor note

Do not reopen the selection of Alex or infer ability from family membership. Readiness and willingness are supplied facts. Ask what actually changes in routine decision-making under the chosen transition.

The main student output is a short founder–successor transition compact: a handover or review date, visible relationship introductions, continuing advice and an agreed disagreement route. Use the three-decision map only to check whether that compact gives staff consistent instructions. Under A routine approval remains joint; under B it is bounded management authority.

This concerns a founder’s continuing relationship and authority during succession, not simply a purchase-approval threshold or employee task ownership. No shares are sold, loan signed, employment action taken or legal filing authorised. Advisory roles, applicable duties and formal documents require appropriate advice outside the classroom.

## Sources and case assumptions

All information needed for classroom decisions is in the case. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

The family, company, successor, transition options and concerns are original fiction. Alex is already competent, willing and selected; ancestry is not used to decide eligibility. Mrs Chan’s transition is voluntary and ownership stays unchanged. A includes a workable scheduled daily remote approval window, so its cost is continuing management attention rather than assumed impossibility. B has preparation, defined boundaries and oversight, not unlimited discretion or removal of payment checks. The founder’s willingness is enabling evidence, not observed successful succession. Proposed board reservations do not establish actual legal powers, and no corporate, financial or employment action is executed. Six months and two advisory days are scenario facts, not optimal schedules. Five institutional documents were read by researcher and implementer; the CUHK feature is not the underlying study, and guidance is not causal evidence.

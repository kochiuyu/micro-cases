# The default stays. Is it the preferred choice?

## Remaining enrolled is an observation, not a complete welfare measure

Research on defaults and active decisions distinguishes the outcome that occurs without action from a choice someone explicitly makes. Choi and colleagues’ early active-decisions paper and Beshears and colleagues’ preference analysis explain why passive behaviour may reflect more than stable preference. Johnson and Goldstein also discuss recommendation, effort and status-quo mechanisms.

The CMA’s evidence review and Alma Economics’ government-commissioned online experiment examine how interface defaults can affect behaviour while complicating welfare inference. These settings do not supply the cooperative’s numbers. No retirement, medical-consent or privacy default is being selected here; every observation below is invented.

## Opening decision

**Fictional classroom situation.** An adult reading cooperative offers an optional free service: staff place a reusable folder containing a suggested reading list in a member’s existing collection cubby each month. It adds no fees, delivery, messages, tracking, personal-data category or contractual obligation. There is no penalty for ignoring or returning a folder. Ordinary membership and access to books are unaffected. Capacity and funding cover either design.

Under Opt-in, the service stays off unless a member turns it on. Under Opt-out, it starts on unless a member turns it off. Both display the same clear description and current status with equally prominent on/off controls. Changes are immediate, reversible and free, with one action online or equivalent accessible staff assistance. There is no hidden exit friction or difference in service terms. All necessary permissions and privacy/consent requirements have been satisfied independently; a default never substitutes for required consent.

Two separate convenience groups of fifty consenting pilot members try the designs for one month. Allocation was not random, and no information establishes identical preferences between the groups. At month-end, fifteen of fifty Opt-in members are enrolled; thirty-five of fifty Opt-out members are enrolled. No satisfaction, use or preference-strength measure has yet been collected. These are final states, not counts of affirmative choices.

The board must choose the initial setting for a future optional rollout. Its objective is service that members want, with manageable resources, not simply the highest enrolment total. The service is funded under either option, so an enrolment gap is not itself a financial return. Waiting for more preference evidence is possible.

## Round two

Twenty volunteers from the pilot, ten from each design, now complete a brief active-choice exercise for next month. Staff show the same service description and two unselected, equally prominent buttons: “Receive a folder” and “Do not receive a folder”. Their chosen setting will actually apply, with the same free reversal. All twenty voluntarily choose; participation is not a condition of membership.

Just before this exercise, eleven of these twenty were enrolled and nine were not. Among the eleven enrolled, five choose receive and six choose do not receive. Among the nine not enrolled, two choose receive and seven choose do not receive. Thus seven choose receive and thirteen choose do not receive. The original pilot totals still describe the earlier month; this is a subset follow-up, not a third independent group of twenty.

No price, service or wording changed, but the decision is now salient and occurs later, after experience of the pilot. The volunteers are self-selected; the exercise does not separate attention, learning, changing preferences, recommendation effects or reluctance to act earlier. It does not measure the strength of any preference. The board must not call active choices infallible measures of permanent wellbeing.

A third implementation is feasible: invite an explicit on/off choice at an ordinary member contact, without blocking access or repeatedly nagging. Members may skip; for someone without an affirmative instruction, the announced fallback is off. Existing explicit requests are respected, and no voluntary study answer authorises a different service. This invitation is therefore not literally a default-free system: nonresponse still has a stated outcome.

**Student question:** For the twenty volunteers, calculate the before-and-after enrolment shares and how many changed in each direction. What does this add to your default recommendation, and what evidence would you still need before claiming a welfare improvement?

## Answer the initial comparison

Opt-in enrolment is 15 / 50 = **30%**; Opt-out is 35 / 50 = **70%**. The observed gap is forty percentage points, or twenty additional enrolled members in the equally sized groups. It is not a forty-percent relative increase: 70 / 30 is about 2.33 times, though that ratio adds no causal identification.

Prefer Opt-in provisionally for this nonessential extra, while making the affirmative request easy and visible. This is a cautious judgment against supplying an unrequested extra, not proof that opt-in maximises welfare. Opt-out could spare action for members who genuinely want the service, while opt-in could leave willing members unserved. Because groups were not randomised, even the observed gap cannot be attributed entirely to the default.

## Answer the active-choice calculation

In the volunteer subset, 11 / 20 = **55%** were previously enrolled. After the exercise, 7 / 20 = **35%** choose enrolment: a twenty-percentage-point decline, or four fewer net enrolments. Six switch from on to off; two switch from off to on. Eight people change status, even though the net count changes by only four.

This shows that prior status and the later explicit choice differ for eight volunteers. It does not prove that six had always disliked the service, that all passive enrolment is mistaken, or that exactly 35% of all members want it. Experience, time, selection and the newly salient choice all matter. Nor can this subset’s 55% be compared with the whole Opt-out group’s 70% as if they were the same denominator.

## A choice process rather than an enrolment target

Use the **voluntary explicit-choice invitation with a disclosed off fallback** for new members without an instruction, while preserving existing explicit choices and easy changes. This brings the member’s current request into the process without making an optional service a gate to ordinary access. The fallback is a design choice and should be acknowledged, not hidden behind “no default”.

Provide equal prominence, plain terms, accessible assistance and no pressure. Do not infer that a free service is costless to attention or unwanted material. Equally, do not describe members who retain a default as irrational: they may like it, trust the cooperative, be indifferent or have more important uses for their time.

## What stronger evidence would look like

With appropriate voluntary participation and privacy protections, compare clearly specified designs on comparable groups, ideally with random assignment when suitable. Measure actual use, understanding, unwanted folders, ease of changing, reported usefulness and persistence of choices, not enrolment alone. Keep service terms and disclosure constant, and distinguish a short-term behaviour effect from durable benefit.

Ask why people choose either option without treating an answer as a diagnosis. An active choice reduces one ambiguity but can still be affected by framing, momentary attention or weak preferences. No single percentage supplies a monetary welfare value or a universal best default. Required consent must remain affirmative where applicable regardless of observed uptake.

## Instructor note

Ask students to separate four objects: the default, observed enrolment, explicit choice and welfare. Require percentage points and denominators. The active-choice follow-up is self-selected and later; do not let students casually treat it as a randomised causal test or a revelation of immutable true preference.

MKT-15 holds a benefit constant while changing its frame. Here information and terms are fixed while the no-action outcome changes. The exercise also differs from personal commitment devices: members are free to reverse their setting immediately, rather than deliberately restricting future options. Accept a different provisional default only with a clear objective, consent constraints and a plan to hear from members.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

All cooperative, service, pilot and follow-up details are original fiction. Optional free reading-folder service, no extra tracking, messages, fee or access condition. Same information and easy costless reversal across defaults; cognitive attention can still matter. Separate nonrandom fifty-member pilot groups yield 30%/70%, descriptive only. Twenty self-selected follow-up volunteers are a subset, with 11 previously on and 9 off;5 stay on,6 turn off,2 turn on,7 stay off. Seven enrol afterwards; eight switch, net decline four. Later explicit choices are consequential but not permanent welfare measures. A voluntary active-choice invitation retains an announced off fallback for nonresponse. Existing explicit instructions and all independent consent requirements remain binding.

# We trained them. Can they use it?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 365 -->

PPL-31 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Learning transfer · Skills utilisation · Supervisor support

![A fictional Hong Kong service-team supervisor and two colleagues compare a training checklist with an everyday booking screen in a modest office.](../trained-them-can-they-use-it-scene.jpg)

## A skill acquired is not necessarily a skill used

The OECD’s February 2026 report on skills use distinguishes proficiency from deployment at work. Using adult-skills survey data, it finds that higher proficiency does not necessarily accompany more frequent workplace use. This is a broad observational finding, not proof of what any particular training programme will achieve.

The Hong Kong team, records and budgets below are fictional teaching assumptions.

## Opening decision

You lead the six established booking coordinators at fictional Harbour Repairs, a Hong Kong furniture-repair service. They have completed a course on clarifying a customer’s requested repair before selecting a standard appointment type. The method adds two diagnostic questions and a short structured summary; it does not authorise technical advice or change escalation rules.

You must allocate next week’s development block: **W**, repeat the workshop using worked examples, or **P**, supervised application on ordinary eligible bookings. Either option uses 12 paid staff-hours in total and three supervisor-hours. Cover for normal duties is already arranged; neither option gets extra hours or unpaid preparation.

A recent desk review finds that the new method was documented in only two of 12 eligible bookings. Management calls this a motivation problem and asks for another workshop. You have not yet seen the class assessment or reviewed the workflow.

## Evidence

| Item | Supplied information |
| --- | --- |
| Established coordinators | 6; all attended the course |
| Eligible bookings reviewed | 12 |
| Bookings documenting the new method | 2 of 12 |
| Paid staff time available | 6 × 2 hours = 12 hours total |
| Supervisor time available | 3 hours total, separate from staff time |

Eligible means a routine booking for which the new clarification method is intended. Attendance is known; classroom proficiency, workplace permission and reasons for non-use are not yet supplied. The small desk review is not a representative performance estimate.

**Student question:** Would you provisionally allocate the block to W or P? What evidence must you check before treating two documented uses as a failure to learn or a lack of motivation?

## Round two

The assessment sheets now show that **all six coordinators correctly demonstrated the method** on two different classroom scenarios, without prompts. This supports acquisition in those scenarios, not mastery of every live booking.

The workflow review identifies two conflicts. The live screen’s standard template contains only an appointment code; the permitted notes box exists but has no prompt for the new summary. More importantly, the supervisor has continued to instruct staff to follow the old short script and omit the extra questions. The two documented uses occurred when a duty supervisor explicitly allowed the method. No evidence of refusal after permission is supplied.

You have authority to update this local script and its notes prompt without changing software, access rights, privacy safeguards or technical escalation. Within the same three supervisor-hours, allow one hour to prepare and explain the revised prompt and permission, then two hours to coach a rotating group. Each coordinator has two paid hours within the staggered block; eligible work is expected but its exact number is unknown. The remaining practice is ordinary work using the authorised prompt, not continuous one-to-one supervision.

**Student question:** Keep W, run P under the unchanged rules, or revise the conditions before P? Give a feasible use of the same hours and distinguish a measure of learning from a measure of transfer.

## Debrief · Diagnose the missing link

Round one does not establish why use is low. Attendance is not proficiency, and a low documented-use count is not a motivation diagnosis. A provisional choice is defensible only if accompanied by checks on demonstration, opportunity, permission and recording.

After the reveal, **revise the conditions before P**. Repeating content does not remove an instruction to omit the method. Practice under that same instruction is also incoherent. The supplied assessment makes a blanket refresher less well targeted, although later difficulties may justify focused instruction.

## Debrief · Change conditions within the block

Use one supervisor-hour to replace the conflicting local instruction, add the summary prompt to the existing permitted notes template, and explain when to use it. Use the other two supervisor-hours for rotating coaching during the scheduled practice. The six coordinators still receive two paid hours each: **12 staff-hours and three supervisor-hours**, not 15 staff-hours or three hours of individual coaching per person.

Keep technical escalation, privacy and access controls intact. Do not require the new questions on ineligible bookings or bypass a required safeguard. If an essential change lies outside the manager’s authority, seek the relevant approval rather than calling non-use resistance.

## Debrief · Measure application, not attendance

**Learning:** can each coordinator correctly perform the method on a fresh scenario without prompts? **Transfer:** on eligible live bookings, was the authorised method applied appropriately and its summary usable? Record eligible opportunities, attempts, correct use and remaining barriers separately; include quality and service-delay checks, not just a count of checked boxes.

The initial two of 12 records describe documented use, not two competent employees, a 17% learning score or proof that ten people refused. There are only six employees. Absence of a record is not always absence of behaviour. Later observation and record review should distinguish these.

Even improved use after the change would not isolate its causal effect: permission, prompts, coaching and case mix can change together. No productivity gain, customer benefit or financial return is supplied. The justified decision is to make application possible and evaluate it, not to promise that removing barriers guarantees success.

## Instructor note

Minutes 0–4: show the first-round brief and collect a provisional W/P vote. Ask what the two-of-12 denominator counts. Minutes 4–8: reveal the class demonstrations and conflicting instruction. Minutes 8–12: ask teams to allocate the fixed hours without inventing extra coaching capacity. Minutes 12–18: compare acquisition and transfer measures, then discuss attribution limits.

Ask students favouring another workshop which newly identified barrier it removes. Ask students favouring practice whether permission and workflow remain unchanged. Reward diagnosis rather than a universal claim that training is wasteful or supervisors are always responsible.

This case concerns established staff after training, not newcomer onboarding (PPL-04) or the specificity of feedback (PPL-21). Source studies motivate the distinction; they do not validate the fictional team’s figures. A separate instructor discussion can consider cases where proficiency has not been acquired and additional training is appropriate.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

All organisations, assessments, booking counts, time allocations and managerial permissions are fictional teaching assumptions. Six established employees each have two paid development hours; three separate supervisor-hours are fixed. Cover is supplied, and no additional staff time, continuous individual coaching or unapproved systems change is assumed. Classroom demonstrations establish performance only on the supplied scenarios. The twelve records are a small desk review, not a causal experiment or a measure of individual motivation. Future live opportunities and outcomes are unknown. No claimed productivity, financial or customer-service gain is drawn from the research.

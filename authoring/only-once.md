## In the news

On 14 July 2025, Hong Kong-listed Ascentage Pharma announced a conditional placing linked to a subscription of new shares, describing intended uses of funding and the enlarged share capital.

This was an announcement, not evidence of completion or returns. The product-photography studio below is unrelated and fictional.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. LightBench, a Hong Kong product-photography studio, is considering an expansion. Current owners collectively hold 100%. Existing services, wages, rent and required reserves remain funded and viable without expanding; the owners can decline both offers.

Phase 1 needs HK$400,000 for a complete tabletop-product service that is useful on its own. Optional Phase 2 adds a larger-object service for another HK$600,000. Both budgets include all relevant setup, staffing, operating support and reserves for the stated expansion horizon.

Two illustrative new-share offers are available before commitment. Funds enter the company, not the owners’ pockets. No profit or future company-value forecast is supplied.

## Evidence

| Offer now | Company funding | New investor / original owners after issue | Budget covered |
|---|---:|---|---|
| A · First phase only | HK$400,000 | 20% / 80% | Phase 1 |
| B · Both phases funded | HK$1,000,000 | 30% / 70% | Phases 1 and 2 |

All percentages are of the whole company immediately after the relevant issue. A needs a later financing decision if Phase 2 is chosen. B permits pausing Phase 2 and keeping unused funds in the company, but pausing spending does not reverse shares already issued.

The shares have the same ordinary economic and voting rights. There are no special vetoes, preferences, options, convertibles, anti-dilution protections or extra subscriptions; issuance costs are separately funded equally. Actual terms and governance would need professional review before commitment.

**Student question:** Which route would you provisionally prefer, and why? Does 20% now establish that A preserves more ownership through the whole expansion? Name future-funding evidence that could change your answer.

## Round two

The team adds a conditional financing scenario to today’s planning sheet. If it chooses A, completes Phase 1 and then elects Phase 2, a second investor would supply HK$600,000 for 20% of the whole company immediately after that second share issue.

Use those terms for this classroom comparison only. They do not guarantee that a future investor, valuation or agreement will be available, and no successful first phase has occurred in the story.

The original owners and first investor receive no special protection and make no extra investment. Both existing holdings are diluted by the new shares. They can instead stop after Phase 1; B also permits pausing operations, but its issued ownership stakes remain.

**Student question:** If both rounds occur on those terms, what do the original owners, first investor and second investor each own? Compare A’s completed funding path with B. Would you revise your choice, and what remains unknown?

## The second 20% uses a new denominator

After A’s first issue, original owners hold 80% and the first investor 20%. The second investor then receives 20% of the enlarged company, leaving all earlier shareholders with 80% together.

Original owners retain 80% × 80% = 64%. The first investor retains 20% × 80% = 16%. The second investor holds 20%. Together they hold 100%.

Cumulative original-owner dilution is 36%, not 40%. The two 20% stakes were measured against different totals.

## Compare completed financing paths, not just the first offer

If both A rounds occur, the company raises HK$400,000 + HK$600,000 = HK$1,000,000 and original owners retain 64%. B raises HK$1,000,000 now and leaves them with 70%, six percentage points more.

On the supplied full-expansion scenario, B therefore requires less cumulative dilution and provides the funding earlier. This is a conditional ownership comparison, not proof that B creates more value.

The HK$1,000,000 raised along A is cumulative funding, not closing cash: money may be spent between rounds.

## A pause in spending is not a reversal of financing

A can stop after its useful first phase with original owners at 80%, having raised HK$400,000. That is a smaller funded scope than B, so the percentages alone are not a like-for-like value comparison.

B also allows the team to pause Phase 2. Unspent funds remain company assets; they are not a personal windfall or freely withdrawable owner money. Its 30% investor stake is not automatically cancelled because spending pauses.

A can be defended when limiting today’s equity commitment matters or credible later terms would differ. B can be defended when securing both budgets now is the priority. Neither forces unwanted or unsafe expansion spending.

## Ask what the ownership percentage cannot tell you

No probabilities, profits or exit values are supplied. A larger percentage of a different future company need not produce more money. Nor does an ordinary voting majority answer every board or legal question.

Compare actual financing terms, rights and business needs before commitment. Negotiation is possible, but a new cash amount at the same percentage is not an offered free alternative.

## Instructor note

Require a three-holder ownership table, not just an original-owner answer. The first investor is diluted too. Ask students to reconcile 64% + 16% + 20% = 100% and distinguish six percentage points from a percentage change.

Keep the reveal conditional. A planned second round is not guaranteed finance or evidence that Phase 1 succeeded. Both routes allow an operating pause; only A avoids issuing the second round’s shares if it never occurs.

Optional extension: if the second investor receives fraction x, owners retain 0.8(1 − x). This equals 70% at x = 12.5%. It is a conditional comparison, not a prediction that those terms can be obtained. Do not add valuation appraisal, investment recommendations or forced-spending claims.

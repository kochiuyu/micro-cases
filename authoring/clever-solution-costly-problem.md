## Discover the problem before polishing the answer

The US National Science Foundation describes customer discovery in its I-Corps programme as conversations with potential customers, partners and other stakeholders, rather than simply preparing a presentation. This is a programme description, not proof that interviewing guarantees a successful venture.

The founder, gadget, studio owners, time allowances and research accounts below are fictional. The decision is which uncertainty deserves the next few hours, not whether a real product has succeeded.

## Opening decision

**Fictional classroom situation.** A Hong Kong founder has made a safe, non-connected tabletop mock-up: a small dial and coloured windows showing which craft-workshop slots are free. She imagines selling a finished scheduling gadget to independent studios running classes for adults. It has no real bookings or customer data. No product has been launched, paid for or promised.

Two studio owners have said that checking availability can be annoying. They liked the appearance, but have not tried the mock-up in their work or described the last time the problem occurred. The founder can already arrange either activity below with willing owners from the same studio type. Access, preparation and compensation costs are funded; six founder hours are available before she chooses the next research task. She cannot complete both six-hour routes now.

A clearer demonstration could reveal whether people understand the proposed interaction; it is not merely a prettier sales pitch. Investigating workarounds could reveal whether the task matters enough to pursue, but would delay that usability feedback. Neither route commits her to manufacture, a new customer group or a particular solution. Use consented, anonymised descriptions or synthetic examples, not participant names or live booking records.

## Evidence

| Six-hour route | What it can teach | What it postpones |
|---|---|---|
| D · Demonstration | 3 hours improving the mock-up; 2 hours of guided try-outs; 1 hour reviewing observations | Learning the wider work surrounding the task |
| W · Workarounds | 1 hour preparing; 3 hours of recent-event interviews; 2 hours comparing accounts | Learning whether the mock-up is understandable |

**Student question:** Would you spend the next six founder hours improving and testing the demonstration, or investigating current workarounds? State the uncertainty your choice would reduce and what it would leave unanswered.

## Round two

Before either route starts, three willing owners from this same studio type send short, anonymised accounts of the previous week. These are exploratory contacts, not a random sample. The founder has not yet spent the six-hour allowance. Each owner distinguishes active work checking free slots from active work reconciling participant counts for preparing craft-material packs; the categories do not overlap.

| Owner account · previous week | Checking which slots were free | Reconciling participant counts for materials |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 6 active minutes | 45 active minutes |
| B | 8 active minutes | 60 active minutes |
| C | 4 active minutes | 30 active minutes |

These are owners’ reconstructed estimates, not stopwatch measurements or representative averages. All three check a paper planner for availability and call that mildly irritating. For materials, they compare bookings, cancellations and late changes across messages and a list. They describe repeated corrections to pack counts before a workshop. No verified loss, missed class or cash saving is supplied. The mock-up only displays free slots: it does not reconcile participant counts.

All three are willing to discuss the recent sequence in more detail. No one has agreed to pay for either problem to be solved. You may redirect the same six hours toward understanding material-count reconciliation, retain the availability focus, or investigate why the accounts differ. This is a research choice, not permission to build a replacement product.

**Student question:** After the new accounts, which problem would you investigate next? Write a solution-neutral problem statement, one question about a recent event, and one finding that would weaken your chosen direction.

## Which uncertainty comes first?

D tests the legibility of a proposed interaction. W tests the founder’s description of the work worth improving. Both can produce useful information, but answering “can you understand this?” does not answer “does this solve a consequential problem?” The initial compliments leave problem importance particularly uncertain. W is a defensible priority; D is defensible if the founder explains why interaction feedback is needed before further investigation.

## Read the numbers without inventing a market

Within these accounts, availability checking totals 18 active minutes and material-count reconciliation totals 135. The difference is 117 minutes across three owners’ reported weeks, not 117 minutes the gadget can save. The accounts do not establish frequency in other weeks, market prevalence, avoidable effort or willingness to pay. Summing unlike businesses is only a compact description of these accounts, not a forecast.

The adjacent task deserves attention because its reported effort and the described repeated corrections align. Yet a larger time figure does not automatically identify the better venture. Owners may tolerate it, an existing tool may solve it cheaply, or the proposed intervention may create more work. Keep the original availability hypothesis available for comparison.

## Name an outcome, not a device

A possible revised statement is: “Owners preparing adult craft workshops need a dependable participant count at the materials deadline so that they can prepare the appropriate number of packs without repeatedly reconciling changes.” This is a tentative interpretation of three accounts. It names a user, situation and desired outcome without prescribing an app, dial, automation or subscription.

“Studios need our scheduling gadget” embeds the answer in the problem. “Studios need efficiency” is too broad to guide inquiry. Keep the same studio type while investigating a different task; this is not yet a segment pivot or a new product decision.

## Use the next six hours to challenge the framing

One feasible research plan uses one hour to prepare neutral questions, three hours for three one-hour conversations, and two hours to compare the accounts. Ask each owner to reconstruct the last material-count change: what happened, which steps followed, what was already recorded and what remained uncertain? Seek the last smooth workshop too, not only a frustrating example. Obtain consent and use anonymised summaries rather than collecting attendee records.

Separate what the owner reports from what an example demonstrates and from the founder’s interpretation. A small number of long conversations can expose a task worth examining; it cannot measure population demand. If the apparent reconciliation burden was an unusual one-off event, or the owners show a reliable existing process requiring little effort, weaken or revise this framing instead of designing around it. Next, investigate recurrence, alternatives and who would choose and pay for an improvement before proposing a commercial test.

## Instructor note

Allow 3 minutes for the first choice, 3 for the reveal and arithmetic, and 5 for pairs to write and challenge a problem statement. Ask: what does a good demonstration prove? Does more reported time imply more recoverable value? Can the proposed statement be met in more than one way? Accept a reasoned continued availability investigation; reject a confident new-product launch based on three accounts. This case concerns problem formulation before product validation, not market sizing, pricing, a segment pivot or an experiment estimating causal effects.

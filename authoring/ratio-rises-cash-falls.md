# A healthier ratio. A healthier business?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 349 -->

ACC-30 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Current ratio · Payment timing · Liquidity interpretation

![A controller compares two subsidiary balance-sheet folders beside a payment calendar.](../ratio-rises-cash-falls-scene.jpg)

## The reporting question

Liquidity ratios compare balance-sheet amounts at a point in time. ACCA and OpenStax explain that interpreting them requires attention to cash flows, asset composition and context, not simply a preference for a larger number. Paying a recognised payable changes both sides of the current ratio.

All entities, balances and payment terms below are fictional. Both payment dates are lawful and fully disclosed. The exercise is about explaining a ratio, not hiding obligations, delaying overdue payments or engineering a misleading year-end picture.

## Opening decision

A Hong Kong group controller reviews subsidiary Harbour’s supplier payment. Its HK$20,000 payable is already recognised within current liabilities. The written contract expressly permits settlement on either 30 December or 2 January at the same amount; neither is overdue. The supplier has confirmed that either date is acceptable, with no relationship or supply consequences. The established treasury schedule is 2 January. A colleague proposes 30 December because it would “make the year-end current ratio healthier”.

Immediately before the proposed December payment, Harbour has HK$150,000 current assets, including HK$50,000 unrestricted cash, and HK$100,000 current liabilities. These are the projected 31 December balances if payment remains on 2 January. All other balances stay fixed between the two comparison snapshots. Paying HK$20,000 reduces cash and the recognised payable by exactly that amount; it is not a new expense. Define current ratio as current assets divided by current liabilities, and net working capital as current assets minus current liabilities.

The two dates have no discounts, fees, interest, tax, foreign-exchange or profit effects. The approved cash forecast shows either date meets all obligations and the operating cash minimum. There is no covenant, bonus or legal threshold tied to the ratio. Payment timing and both pro forma snapshots will be disclosed to the board. Compare the dates and decide whether the ratio alone justifies changing the established schedule. Do not execute a payment.

## Round two

The controller checks a second subsidiary, Cove, under the same payment terms and operational assumptions. Its projected 31 December balances before a separate HK$20,000 supplier payment are current assets HK$80,000, including unrestricted cash HK$30,000, and current liabilities HK$100,000. This payable is already included. Cove’s established payment date is also 2 January; either permitted date meets its cash forecast and operating minimum. No balances are transferred between subsidiaries.

Apply the same HK$20,000 cash settlement to both subsidiaries independently. Keep the payment purpose, terms and accounting treatment unchanged. The board now asks whether reducing current liabilities necessarily improves the current ratio, and whether the ratio’s direction establishes a gain or loss in economic health. Report both entities without netting their balances or withholding the less flattering result.

**Student question:** Why does the same payment move the two subsidiaries’ current ratios in opposite directions, and what should the controller say about economic health and payment scheduling?

## First answer: Harbour’s snapshots

If payment stays on 2 January, the 31 December current ratio is 150,000/100,000 = 1.50; cash is HK$50,000 and net working capital HK$50,000. If paid on 30 December, current assets become HK$130,000 and current liabilities HK$80,000: the ratio is 1.625. Cash falls to HK$30,000. Net working capital remains 130,000 − 80,000 = HK$50,000.

The larger ratio does not create cash or profit. HK$20,000 of available cash has settled HK$20,000 of an existing obligation. With all other benefits and costs explicitly held equal, the ratio alone is not a reason to abandon the established 2 January schedule. Either date is permitted, but any choice should follow the cash plan and commercial terms rather than a cosmetic ratio target.

## Second answer: Cove’s snapshots

If paid on 2 January, Cove’s year-end ratio is 80,000/100,000 = 0.80; cash is HK$30,000 and net working capital negative HK$20,000. If paid on 30 December, current assets fall to HK$60,000 and current liabilities to HK$80,000. The ratio becomes 0.75 and cash HK$10,000. Net working capital is still negative HK$20,000. The same legitimate settlement lowers this ratio.

## The denominator explains the direction

Subtracting the same positive payment from numerator and denominator moves a ratio away from one, provided the remaining denominator is positive. If current assets exceed current liabilities, the ratio rises; if assets are smaller, it falls; if equal, it stays one. In both subsidiaries, the absolute difference between current assets and current liabilities is unchanged. The percentage coverage changes because that same difference is measured against fewer remaining liabilities.

For current assets A, current liabilities L and payment p with 0 < p < L, the change is (A − p)/(L − p) − A/L = p(A − L)/[L(L − p)]. Its sign follows A − L. This algebra explains the snapshots; it is not a policy to make ratios look better.

## Explain the economic boundary

Both entities have less cash and less debt to suppliers after payment. Neither earns profit, receives new funding or improves its net working-capital amount from the settlement itself. The rise in Harbour and fall in Cove do not, on their own, prove improved or worsened overall health. Asset convertibility, remaining due dates and future cash flows still matter. A ratio below one deserves contextual examination, not an automatic conclusion of insolvency.

Retain the established schedule absent a genuine commercial or cash-management reason to change it, while ensuring actual settlement remains within agreed terms. Present the original and payment-adjusted snapshots and the date assumption together. A payment in January also reduces cash and the payable then; it does not extinguish the obligation at December year-end.

## Instructor note

Calculate Harbour first and ask whether “healthier” describes more cash, more profit or only a higher fraction. Reveal Cove and require the same journal logic before calculating its ratio. Check that students neither subtract the payment twice nor omit it from current assets. Use the algebra only after the two numerical examples.

The problem differs from a profit-to-cash bridge and discretionary distributions. No liability is reclassified as non-current merely because payment is in January. The supplied classifications and permitted dates remove legal ambiguity; external standards provide background, not a full reporting opinion. Both pro forma results must be disclosed, and no selective presentation or late payment is endorsed.

Explains balance-sheet snapshots, cash-flow statements and working capital. US disclosure rules are not asserted as obligations for these fictional subsidiaries.
- [OpenStax, Principles of Finance, 19.1 What Is Working Capital?](https://openstax.org/books/principles-finance/pages/19-1-what-is-working-capital). Original textbook discussion distinguishes relative liquidity measures from a future cash-flow forecast and notes asset-composition limits. Numerical examples are independently constructed.
- [New Zealand XRB, NZ IAS 1: current assets and liabilities](https://standards.xrb.govt.nz/standards-navigator/nz-ias-1/). Paragraphs 66–76A provide classification and settlement context. A January payment does not itself make a payable non-current; the case supplies its classifications rather than importing foreign law.
- [ACCA, Assessing organisation performance, adapted from Bobbie Retallack](https://www.accaglobal.com/gb/en/student/exam-support-resources/professional-exams-study-resources/strategic-business-leader/technical-articles/performance-appraisal.html).

# A tax on the seller. Who pays in the end?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 395 -->

ECO-33 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Tax incidence · Competitive equilibrium · Relative elasticity

![Two fictional adult economics analysts compare plain buyer-price and seller-receipt cards beside identical empty drink containers and two simple market worksheets.](../seller-tax-who-bears-it-scene.jpg)

## Remitting is not the same as bearing

Tax incidence asks who bears a tax after prices and trading adjust, rather than only who sends the payment to government. Competitive-market teaching separates the buyer’s total price from the seller’s receipt after tax. The two markets, schedules and tax below are invented, not a description of any Hong Kong policy.

## Opening decision

You prepare a classroom briefing about an invented Hong Kong market for identical empty drink containers. Many buyers and sellers are price takers. There is no platform, market power, price control, import trade or collusion. Compare one day’s equilibrium before and after a hypothetical HK$4 tax on every container sold.

The stipulated rule requires sellers to remit the full HK$4 per sale. There is full compliance, no exemptions, administration cost or change in product quality. Buyers know the complete price before purchasing. The tax creates no separate information or attention effect in this model.

Let Q be containers traded per day, P_b the total HK dollars paid by a buyer per container, and P_s the HK dollars retained by its seller after remitting tax, before production costs. Without tax, P_b equals P_s. With tax, P_b = P_s + 4. The tax is a fixed amount, not 4%.

Demand is P_b = 20 − 0.1Q. Market A supply is P_s = 4 + 0.1Q. These are inverse schedules: they give willingness to pay or required net receipt at each quantity. Equilibrium requires the same Q on both schedules and the stated price wedge.

Treat the schedules as exact assumptions over the relevant range of 50–90 containers daily, with no other shifts. They are not survey estimates or forecasts. Quantity may adjust freely within that range; all solutions here are whole containers. There is no rationing or fixed stock constraint.

A colleague proposes the headline “Sellers pay the tax, so buyers pay nothing.” Decide whether to use it. Find the untaxed equilibrium first, then solve the taxed equilibrium. Measure each side’s per-container price burden against the common untaxed price, not against an unchanged-price assumption.

## Evidence

| Relationship | Equation |
| --- | --- |
| Buyer demand | P_b = 20 − 0.1Q |
| Seller supply | P_s = 4 + 0.1Q |
| Tax wedge | P_b − P_s = HK$4 |

**Student question:** Would you approve the proposed headline? Calculate Market A’s untaxed and taxed quantities, buyer price and seller net receipt, then divide the HK$4 per-container burden between the two sides.

## Round two

Now compare Market B, a separate hypothetical market with the same demand, container quality and HK$4 seller-remitted tax. Its supply is P_s = −12 + 0.3Q over the same relevant range. All other assumptions remain unchanged. This is an alternative supply environment, not a second tax imposed on Market A.

At the common untaxed equilibrium, B’s sellers change quantity less in response to a given change in net price. That is lower supply responsiveness, not evidence that sellers are poorer or behaving improperly. The negative intercept lies outside the stated range; do not interpret it as an actual payment to dispose of containers.

Recalculate equilibrium and tax incidence in B. For each market also reconcile buyers’ total spending, sellers’ total receipts after tax and government revenue. No use of the tax revenue, environmental benefit, household incomes or downstream wage effects is supplied, so do not rank fairness or overall social desirability.

**Student question:** Why does the burden split change in Market B even though sellers still remit every tax payment? Verify both markets’ price wedges and cash totals, then write a defensible replacement headline.

## Debrief · Establish the common starting point

Without tax in A, 20 − 0.1Q = 4 + 0.1Q, so 16 = 0.2Q and Q = 80. Both prices are HK$12. In B, 20 − 0.1Q = −12 + 0.3Q gives 32 = 0.4Q, again Q = 80 and price HK$12.

## Debrief · Insert the wedge in A

With tax in A, 20 − 0.1Q = 4 + 0.1Q + 4. Thus 12 = 0.2Q and Q = 60. Buyers pay HK$14; sellers retain HK$10 after remitting HK$4. Demand and supply both support 60 at those different prices.

Relative to HK$12, buyers pay HK$2 more and sellers retain HK$2 less per container. Each bears half the HK$4 price wedge. The statutory payment is all made by sellers, but the per-unit economic price burden is shared. The proposed headline is false in this model.

## Debrief · Solve B, do not reuse A’s split

With tax in B, 20 − 0.1Q = −12 + 0.3Q + 4. Thus 28 = 0.4Q and Q = 70. Buyers pay HK$13 and sellers retain HK$9. The HK$4 wedge now comprises a HK$1 buyer-price rise and a HK$3 seller-receipt fall.

Buyers bear 25% and sellers 75% of that per-unit wedge in B. Both sides still bear a strictly positive part: 0 < buyer price rise < HK$4 and 0 < seller net-price fall < HK$4 in both markets. Seller remittance alone predicts neither the equal split nor the unequal one.

## Debrief · Compare responsiveness on a common basis

Demand falls ten containers for each HK$1 increase in buyer price. A supply rises ten for each HK$1 increase in seller net price; B supply rises only 10/3. Because both markets start at price12 and quantity80, this smaller quantity response also means a smaller initial supply elasticity.

For those using elasticity, multiply the quantity response per HK$1 by initial price divided by quantity: demand’s absolute elasticity is 10 × 12/80 = 1.5. A supply elasticity is also 1.5; B is (10/3) × 12/80 = 0.5. These are point elasticities at the common untaxed equilibrium, not constant elasticities along each line.

B’s relatively less responsive suppliers bear more through a lower net price, while quantity falls only ten rather than twenty. The equations establish that outcome here. Do not infer a universal incidence percentage from a graph’s visual steepness when axes, units, prices or quantities differ.

## Debrief · Trace the cash once

In A, buyer spending is 60 × HK$14 = HK$840. Sellers retain 60 × HK$10 = HK$600, while government receives 60 × HK$4 = HK$240. In B, the respective totals are HK$910, HK$630 and HK$280. In each market, buyer spending equals seller net receipts plus tax revenue.

The government taxes actual sales, not the untaxed 80 containers. Neither HK$600 nor HK$630 is seller profit: production costs remain to be deducted. 

The price burden times traded quantity allocates the tax-revenue rectangle between buyers and sellers. Revenue is a transfer to government, not itself deadweight loss; foregone mutually beneficial trades create the latter.

## Debrief · State the conclusion narrowly

A defensible headline is: “Sellers remit the tax; market responses determine how its price burden is shared.” In A the buyer and seller price changes are equal; in B sellers bear more. No seller coordination or unilateral promise of full pass-through is needed to obtain these competitive equilibria.

Within the same fully informed competitive model, changing only the side required to remit the identical per-unit tax would leave the same wedge and equilibrium. Actual markets may have different enforcement, salience, contracts or market power. This is not a claim that every real tax or fee has identical effects regardless of design.

# The score flagged the refund. Who can review it?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 153 -->

GVR-13 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Contestability · Human review · Procedural fairness

![Two retail colleagues discuss a plain return parcel and a tablet at a household-goods service counter.](../flag-and-review-scene.jpg)

## A Hong Kong governance context

In a May 2025 paper for the Legislative Council, the Privacy Commissioner’s Office described its AI guidance, including proportionate human oversight and communication with affected stakeholders. These are governance recommendations, not a promise that adding a person makes every decision fair.

The retailer, system and customer below are fictional. No real retailer is accused, and the case does not state a universal legal right to a particular appeal process.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong household-goods retailer uses an AI-assisted score to flag unusual refund requests. A flag sends a request to a person; it must not automatically deny a refund or label the customer dishonest. A customer’s request has just been flagged, and the manager must select its review route.

A central specialist can review remotely within one working day, using transaction records and material the customer submits. Alternatively, a trained store reviewer can hold a conversation and inspect the item within two working days, in person or by video. Both have authority to reach a different conclusion from the score and apply the same published return policy.

The retailer has funded equal capacity for either route, not both complete reviews. The customer can use either format but would prefer a quick answer. The retailer preserves the original request date and all existing rights; any legally required faster remedy takes priority. A supervisor can handle a further challenge under either route. No final decision has been made.

## Evidence

| Review route | Advantage to test | Limitation to manage |
|---|---|---|
| A: central specialist, one working day | Consistent access to transaction history | Customer must be able to add missing context remotely |
| B: store conversation, two working days | Discuss and inspect the item with the customer | Extra delay and access to the reasons behind the flag |

**Student question:** Which review route would you choose, A or B? Explain what the reviewer must be able to hear, check and change, rather than treating a human signature as enough.

## Round two

The flag’s main recorded factor is several returns attached to the account in a short period. The customer says these were replacements for one damaged delivery, not unrelated purchases, and offers the delivery note and photographs. The scoring inputs record return dates and counts but not the reason for a replacement. The supporting material has not yet been checked.

Store staff currently see only “manual review required”. The central specialist can see the transaction sequence and relevant reason category. An authorised case summary can now be shared securely with either reviewer without exposing detection thresholds, other customers’ information or source code. Both routes can accept and verify the customer’s documents. A can ask follow-up questions remotely; B can examine the item and discuss the sequence.

The missing context might change the interpretation, but it does not establish that the refund is owed or that the score malfunctioned. The manager must choose the route and an understandable explanation of what is being checked. The one- and two-day timings and further supervisor review remain available.

**Student question:** Would you keep or change your review route? Draft a short explanation of the flag and specify how the customer can correct missing information and obtain a genuinely reconsidered decision.

## A flag is not a verdict

The system noticed a pattern in the fields it receives. It did not examine the replacement history now offered by the customer. Separate why the request was selected for attention from the policy reasons for eventually accepting or refusing it. An anomaly is not evidence of dishonesty.

## Make the challenge usable

A useful message identifies the relevant concern, invites the delivery record and explains who will examine it and when. For example: “Several returns appear on this account. We are checking whether they concern replacements from the same delivery. Please send the delivery note or discuss another way to provide it. A reviewer will consider this alongside our records.”

This is not a promise to approve. The final response should explain the policy applied, the evidence considered and how to request supervisor reconsideration. Avoid asking for irrelevant personal information.

## Choose the route, not a rubber stamp

A is defensible when documents and remote questions can establish the sequence, especially given the preference for speed. B is defensible when inspection or conversation is important enough to justify the extra day. Neither route works merely because a person is present: the person needs the relevant records, time and authority to disagree.

The authorised summary removes an information barrier without disclosing exploitable thresholds. Record what was corrected, why the decision was reached and whether similar missing-context cases need a system or process review. Do not assume one corrected case proves a population-wide error rate.

## Instructor note

Ask students for a route, a customer-facing explanation and a correction mechanism. These are three different outputs. Do not award a refund simply because a customer offers an explanation; require proportionate verification under the stated policy. Equally, repeating the score is not reconsideration.

IS-02 asks which automated replies an assistant may send. This case concerns the affected customer’s practical ability to challenge evidence used in their own treatment. GVR-08 concerns a reporting channel for misconduct, not a refund review. Foreign guidance supplies process concepts, not Hong Kong statutory advice or a new appeal entitlement.

# One company. Two identities.

<!-- legacy-case-id: 401 -->

PPL-34 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Post-merger identity · Status and recognition · Employee voice

![A fictional Hong Kong services manager listens to adult colleagues from two acquired teams around a shared office table, with two old unbranded name cards beside one new shared card.](../one-company-two-identities-scene.jpg)

## Integration is not only a systems question

After an acquisition, compatible systems do not necessarily create a shared sense of belonging. Research on merger identities and practical employee-voice guidance inform this fictional discussion. They do not establish that keeping or removing an old team name will reliably produce acceptance.

## Opening decision

You manage fictional Harbour Common, a Hong Kong services company formed after Harbour acquired Cove. The workforce includes 40 former Harbour colleagues, 20 former Cove colleagues and newer hires. Leadership has already settled the legal employer, customer-facing name, reporting lines and shared operating systems. Those arrangements work and are not being reopened.

Choose how internal team introductions and the staff directory describe people for the next six weeks. Option A uses Harbour Common only from Monday. Option B uses Harbour Common with an optional Harbour or Cove legacy descriptor during the transition. Neither option changes roles, pay, benefits, promotion criteria or job security.

Both options require the same shared client-service goals and mixed-team work. Neither permits separate procedures or preferential treatment by origin. There are no redundancies or employment-contract changes in this exercise. Costs and delivery capacity are equivalent, so this is not a cost-saving or technical-integration decision.

A offers one clear internal identity immediately and avoids formally prolonging an old boundary. B recognises existing attachments while people develop a shared identity, but may keep divisions visible. Leadership can revise internal wording after six weeks; employees cannot be required to feel proud or to abandon their personal histories.

## Round two

Private exploratory conversations with six volunteers from each legacy group reveal conflicting meanings. Some Cove colleagues hear immediate removal of their name as erasing a valued history. Some Harbour colleagues hear continued legacy labels as evidence that equal commitment is being postponed. These are reported concerns, not verified intentions of either group.

The 12 volunteers were not randomly selected. The notes do not say how many hold each view, and opinions vary within both groups. No vote, representative approval rate or effect on performance can be calculated. Staff hired after the acquisition have not yet been consulted; neither legacy label necessarily fits them.

The director confirms that common service goals and the customer-facing name stay fixed. Internal wording and recognition of both histories remain open. Paid listening sessions and a private feedback route are available to everyone, including newer staff. The manager must explain what feedback changed and what it did not change.

**Student question:** Keep or revise your choice. Propose a fair six-week process that addresses both concerns without reopening operating systems, treating silence as agreement or promising that everyone will identify with the new company.

## Debrief · Diagnose the meaning

The disagreement concerns what membership and recognition mean, not whether staff can use the same software. A common name may signal equal membership to one person and loss of status to another. Continuing a legacy name may signal respect to one person and separation to another. Neither interpretation proves disloyalty.

Do not treat Harbour and Cove as two unanimous camps. The groups differ in size, but a larger headcount does not make its interpretation inherently fairer. Newer staff and people who dislike either legacy label also need a place in the shared identity.

## Debrief · A defensible provisional choice

B is a defensible starting proposal if descriptors remain optional and both histories receive equal recognition. Explain that common responsibilities already apply, that legacy references do not confer privileges, and that newer staff may use Harbour Common alone. A transition recognises attachments; it does not establish two classes of membership.

A can also be defended if listening shows optional labels would harden boundaries and meaningful recognition is available without them. For example, a shared history display can credit both teams. The interviews do not identify a universally correct label. State the risk your choice accepts and how you will notice harm.

## Debrief · Give voice a real purpose

Invite people across origins, roles and tenure to discuss specific wording and recognition proposals during paid time. Offer private input alongside group discussion so speaking publicly is not the only route. Explain confidentiality limits, prohibit retaliation for respectful disagreement, and avoid publishing small-group comments that could identify a speaker.

Before listening, state the fixed decisions and the choices that remain open. Afterwards, publish a short response explaining proposals accepted, rejected or still under consideration and why. Consultation is not a promise that every preference wins, and a completed meeting is not evidence that employees feel heard.

## Debrief · Review without a loyalty test

At six weeks, ask whether people understand the common responsibilities, feel their previous contributions are recognised, and can work across origins without unequal treatment. Examine concrete incidents and anonymous feedback where anonymity is feasible. Compare experiences across groups cautiously rather than ranking employees by attachment to a name.

Use the review to retain, revise or retire the temporary wording with reasons. Do not require a positive identity survey score or a pledge of enthusiasm. Smooth service delivery cannot alone prove belonging, and discomfort cannot alone prove operational failure. Keep ordinary fair-treatment and grievance routes available.

## Instructor note

Minutes 0–4: choose a provisional label policy. Minutes 4–8: interpret the interview reveal without inventing vote totals. Minutes 8–13: design a bounded, inclusive listening process. Minutes 13–18: defend a review criterion that tests treatment and understanding rather than demanding loyalty.

STR-27 chooses which operating activities to integrate after an acquisition. Here the systems and operating choices are already settled. The distinctive issue is continuity, recognition and perceived status in a shared identity, with neither immediate uniformity nor prolonged legacy labels guaranteed to create acceptance.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

Original fictional company, workforce, interviews, options and six-week review. Operational integration is settled and compatible. Labels concern internal introductions and directory descriptions only. Equal employment terms, no redundancies or contractual changes, and equivalent cost/capacity are stipulated. Twelve volunteer conversations are exploratory, not representative; no count supports either view. Identity acceptance, performance improvement and consensus are not guaranteed. Guidance informs process choices rather than establishing local legal duties. Research settings and sample findings are not transferred as facts about this company.

- [Schönian · Rethinking the Role of Organizational Identities · 2026](https://storage.imrpress.com/IMR/2033438892988928000/application/1861-9908-37-1-39442.pdf). Original interpretive study of identity work in an Austrian telecom merger. Supports attention to varied meanings and power; its single-case findings do not predict the effects of these fictional labels.
- [Frontiers in Human Dynamics · Nonprofit post-merger identification · 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-dynamics/articles/10.3389/fhumd.2022.933460/full). Original single-organisation mixed-method study, including a cross-sectional staff survey. Relationships between identification and integration experience are not causal proof or a transferable success threshold.
- [Acas · Consulting employees](https://www.acas.org.uk/consulting-employees). Primary guidance on genuine listening and explaining decisions. Used for process ideas, not as a statement of Hong Kong legal consultation duties.
- [CIPD · Employee voice · 2026](https://www.cipd.org/uk/knowledge/factsheets/voice-factsheet/). Institutional guidance on complementary voice channels and responding to input. Not evidence that a particular merger name will improve performance.
- [Hong Kong Labour Department · Good People Management Practices · June 2019 revision](https://www.labour.gov.hk/eng/public/wcp/practice.pdf). Section VI describes clear, regular two-way communication and responding to staff views. Practical context, not a legal determination or guarantee of acceptance.

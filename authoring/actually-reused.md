# Reusable fifty times. Used how many?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 49 -->

SUS-05 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Sustainability · Reuse systems · Life-cycle thinking

![Two market organisers discuss reusable cups packed in transport crates beneath a market awning.](../actually-reused-scene.jpg)

## In the news

On 24 March 2025, Hong Kong’s Leisure and Cultural Services Department reported that the Flower Show had offered a free tableware rental service among its waste-reduction measures.

That establishes a real local example, not a measured comparison of every environmental impact. The organiser and alternatives below are fictional.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. A small Hong Kong market organiser is arranging four weekend markets. Each market needs 500 drink servings in cups of the same capacity and appropriate food-safety standard.

Buy: 500 newly made custom-printed reusable cups, specified by the supplier as suitable for at least 50 uses. The organiser keeps them.

Rent: 500 plain reusable cups per market from an existing shared pool. The service collects, washes and stores them between bookings.

Both complete quotations fit the approved budget and include appropriate cleaning during the four markets. Buying offers branding and ownership; renting offers a managed return service. The organiser wants a workable, lower-impact system, not merely a reusable-looking purchase.

## Evidence

Both plans provide the same capacity and food-safety standard, fit the budget and cover appropriate cleaning for the four markets. No comparable life-cycle assessment is supplied.

**Student question:** Would you buy the custom cups or rent from the shared pool? What would make your choice environmentally preferable?

## Round two

The purchased cups are planned to be used once per cup at each of the four markets. Assume all 500 are returned and remain usable through those markets. Beyond them, the organiser has no scheduled event, receiving organisation, named storage owner or funded storage plan.

The supplier’s “50 uses” is a durability specification, not a forecast of actual circulation.

The rental service specifies collection, washing and return to its shared stock. It offers records of issues, returns and subsequent circulation, but these have not yet been checked. Neither plan has supplied a comparable assessment of manufacture, cleaning, transport, losses and end-of-life impacts.

**Student question:** Would you keep or change your choice? Identify the missing operating plan, and explain what evidence you need before ranking the environmental impacts.

## Count useful service, not capacity

The purchased batch would provide 500 × 4 = 2,000 planned servings: four uses per cup, not fifty. Future uses are possible but are not yet organised or guaranteed.

A durable object only creates repeated service if somebody arranges returns, safe cleaning, storage and the next user. Do not skip necessary hygiene to improve an environmental calculation.

## Choose a system, with conditions

Renting is a defensible provisional choice because the quoted service provides an operating loop beyond one owner’s four events. Check that the arrangements and circulation records are credible; a promise to reissue is not completed reuse.

Buying can also work if the organiser names and funds the storage, collection and future-use arrangements. A durable custom cup need not be discarded after the fourth market.

It is reasonable to withhold a firm environmental ranking while obtaining comparable evidence. Neither ownership nor pooling automatically wins every impact category.

## What would you measure?

Compare equivalent drink-serving volumes and service standards. Record cups issued, returned and reissued; losses and replacements; cleaning inputs; transport routes and loads; storage and end-of-life treatment. Keep observed use separate from forecast use.

Ask whether changes reduce the relevant impacts over the complete system. Lower waste, lower greenhouse-gas emissions and lower water use are different claims; do not assume one proves the others.

## Optional calculation · Allocating manufacture

Let M represent the manufacturing impact of one purchased cup in one specified impact category. Allocating it over the four planned uses gives M/4 per serving. Allocating it over an unsupported fifty uses gives M/50.

The former is 12.5 times the latter. This compares only that manufacturing allocation, not total emissions, and does not quantify rental’s advantage. Actual future use could change the allocation. No universal reuse break-even count is supplied here.

## Instructor note

Invite an initial buy-or-rent vote before opening round two. Ask students to name who performs the next action after a drink is finished. Finish with one operational commitment and one piece of evidence, not an unsupported “green” winner.

This is a reuse-system design case, not a claim that real suppliers have failed. All quantities and quotations are fictional; existing public programmes do not guarantee eligibility or availability for this organiser.

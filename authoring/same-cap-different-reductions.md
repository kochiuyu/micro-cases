# Same target. Same reduction at every plant?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 311 -->

ECO-26 · Fictional two-plant pilot · 15–20 minutes · Marginal abatement cost · Tradable allowances · Allocation and efficiency

![Two factory managers compare a plain emissions-planning sheet beside a window overlooking separate industrial buildings.](../same-cap-different-reductions-scene.jpg)

## The cap and the allocation answer different questions

US EPA explainers distinguish an overall emissions cap from the allowances that individual sources hold and trade. The European Commission’s monitoring guidance stresses verified emissions and compliance records. A trade changes who may emit under the cap; it does not by itself lower the cap.

EPA also discusses distribution and local pollution concerns. The World Bank describes carbon pricing as one part of a wider policy package. The fictional pilot below isolates the cost of meeting a fixed carbon target, not the optimal target, the fairness of free allocation or permission to relax other environmental requirements.

## Opening decision

**Fictional classroom situation.** Two independent facilities, North and South, already comply with every mandatory environmental and safety requirement. They are evaluating an additional, authorised one-year carbon-reduction pilot. At unchanged output and service, verified baseline emissions within its boundary are **50 tonnes of CO2 per plant**. The pilot must reduce the combined 100 tonnes by 40 tonnes, leaving a maximum of 60.

Uniform requires each plant to cut 20 tonnes, with no transfer of its individual limit. Trading begins with **30 free allowances per plant**; each allowance covers one tonne emitted in this same year and boundary. The total issued is fixed at 60. A plant may transfer allowances only through the pilot registry and must surrender enough to cover its verified remaining emissions. The independent administrator enforces those agreed pilot rules; they are not a substitute for law.

No offsets, extra allowance issue, banking, borrowing, entry, output changes or emissions leakage are permitted in this simplified exercise. Local air-quality and safety limits remain independently binding. The reductions are comparable CO2 reductions; the exercise does not assume that different locations are interchangeable for every other pollutant. Both routes have the same already-funded monitoring and administration costs. Additional trading transaction costs are zero for the base calculation.

The plants’ engineers will report feasible reduction costs before the choice is final. Do not assume that equal baseline emissions imply equal technology or cost. You advise the administrator on cost-effectiveness while preserving the same aggregate target, not on how much environmental protection society should choose.

## Round two

Engineers verify the following incremental abatement costs, in HK dollars per tonne. North’s first 10 tonnes cost HK$100 each, its next 10 cost HK$300 each, and its final 10 cost HK$500 each. South’s first 10 cost HK$400 each, next 10 HK$700 each, and final 10 HK$1,000 each. Each plant can reduce at most 30 tonnes. These are successive blocks: a plant must take its earlier reductions before later ones. Costs within each block are constant and divisible by tonne. All relevant implementation and operating costs are included.

North proposes cutting 30 tonnes and selling **10 of its original allowances to South at HK$600 each**. South would cut 10 tonnes. Both have capacity, funding and time to implement the proposed reductions. The HK$600 price is a proposed bargain for this classroom example, not an observed carbon price or a uniquely predicted market equilibrium. No additional credit is created when North abates.

Compare that proposal with each plant cutting 20 tonnes. Calculate real abatement spending separately from net private expenditure after the allowance payment. Then verify each plant’s remaining emissions against its final allowance holdings and the unchanged combined cap. As a robustness check, ask how much total extra transaction cost the pilot could bear before the aggregate saving disappears.

**Student question:** Would you approve the proposed trade under the pilot rules? Calculate each plant’s reduction cost, net expenditure and allowance balance, then distinguish aggregate resource savings from the way the bargain distributes them.

## Start with the uniform benchmark

North’s 20-tonne cut costs 10 × 100 + 10 × 300 = **HK$4,000**. South’s costs 10 × 400 + 10 × 700 = **HK$11,000**. Total abatement spending is **HK$15,000**. Each emits 30 tonnes; combined emissions are 60. Equal physical cuts do not mean equal financial burdens.

## Move the expensive margin

Under the proposal North cuts 30 tonnes for 1,000 + 3,000 + 5,000 = **HK$9,000**. South cuts 10 for **HK$4,000**. Combined abatement spending is **HK$13,000**, saving HK$2,000 for the same 40-tonne reduction. Moving ten reductions from South’s HK$700 marginal block to North’s HK$500 block saves HK$200 each.

This is the least-cost feasible allocation under the supplied blocks: select North’s first 10 at 100, next 10 at 300, South’s first 10 at 400 and North’s last 10 at 500. North then reaches its 30-tonne capacity. Do not require equal marginal costs at a capacity boundary, or compare average costs when deciding which next tonne to move.

## Check holdings and private incentives

North emits 50 − 30 = **20 tonnes** and retains 30 − 10 = **20 allowances**. South emits 50 − 10 = **40 tonnes** and holds 30 + 10 = **40 allowances**. The totals remain 60 tonnes and 60 allowances; nothing has been counted twice. Buying allowances does not excuse South from its other legal limits.

The payment is 10 × HK$600 = **HK$6,000**. North’s net expenditure is 9,000 − 6,000 = **HK$3,000**, HK$1,000 less than its uniform cost. South’s is 4,000 + 6,000 = **HK$10,000**, also HK$1,000 less. Summing the two nets gives HK$13,000 because the payment is a transfer between them, not an extra resource cost.

A price strictly between HK$500 and HK$700 per allowance gives both a positive gain from these ten transfers, before transaction costs. At either endpoint one party is indifferent. Initial allocation and price affect who pays and receives; under the stated costless, feasible-trade assumptions they do not change which reductions are cheapest.

## Keep the conclusion within the model

Approve the proposal if the registry transfer, verified emissions and implementation checks hold. Extra real transaction costs below HK$2,000 leave an aggregate saving; at HK$2,000 the aggregate advantage vanishes. Their distribution may still make one party unwilling to trade at HK$600, so aggregate gains alone do not guarantee participation.

Trading achieves the same target more cheaply here, not a larger environmental reduction or proof that 60 tonnes is socially optimal. A thin market, bargaining power, inaccurate reports, local co-pollutants or implementation constraints could change practical conclusions. Do not issue extra allowances to make a reluctant trade easier or infer that free allocation has no distributional significance.

## Instructor note

Start with a vote on identical cuts versus flexibility. Reveal the cost blocks, then ask students to keep three ledgers: tonnes abated, allowances held and money spent or transferred. Require both plants’ compliance checks before accepting a cost-saving answer. Use the HK$500–700 bargaining interval to separate marginal cost from average cost and allocation from efficiency.

Unlike ECO-18, there is no shared-stock depletion across days, replenishment or future resource use. This is a single-period, fixed-cap allocation of abatement. All plants, costs, baselines, rules and the proposed price are invented. Public sources explain instruments and safeguards; they do not establish that a two-firm real market will automatically reach this outcome. Actual environmental obligations remain outside the discretionary trade-off.

# One expert. Who else can cover?

PPL-16 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Cross-training · Skill redundancy · Knowledge concentration

![Two framing-workshop colleagues discuss a plain training folder beside frame samples and backing boards.](../someone-else-can-cover-scene.jpg)

## A real training context, a fictional staffing choice

On 14 November 2025, HKPC announced its 2024–25 annual report and described its FutureSkills training activity. That is a local context for investing in skills, not evidence that one training design always works. The workshop and decision below are invented.

## Opening decision

A small Hong Kong framing workshop has one training allocation this quarter: HK$12,000, including course or coaching costs, paid learning time and cover for displaced work. It can fund one complete programme, not both.

A deepens specialist Mei’s advanced conservation-framing skills. It supports a possible future service, but no new orders or revenue are promised. B cross-trains willing, experienced colleague Jo on a limited part of Mei’s current work. Mei supports both options; Jo’s existing workload will be reduced during training and any later cover, not squeezed into unpaid hours.

Both programmes are feasible within the allocation. Current customer commitments and safety requirements remain protected under either choice. Neither employee is leaving. Decide how to use this quarter’s development opportunity.

## Evidence

| Programme | What the allocation buys | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| A: deeper expertise | Advanced conservation-framing development for Mei | New demand or additional revenue |
| B: bounded cover | Mei coaches Jo, with documented practice and assessment | Competence in Mei’s whole role |

**Student question:** Would you use this quarter’s allocation for A, deeper expertise, or B, bounded cover? Explain which organisational capability you would strengthen and what evidence could change your choice.

## Round two

A task map now separates 12 routine order types from four specialist order types. The numbers count types, not hours or workload shares. Routine manual fitting and documented visual checks can be transferred to Jo through the proposed supervised practice and assessment. Conservation decisions and machine setup remain restricted to Mei under the workshop’s competence rules; B does not qualify Jo for them.

Mei has approved leave in six weeks. There is enough paid time before then to complete B and assess Jo, but passing is not guaranteed. If Jo passes the task-specific checks, funded workload cover lets Jo handle the mapped routine work during the absence. Specialist work has already been scheduled around the leave with customers’ agreement.

Under A, or if Jo does not pass B, an already funded reduced-service plan protects accepted orders and staff workloads; the shop will temporarily decline new routine requests it cannot cover. No lost-order value is supplied. A’s advanced course is offered again next quarter, but next quarter’s funding is not yet approved. Mei’s leave will not be cancelled.

**Student question:** Would you retain or change your training choice after the task map and planned absence? Specify what Jo may do only after assessment, what remains outside the cover role, and the fallback if readiness is not demonstrated.

## Map tasks, not a substitute person

The useful unit is a task with a clear boundary. Cover for routine fitting does not transfer authority over conservation decisions or machine setup. Twelve of 16 order types is 75% of the listed categories, not 75% of time, capacity or revenue. Their workloads are unknown.

## Depth and resilience solve different problems

A builds specialist depth for a possible future service. B reduces dependence on one person for a defined routine subset. The absence makes that subset more relevant, without proving B has the better financial return. The funded reduced-service plan leaves A a credible choice if deeper expertise is the stronger strategic priority.

## Make coverage a demonstrated capability

For B, agree the task list, practice examples, competent assessor and pass criteria before training. Observe Jo completing those tasks safely without inappropriate prompting; keep records and a clear escalation route. If readiness is not demonstrated, use the reduced-service plan rather than broadening authority. Review skill retention after the leave.

## Instructor note

Allow a provisional vote, then reveal the task map and leave. Ask groups to draw a small coverage map: Mei only, Jo after assessment, and fallback. Reward a justified A or B, not an automatic cross-training slogan. Contrast new-hire onboarding with this allocation of development among established colleagues. Do not treat a short course as a qualification, a checklist as competence, or willingness as permission to add unpaid work.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

The workshop, people, programmes, budget, six-week interval, 12 routine and four specialist order types, assessment feasibility and funded cover plans are fictional assumptions. Counts describe categories, not task frequency, labour hours or market demand. No legal qualification is claimed; the workshop’s stipulated competence restrictions remain binding in both routes. Jo is an experienced adult colleague, voluntarily participating with paid protected time; Mei’s leave and existing customer commitments remain protected. Training completion is not a guaranteed pass. Future training funding, advanced-service sales and the value of declined new requests are unknown. Five relevant source bodies were read: HKPC announcement; USGS guide introductory and role-specific planning sections; HSE work-equipment training and competence sections; NIST workforce directory introduction and incumbent-worker training examples; CIPD succession planning roles and future-needs sections. These are institutional guidance/context, not five causal evaluations. Foreign legal provisions are not presented as Hong Kong compliance advice. Unlike PPL-04 onboarding readiness, the primary decision is specialist depth versus bounded organisational skill redundancy among established staff.

Publicly accessible sections on key roles, skill pools and future capability needs inform the depth-versus-coverage distinction. No effect size or universal replacement model is asserted.

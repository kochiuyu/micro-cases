# A profit. Where did the cash go?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 169 -->

ACC-15 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Profit versus cash · Working-capital bridge · Operating and investing flows

![A packaging-studio owner and accounts colleague review a ledger and plain papers beside stored materials.](../profit-not-cash-scene.jpg)

## A published reporting example

Design Capital’s 2025 annual report, available through HKEX, presents a cash-flow reconciliation that adjusts its reported result for depreciation and operating balances, and shows equipment purchases in investing activities. The statements distinguish a period’s accounting result from changes in cash.

This is ordinary reporting, not evidence of wrongdoing. The packaging studio and all figures below are original fiction, not that company’s results. Its report is a structural example, not a claim that its business has our cash problem.

## Opening decision

A growing fictional Hong Kong packaging studio has completed its monthly accounts. All figures in this case are HK$000, so 30 means HK$30,000. Revenue is 150, cost of goods sold is 80, other operating expenses are 30 and depreciation is 10. The reported profit is 30. Opening unrestricted bank cash was 100; closing cash is 65. There is no petty cash or other cash equivalent to reconcile.

The owner asks why a profitable month used 35 of cash. One proposed response is an immediate across-the-board cut in next month’s operating-expense budget. Another is to first trace customer collections, stock purchasing and investment payments, then target the actual cash constraints. The cash balance covers all currently due obligations; there is time for this review without delaying wages, taxes, supplier payments or safety work. No evidence of waste, fraud or an accounting error has yet been found.

The first dashboard contains only the profit summary and bank balances. It does not show cash collected from customers, purchases paid, changes in stock or unpaid invoices, or capital purchases. A profitable month does not rule out an expense problem, but the dashboard alone does not identify one. Choose the first management response, not whether to stop monitoring liquidity.

## Round two

The accountant supplies complete movements, still in HK$000. Trade receivables increased from 20 to 55; inventories at cost increased from 40 to 65; trade payables for stock purchases increased from 15 to 20. Revenue is fully earned; all balances relate to the stated sales or stock transactions. There are no write-offs, impairments, returns, foreign-exchange movements or non-cash acquisitions affecting these balances. Other operating expenses of 30 were all paid this month, with no accrual or prepayment change.

Depreciation of 10 is a non-cash expense on existing equipment. Separately, a new machine cost 20 and was paid in full this month. It meets the capitalisation conditions and is not part of cost of sales or the 30 of other expenses. It was not yet available for use at month-end, so no depreciation on it is included this month. There are no other investing flows, loans, equity contributions, owner withdrawals, leases, interest, taxes or other cash movements in this simplified exercise.

For management follow-up, the closing receivables of 55 comprise 50 within agreed terms and 5 overdue. The closing stock of 65 includes 50 allocated to scheduled orders and 15 not yet allocated. These are closing balances, not additional cash outflows to add to the movements. No default, obsolescence or immediate saleability is established. Existing supplier terms remain binding; their unpaid balance is not permission to pay late. The machine’s future return has not been assessed in this case.

**Student question:** Reconcile profit to operating cash flow and then to the closing bank balance. Which specific cash-management action would you prioritise next, and why would neither adding back depreciation nor cancelling future expenses undo this month’s cash movements?

## Build the operating bridge

All figures are HK$000. Begin with profit 30. Add back non-cash depreciation 10. Subtract the receivables increase of 55 − 20 = 35, and the inventory increase of 65 − 40 = 25. Add the trade-payables increase of 20 − 15 = 5. Operating cash flow is 30 + 10 − 35 − 25 + 5 = −15. Adding back depreciation reverses its effect within this reconciliation; it does not create a receipt or erase the economic cost of using equipment.

## Check the result using actual receipts and payments

Customer receipts are revenue 150 + opening receivables 20 − closing receivables 55 = 115. Inventory purchases are cost of sales 80 + closing inventory 65 − opening inventory 40 = 105. Cash paid to stock suppliers is purchases 105 + opening payables 15 − closing payables 20 = 100. Receipts 115 − supplier payments 100 − other paid expenses 30 = operating outflow 15. This direct cross-check gives the same result as the indirect bridge.

## Separate investment from operations

The machine payment of 20 is an investing outflow, not another deduction in calculating operating cash flow. Operating −15 plus investing −20 and financing 0 equals the cash change −35. Opening cash 100 − 35 = closing cash 65. Do not subtract the machine twice or treat its full purchase as this month’s expense. The bridge explains classification and timing, not whether buying the machine was a good investment.

## Target the mechanism, not the headline

First assign follow-up on the overdue 5, verify the expected collection dates for the 50 still within terms, and review whether the 15 of unallocated stock should change the next purchasing plan. Do not label all 55 overdue or assume all 15 can be converted to cash immediately. Coordinate the stock allocated to orders with delivery and collection timing. Maintain agreed supplier payments and a near-term cash forecast. A review may also find avoidable expenses, but the bank decline alone does not establish waste in the paid 30.

## Growth can absorb cash without making profit fictitious

A rise in receivables delays cash relative to earned revenue. Building stock uses purchases not yet all recognised as cost of sales; supplier credit partly offsets that timing. These movements can accompany growth, but the reconciliation does not guarantee eventual collection or demand for stock. Future spending choices affect future cash; they cannot reverse completed purchases. Nor does a positive profit justify ignoring a negative operating flow.

## Instructor note

Ask students to propose the missing bridge before showing the balances. Reveal the movements and have pairs assign a plus or minus sign before calculating. Then cross-check receipts and supplier payments directly. Challenge treating closing balances as changes, adding depreciation as actual income, or placing the machine in operating expenses. Finish with a named follow-up action tied to the overdue or unallocated amount, not an assumed cash recovery. This is a simplified management reconciliation, not a complete statutory statement or investment appraisal.

There are no valuation changes, returns, write-offs, currency effects or other movements. Current due commitments are covered; supplier payment terms remain. Closing 55 receivables and 65 stock provide management composition, not extra movements. The exercise uses a common simplified operating bridge; it does not identify the reporting framework applicable to any actual small firm or resolve forthcoming presentation amendments.

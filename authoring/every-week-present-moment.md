# Advertise every week. Or be present at the moment?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 291 -->

MKT-25 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Media scheduling · Carryover · Buying occasions

![Two fictional event-photography studio colleagues arrange blank advertising cards on a plain four-column planning board beside a camera.](../every-week-present-moment-scene.jpg)

## Equal spend does not mean equal timing

Media-scheduling research treats advertising as something whose effects can persist and change over time. A schedule that creates a large peak need not fit the moments when customers choose. Different models and objectives can favour different schedules; neither constant advertising nor bursts is universally best.

This fictional exercise uses a small, explicitly invented visibility model. It is not a measured memory law, a platform forecast or a universal rule about how often people should see an advertisement.

## Opening decision

A fictional Hong Kong event-photography studio is buying a four-week public advertising campaign before a seasonal booking period. It has HK$8,000 for eight identical media blocks at HK$1,000 each. Creative, production and placement costs are included. Steady buys two blocks each week. Bursts buys four in week 1 and four in week 3. These are the only two available packages, both feasible and not yet booked. Prices, service capacity, creative quality and the broad adult audience are otherwise held fixed; no personal targeting or direct messages are proposed.

Management wants communication to be present when prospective clients make their booking decision, not merely to maximise a peak. The detailed decision calendar is being checked. The agency initially suggests comparing the four weekly visibility scores with equal weight. You may use that as a provisional comparison, but there is no evidence yet that an equal number of decisions occurs each week.

For this classroom comparison only, a visibility index starts at zero before week 1. Each paid block adds 10 index points during its week; half the previous week’s end score remains. Thus this week’s score equals half last week’s score plus 10 times this week’s blocks. Bookings are considered at each week’s end, after that week’s ads. The same rule applies to both packages and all audience groups. It describes an assumed communication proxy, not a percentage, unique reach or probability of purchase. No saturation, creative wearout, competitor response or extra cost is modelled within these two packages. Do not extrapolate the rule to unlimited spending.

**Student question:** Would you provisionally choose steady advertising or two bursts? Compare their four-week visibility profiles, and identify what you still need to know about when customers decide.

## Round two

The planning team now checks the decision calendar for 100 distinct prospective client groups. Ten will consider booking at the end of week 1, 40 at the end of week 2, 10 at the end of week 3 and 40 at the end of week 4. Each group appears in exactly one window. These are verified timing inputs for this exercise, not 100 confirmed customers or a representative survey of the whole market. The campaign does not move these dates in the model; no identities or contact details are needed.

The agreed objective is now the average visibility index at these decision windows: multiply each weekly score by the number of groups deciding then, add, and divide by 100. Every group has equal weight. Either package still costs HK$8,000. There is no conversion rate, margin, measured causal response or value for decisions after week 4. Any visibility remaining beyond the exercise may matter to a real business, but no additional terminal value is included here. Choose only between the two offered schedules; a newly invented timing package has not been priced or made available.

**Student question:** With the booking-window evidence, which schedule would you choose under the supplied model? Calculate visibility at decision time, and explain why that score is neither a count of new customers nor a sales forecast.

## Build the profiles before judging them

Steady scores are 20, 30, 35 and 37.5: for example, week 3 is half of 30 plus 20. Bursts scores are 40, 20, 50 and 25: week 3 is half of 20 plus 40. Both buy eight blocks; the score differences arise from placement in time and the supplied carryover assumption.

Equal-weight averages are 30.625 for Steady and 33.75 for Bursts. That initially favours Bursts for an equally weighted calendar, not automatically for the studio’s actual booking objective. No advertising spend has disappeared in the quiet burst weeks; earlier communication simply retains some assumed effect.

## Weight the moments that matter

Steady’s decision-weighted score is (10 × 20 + 40 × 30 + 10 × 35 + 40 × 37.5) ÷ 100 = 32.5. Bursts gives (10 × 40 + 40 × 20 + 10 × 50 + 40 × 25) ÷ 100 = 27. Steady leads by 5.5 index points under the supplied inputs.

The larger burst peaks fall in weeks with only ten decision groups, while 40 decide during each quieter week. Steady therefore better meets this bounded objective. That is not evidence that bursts are inherently wasteful or that continuous ads are always superior.

## Keep the score in its own units

A 32.5 score is not 32.5 people, a 32.5% awareness rate or 32.5 bookings. The 100 groups are prospective decision-makers, not sales. The case gives no link from score to purchase, so revenue, profit and customer-acquisition cost cannot be calculated.

Real planning would check recall or response evidence, audience comparability, saturation and the durability of communication, alongside booking timing. A model ranking is a reason for a testable plan, not proof that actual behaviour will follow the model.

## Test the assumption rather than worship the schedule

As a sensitivity exercise only, if all prior score remained instead of half, Steady would score 20, 40, 60, 80 and Bursts 40, 40, 80, 80. Using the same timing weights gives 56 for Steady and 60 for Bursts, reversing the ranking. This is not evidence of full retention; it shows why the carryover assumption needs support.

Likewise, a different decision calendar or value for later weeks could change the objective. Do not silently switch between the highest peak, equal-week average and decision-weighted score. Name what the campaign is trying to achieve before comparing packages.

## Instructor note

Give students 3 minutes to complete four scores, then reveal the calendar and allow 4 minutes to weight them. End with 2 minutes on what the index cannot establish. Use a four-column timeline rather than dense algebra. Distinguish scheduling over buying occasions from cross-channel unique reach and consented email frequency. No campaign deployment, personal-data use or extra schedule design is needed.

All studio details, costs, calendar and visibility coefficients are fictional. Both packages buy eight blocks for HK$8,000, with all costs included. The index is an invented communication proxy, not percentage awareness, unique people, probability or causal sales. Start zero; weekly recurrence S(t)=0.5 S(t−1)+10 b(t). Steady gives 20,30,35,37.5; Bursts 40,20,50,25. Equal-week averages 30.625/33.75; timing weights 0.1,0.4,0.1,0.4 give 32.5/27. Under alternative full retention, profiles 20,40,60,80 and 40,40,80,80 yield 56/60. Only supplied packages and four-week objective are ranked. Real later effects are not asserted to vanish; they are outside the specified objective. Research models use different assumptions, objectives and settings, none supplying these coefficients.

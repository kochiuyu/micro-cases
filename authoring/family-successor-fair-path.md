# A family successor. A fair path into responsibility?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 331 -->

ENT-28 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Successor development · Family governance · Management selection

![A family-business owner and two adult prospective managers discuss development plans as equals.](../family-successor-fair-path-scene.jpg)

## Separate the owner’s role from the manager’s job

Family-business governance guidance distinguishes ownership, family membership and employment. Successor development can combine experience, feedback and explicit selection criteria without treating a family connection as proof of competence. Research also highlights the work required when family owners collaborate with nonfamily managers. The firm, candidates and process choices below are fictional.

## Opening decision

A family-owned Hong Kong display-fixtures firm is preparing for a general manager’s retirement in eighteen months. No successor has been selected or promised the role. The owners want continuity of reliable service and may prefer family management if a willing family candidate becomes genuinely ready. They have not made family management a requirement. Ownership, inheritance and board seats will not change in this decision.

Two adults have voluntarily expressed interest: Alex, a family member currently working elsewhere, and Sam, a nonfamily employee. Neither must participate or give up their current job to enter the development process. The board will choose one nine-month preparation route now. A offers the willing family candidate a staged development-and-review path first. If Alex fails the common readiness standard, A opens a broader process for the remaining nine months. It does not promise appointment or lower the standard. Sam retains normal employee development but is not in A’s first-stage successor pool.

B develops and assesses Alex and Sam in parallel for the future management role. Both routes fit HK$60,000, including assessment and 48 hours of additional one-to-one coaching: A gives Alex all 48 hours; B gives each candidate 24. Existing jobs, pay and ordinary training stay unchanged. A allows deeper early preparation for family management; B tests a broader pool earlier but spreads extra coaching more thinly. No evidence yet establishes who is ready.

Before either route begins, the board will publish the same readiness requirements: internal order-change control, external supplier negotiation, people leadership and budget judgement. Each area must meet its stated standard; strength in one cannot erase a critical gap in another. An independent assessor will use comparable work samples and documented feedback. Nine-month review is not automatic appointment, and the current manager remains accountable until a later formal transition.

## Evidence

A: family-first staged preparation, 48 hours for Alex; broader process at month nine if Alex is not ready. B: parallel preparation, 24 hours each for Alex and Sam. Each costs HK$60,000 including assessment. Same four readiness standards; ownership unchanged; no appointment promised.

**Student question:** Would you choose the family-first staged route or the parallel candidate route? Explain the trade-off in preparation and comparison, and state what evidence must be required before anyone gains managerial responsibility.

## Round two

A preliminary assessment, already completed outside the proposed budget, shows complementary profiles. Alex has led external supplier negotiations successfully in another firm, with verified references, but has never managed this firm’s order-change approval process. In a simulated change request, Alex misses a required approval. Sam correctly manages that internal process in the same simulation, but has no demonstrated independent external negotiation experience; past attendance at meetings does not establish that skill. Neither profile establishes overall readiness. People leadership and budget judgement still need comparable evidence for both.

Both remain willing and accept feedback. Alex’s outside experience is relevant but does not automatically transfer to the internal approval system. Sam’s internal experience is valuable but is not proof of capability in the external role. The firm can provide safe supervised practice in each missing area within either chosen route’s scope. Simulations and supervised tasks do not confer independent authority. The owner confirms that a competent nonfamily manager would not require selling shares or abandoning the family’s service values.

The same budgets and coaching allocations remain available. No one is selected by this assessment, and neither gap is known to be unfixable within nine months. If no candidate meets every critical standard, the board must use further development, a broader search or an interim plan rather than appoint someone by default. The eighteen-month retirement horizon allows planning but is not a guarantee that a ready successor will emerge.

**Student question:** Would you keep or change the preparation route? Design one fair development-and-review step for each candidate’s gap, and explain why ownership continuity does not settle managerial succession.

## Answer the first decision: make the process credible

A is defensible if the owners openly prioritise testing a family-management path, provide meaningful preparation and keep a real readiness gate and fallback. It should not be sold to Sam as an equal first-stage contest. B is defensible when early comparison and preserving options matter more than concentrated coaching. Neither family membership, tenure nor a polished external CV establishes readiness. Define the role and evidence before rewarding a preferred identity.

## Answer the revealed decision: compare development, not labels

I favour B: the preliminary evidence reveals two plausible but incomplete profiles, and parallel development tests both paths before the retirement approaches. This is not a verdict that Sam should win or that 24 coaching hours will suffice. Alex needs guided order-change practice, followed by a fresh simulated case demonstrating every approval. Sam needs supervised negotiation preparation and participation, followed by an independently assessed negotiation exercise with the same role-relevant standards. Both also need people-leadership and budget evidence.

## Common standards, targeted support

Use a documented baseline, tailored learning goals, feedback at months three and six, and an independent month-nine review. Comparable standards do not require identical exercises when the gaps differ, but evidence must be equally demanding and relevant. Record coaching access, task difficulty, observed actions and limitations. Protect candidate confidentiality, declare assessor conflicts and allow corrections to factual errors. Do not turn family gatherings into informal interviews or assume that willingness is permanent consent.

## Ownership is not an appointment letter

Ownership continuity concerns who holds and governs the shares; managerial succession concerns delegated responsibility and competence. A nonfamily manager can work within owner-approved strategy and accountability arrangements. Family members can remain owners without becoming managers. If nobody is ready, preserve supervision and choose a supported development, wider-search or interim route. Readiness is an evidence gate, not an obligation to fill the role on a preferred date.

## Instructor note

This case precedes appointment; it is not a handover to an already competent successor. Ask students to separate choosing a process, developing capability and eventually selecting a manager. No candidate is declared superior by the reveal. Keep the coaching trade-off visible: 48 hours for one versus 24 each for two, with assessment included under either route. Do not infer ability from age, gender, family status or private health. This is an original governance exercise, not advice about inheritance law or a real employment decision.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

The firm, candidates, profiles, simulations, costs and timetable are fictional. Alex and Sam are adults and voluntary participants. No ownership, inheritance, share transfer, board appointment or employment contract is executed. A is explicitly a family-first development route, not a purportedly equal simultaneous contest; it preserves a common readiness standard and a broader fallback at month nine. B divides 48 coaching hours equally: 48/2 = 24. Each complete route costs HK$60,000 including assessment; no hourly price or success probability is implied. Nine months of preparation leaves nine months before the planned eighteen-month retirement. The preliminary assessment is already completed outside the unspent allocation. All four readiness areas matter; two remain untested for both candidates. No amount of coaching guarantees a pass. Sources offer governance concepts, qualitative evidence and contextual survey findings, not a validated nine-month training prescription or a general superiority of family or nonfamily managers.

- [IFC: Family Business Governance Handbook, fourth edition, 2018](https://www.ifc.org/content/dam/ifc/doc/mgrt/family-business-governance-handbook.pdf). Primary governance handbook. Sections on family employment and management succession discuss qualification, career development, feedback and selection before transition.
- [John Davis: Organizing the Family-Run Business, Harvard Business School](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/organizing-the-family-run-business). Original expert exposition distinguishing family, ownership and business roles and their coordinating governance structures. Not a universal legal structure or causal study.
- [Bennedsen and Henry: When Succession Skips a Generation, INSEAD](https://knowledge.insead.edu/family-business/when-succession-skips-generation). Original faculty commentary on nonfamily executives, family roles and board involvement. Illustrates possibilities; does not prove a preferred appointment for this firm.
- [DCU National Centre for Family Business: To Join or Not to Join?, 2024](https://doras.dcu.ie/29891/1/DCU%20NCFB%20Succession%20Report%202024.pdf). Primary report based on surveys and interviews with higher-education students from family-business backgrounds in Ireland. Distinguishes willingness, experience and ownership perceptions; not a representative forecast for Hong Kong successors.
- [Van Helvert-Beugels, Nordqvist and Flören: Managing tensions as paradox in CEO succession, 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0266242619898609). Original open-access three-year qualitative study of one Dutch family firm. Shows role and collaboration tensions after appointing a nonfamily CEO; lacks statistical generalisability.

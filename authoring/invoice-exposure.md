# The invoice is not the exposure

Case 38 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Finance · Foreign exchange · Cash matching

![A stationery wholesaler and accounting colleague discuss an invoice folder beside stock shelves in a warm watercolor-and-ink office scene.](../invoice-exposure-scene.jpg)

## In the news

In its 13 November 2025 interim results, Hong Kong-listed VTech described using foreign-exchange forwards where appropriate for operating currency risks, while excluding speculative derivative transactions from its policy.

There is also a simpler starting question: can receipts pay bills in the same currency? Japanese distributor Marubun’s 16 May 2025 results presentation described matching US-dollar purchases with corresponding US-dollar sales. Neither company is the fictional wholesaler below, and their policies do not establish which financial product this shop should buy.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. You own a Hong Kong stationery wholesaler. A Japanese supplier must receive JPY1,000,000 in 30 days. Your first budget assumes you will buy all those yen using Hong Kong dollars on the payment date.

The accountant shows two possible exchange-rate scenarios. A rate of HK$0.050 per yen means one yen costs five Hong Kong cents. These are invented comparison rates, not current quotes or forecasts. No probabilities are supplied. Ignore conversion fees in the arithmetic.

You want to decide how much supplier-payment uncertainty to carry. There is time to inspect all yen receipts and payments before arranging anything. No financial product has been priced or purchased.

## Evidence

| Hypothetical payment-date rate | HK dollars to buy JPY1,000,000 |
|---|---:|
| HK$0.050 per yen | HK$50,000 |
| HK$0.055 per yen | HK$55,000 |

The higher rate requires HK$5,000 more if buying the full invoice amount. Rates are invented scenarios, with no probabilities; conversion fees are ignored.

**Student question:** Would you seek budget certainty for the full invoice, leave the amount uncertain, or check other yen cash flows first? What information could change your answer?

## Round two

Your accounts colleague finds a separate customer receipt of JPY600,000. It is due before the supplier settlement cutoff on the same day.

For this classroom base case, assume that receipt is certain, unrestricted and available in time. The business can use those yen directly to pay the supplier, with no currency conversion. There are no other yen payments or commitments. The supplier still receives the full JPY1,000,000.

Real customer receipts are not automatically certain. We temporarily remove default and timing risk to isolate the currency-matching decision.

**Student question:** How many yen must you now buy with Hong Kong dollars? Would you seek certainty for that amount, and what must be true for your plan to work?

## Match the currency before choosing protection

Start with the supplier payment: JPY1,000,000. Use the available customer receipt: JPY600,000. The remaining yen to buy are 1,000,000 − 600,000 = JPY400,000.

At HK$0.050 per yen, buying the remainder costs HK$20,000. At HK$0.055, it costs HK$22,000. The difference in additional HK-dollar funding is therefore HK$2,000, not HK$5,000.

This matching is often called a natural hedge: an operating receipt offsets an operating payment in the same currency. It does not cancel either customer or supplier contract.

## Choose what uncertainty remains

Seeking budget certainty for the remaining JPY400,000 can make sense if an uncertain HK-dollar outlay would strain the business. Accepting that uncertainty can also be defensible if its cash buffer and risk policy allow it.

The case supplies no instrument price, fees or probabilities, so it cannot show that a hedge is cheaper, free or always better. Hedging aims to manage uncertainty, not to prove that you can predict the yen.

If you arrange to buy the full JPY1,000,000 as well as receiving JPY600,000, the incoming yen are left to hold or convert. That creates another currency position to manage. Gross-invoice protection is not automatically the right size for the net funding need.

## Check the match, not just the amount

Before relying on a real receipt, verify its currency, usable amount, arrival time and access restrictions. Check other commitments that may already need those yen.

If the customer pays late or defaults, you may need to fund more than JPY400,000 by the supplier deadline. Currency matching does not remove credit risk or ensure cash arrives on time. Those risks were explicitly excluded only for our base calculation.

Our HK$20,000 versus HK$22,000 comparison measures additional cash needed for this supplier payment. It is not the company’s total foreign-exchange profit, accounting valuation or overall business exposure.

Key idea: before protecting a payment, check what already pays for it.

## Instructor note

Allow 2 minutes for the gross-invoice comparison, 3 for the receipt reveal and 5–7 for discussion. Ask students to draw two yen arrows into and out of the business, then calculate the unmatched amount. Ask whether “the invoice fell to 400,000” is correct: it did not. Finally relax one assumption, such as receipt timing, without pretending the base answer already accounts for it. Do not turn this into a recommendation to trade a particular instrument.

# A lower return. A better investment?

![Two workshop colleagues discuss a plain investment proposal beside enclosed equipment and component trays.](../lower-return-better-investment-scene.jpg)

## Investing and measuring are different decisions

On 16 September 2024, Hong Kong launched the New Industrialisation Acceleration Scheme to support smart production facilities in strategic industries. That dated announcement illustrates investment policy, not evidence that a particular upgrade earns a return. The smaller workshop division below is fictional, receives no scheme funding and makes no claim of eligibility.

## Opening decision

A fictional Hong Kong workshop group gives its assembly division responsibility for recommending equipment investments. Next year’s existing operation has an agreed investment base of HK$1,000,000 and forecast controllable operating profit of HK$200,000. A safe, optional upgrade adds HK$400,000 to that base and HK$60,000 to annual controllable operating profit. Existing business is unchanged; the upgrade does not replace any required safety expenditure.

For this exercise, ROI means annual controllable operating profit divided by the agreed investment base. Finance has already applied the same accounting conventions, full-year timing and asset-base policy to both columns. Operating profit includes depreciation and all incremental operating costs, but excludes the capital charge. The company uses a 10% annual capital-charge rate for this division.

The group has separately reviewed full-life cash flows and finds a positive net present value even after all tax, timing, support and compensation costs. Treat that conclusion as supplied, not something proved by one year’s accounting return. Cash and capacity are sufficient; no better competing project or hidden obligation is supplied. The investment remains a forecast, not a guaranteed outcome. The manager worries that a lower reported ROI will make the division look worse.

**Student question:** Would you recommend accepting the upgrade or protecting the division’s existing percentage return? Calculate the existing division’s ROI, the upgrade’s standalone ROI and the combined division’s ROI and explain whose objective your recommendation serves.

## Round two

Finance prepares a second measure: residual income equals controllable operating profit minus 10% of the agreed investment base. The existing division therefore earns HK$100,000 above its capital charge. With the upgrade it earns HK$120,000. This HK$20,000 increase is annual accounting residual income, not the supplied full-life net present value.

The manager’s existing signed agreement awards a performance payment only if division ROI is at least 20%. There is no other performance-payment component in this simplified agreement. Accepting the upgrade would miss that threshold; declining would meet it on the forecast. All contractual pay and entitlements must be honoured. The group cannot retroactively alter the threshold or cancel an earned payment.

The board can approve the investment independently and discuss a mutually agreed incentive design for a later period before that period starts. It can retain ROI for comparability while using residual-income improvement, with quality and safety requirements, to assess future investment recommendations. The existing forecast and project cash flows do not change merely because the measurement changes. No bonus waiver or new contract has been agreed in this case.

**Student question:** Would you retain percentage ROI as the main future investment incentive, or propose a residual-income measure alongside operating safeguards? Recommend the project and explain how to resolve the incentive conflict without rewriting an existing employment agreement.

## Separate project return from division return

The existing operation earns 200,000 / 1,000,000 = 20%. The upgrade earns 60,000 / 400,000 = 15% on the specified accounting basis. Together, 260,000 / 1,400,000 is about 18.57%. A 15% increment pulls down a 20% average even though it exceeds the company’s 10% charge. Rejecting it preserves the ratio, not the additional profit.

## Charge for capital in dollars

Without the upgrade, residual income is HK$200,000 − 10% × HK$1,000,000 = HK$100,000. With it, HK$260,000 − 10% × HK$1,400,000 = HK$120,000. The increment is HK$60,000 − HK$40,000 = HK$20,000. This supports the company’s supplied positive-value appraisal but does not replace a full cash-flow review.

## Repair incentives without changing the past

Recommend the upgrade on the stipulated company evidence. Recognise that the manager faces a genuine private incentive to refuse; disagreement does not establish dishonesty. Honour the existing agreement and separate the board’s investment authority from future, mutually agreed performance design. Residual income can reward an above-charge increment, while ROI still describes scale-adjusted performance. Neither measure licenses unsafe output or ignoring customers.

## Instructor note

Unlike FIN-08, which compares cash-flow timing between alternative fit-outs, this case holds the positive-value project appraisal fixed and tests investment-centre incentives. Unlike ACC-03, it does not update a revenue estimate to certify an unchanged bonus. Ask students to distinguish company recommendation, manager incentive and contractual authority. Residual income is an absolute amount affected by division size and accounting choices; a single-year positive amount does not prove positive NPV or an optimal incentive contract.

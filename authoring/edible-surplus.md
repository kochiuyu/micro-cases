# Eighty kilos wasted. Eighty kilos to donate?

Case 67 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Sustainability · Food-waste prevention · Edible surplus

![A chef and manager discuss a plan in a modest canteen kitchen beside a clean covered container.](../edible-surplus-scene.jpg)

## In the news

In a 10 July 2024 Legislative Council reply, the Hong Kong Government described how the Food Wise Eateries Scheme encouraged portioning options and management measures to reduce food waste at source.

Food recovery can also put suitable surplus to use. But prevention and donation address different situations. The fictional canteen below must decide where to direct its first organised project, without treating every kilogram discarded as a kilogram available to donate.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. A busy Hong Kong canteen records 80 kilograms of food-related discards per operating day. Its manager wants a daily donation collection to become the centrepiece of a new waste-reduction programme. A colleague suggests changing purchasing, preparation and portioning first.

The team has limited implementation time this month. It must choose its first organised project, not rule out the other approach forever. Prompt recovery of suitable surplus could benefit recipients; preventing unnecessary waste could reduce what needs handling in the first place.

Only the total weight is currently available. It does not show how much is edible, unserved or suitable for donation. No charity has agreed to collect or accept the canteen’s food. Existing safe handling and any existing donation arrangements continue while the new project is considered.

## Evidence

| Known at the first meeting | What remains unknown |
|---|---|
| 80 kg of food-related discards per operating day | Composition and causes |
| Limited implementation time | Effort and feasible impact of each option |
| Donation and prevention are possible approaches | Suitable surplus, recipient requirements and collection |

**Student question:** Would you put the team’s first implementation effort into donation collection or upstream waste prevention? What would you need to know before committing?

## Round two

An audit measures the same operational stream over comparable service periods, keeping categories separate before any mixing. The hypothetical average daily total comprises:

| Category | Weight per operating day |
|---|---:|
| Food returned on customers’ plates, including uneaten food | 60 kg |
| Inedible preparation scraps | 15 kg |
| Unserved surplus retained separately, potentially suitable for donation | 5 kg |
| Total measured stream | 80 kg |

These categories exhaust the measured stream. The unserved surplus still needs appropriate assessment and a workable recipient arrangement. The audit does not establish why the plate returns are large or how much of them could be prevented.

**Student question:** How would you revise the plan after this audit? What can donation address, and which additional waste-prevention action would you investigate or test first?

## Match the intervention to the material

At most 5 kg per operating day is identified as potentially suitable unserved surplus in this measured stream. That is 5 ÷ 80 × 100 = 6.25%, before checks and acceptance arrangements. It is an upper bound on the identified candidate material, not a promise that 5 kg will be safely donated.

Do not offer plate leftovers for donation. The 15 kg category is explicitly inedible. Unserved food is not automatically safe or acceptable: recipient requirements, assessment and workable handling and collection still matter. Follow relevant food-recovery guidance and the actual receiving organisation’s requirements rather than using this case as a handling manual.

## A small share can still have social value

The donation opportunity is smaller than the original 80 kg headline, but that does not make it worthless. Its value to recipients cannot be inferred from its percentage of the canteen’s discards. Arrange appropriate recovery where feasible without interrupting existing safe practices.

The implementation constraint concerns where to begin the new programme. It does not require abandoning donation or delaying a safe existing arrangement until a prevention study is finished. Prevention, recovery and appropriate handling of remaining material can complement one another.

## Investigate the large stream without promising to eliminate it

Ask why food comes back on plates. Are portions poorly matched to appetite, menu items unpopular, or serving arrangements inflexible? Observe and ask customers before selecting a change. The audit alone does not identify the cause.

A bounded test could offer portion choices or optional seconds, or adjust a menu item after investigating returns. Protect adequate meals, customer choice and service quality. A smaller portion imposed on everyone is not automatically an improvement.

Do not assume all 60 kg is preventable. Nor does calling the 15 kg inedible show that every future preparation scrap is unavoidable; purchasing and preparation changes may affect that stream too.

## Measure the change, not just the collection

Compare like-for-like service periods and record the number and type of meals served. Track both total weights and category weights, actual accepted donation, implementation effort and customer responses, including whether meals remain adequate.

Donation kilograms show recovery, not necessarily prevention. Lower total waste during a quieter day does not by itself show that the intervention worked. The case provides no basis for a carbon-saving figure, financial return, legal-compliance claim or zero-waste promise.

## Instructor note

Let students defend both prompt recovery and upstream prevention before the audit. The opening total should provoke a question about composition, not an assumption that 80 kg is edible. After the reveal, require a plan for the identified streams and a realistic first action.

A strong response treats 5 kg as potential material rather than guaranteed accepted donation, investigates plate returns without blaming customers, and preserves adequate meals. It should not dismiss donation because its measured share is small.

The canteen and all quantities are invented. No real organisation has agreed to accept this food. This is an intervention-design discussion, not technical food-safety or legal advice.

## Sources and case assumptions

All information needed for classroom decisions is in the case. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

The canteen, quantities, audit and implementation constraint are invented. The three categories cover the same measured stream and were separated before mixing. Five kilograms, or 6.25% of 80 kg, is the identified potentially donatable material before assessment, recipient requirements and logistics; actual accepted donation is unknown. Neither all plate returns being preventable nor all preparation scraps being unavoidable is assumed. Sources checked 14 November 2026. The government reply describes a historical programme, not verification of current scheme categories or proof of its causal effect. EPA guidance supports intervention design; its environmental scale does not determine this canteen’s financial or social outcomes. Food-recovery guidance must be applied to the actual food and operation; this case supplies no detailed handling instructions. The Feeding Hong Kong toolkit’s acceptance guidance is organisation-specific, not universal law.

# Two plans. Who chooses which one?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 383 -->

ECO-32 · Singapore · 15–20 minutes · Self-selection · Incentive compatibility · Participation constraints

![Two fictional adult rental-service colleagues compare two plain pricing cards beside a compact photo light and a simple equipment-hours worksheet.](../two-plans-who-chooses-scene.jpg)

## Customers choose the menu, not the label

A menu of prices does not assign customers to the seller’s preferred categories. Economic teaching on screening asks whether each customer voluntarily chooses the intended offer and prefers participating to walking away. The rental service, projects and prices below are fictional.

## Opening decision

You design a new one-month menu for fictional Singapore photo-light rental service Clear Lamp. Both plans provide identical equipment, availability and support. Every eligible adult may choose either plan; the words light-use and high-use are internal design intentions, not eligibility restrictions or personal profiles.

Each customer has one indivisible project requiring either four or twelve equipment-hours. Four-hour customers value completing their project at S$38; twelve-hour customers at S$60. These total values already deduct time, travel and other non-price burdens. Customers know their own requirements; the seller cannot identify individuals’ types before choice.

For this deliberately fixed-project model, partial hours yield no project benefit and extra hours add no value. A customer chooses one plan for the whole project or does not rent, obtaining zero surplus. Accounts and hours cannot be pooled, resold or split across plans. There is no uncertainty about required hours.

Customers choose the lowest complete bill for their required hours and rent only if value minus that bill is strictly positive. The manager wants four-hour customers to strictly prefer A and twelve-hour customers to strictly prefer B, with both participating. Do not assume customers follow a plan label.

All prices include mandatory charges and applicable tax; there are no deposits, add-ons, auto-renewals or other payments. Terms are visible before choosing. Capacity, service and required safety checks are unchanged and sufficient. No offer has been announced or accepted, so a revision changes no existing contract.

**Student question:** Will the original menu make four-hour customers choose A and twelve-hour customers choose B voluntarily? Calculate both complete bills for each type and check participation, rather than assigning customers by the plan labels.

## Round two

Before launch, the team proposes including the first four equipment-hours in B’s monthly fee, while keeping that fee at S$25 and charging S$2 for each hour beyond four. A is unchanged. Included hours are an allowance, not a cash rebate; unused hours have no refund or transferable value.

The manager wants to retain this four-hour inclusion but restore the intended strict self-selection and positive participation. You may change only B’s monthly fee, F, offered identically to everyone. Keep A’s terms, B’s S$2 excess-hour charge, both project requirements and both total values unchanged.

Find all fees that satisfy those customer constraints and propose one clear whole-dollar fee inside the range. Do not introduce eligibility checks, hidden penalties or personal profiling. Type proportions and provider costs are not supplied, so the assignment is a feasible menu repair, not profit maximisation or a welfare ranking.

**Student question:** Does the S$25 fee with four included hours preserve self-selection? Find a range for B’s revised fee that gives both types a strict preferred plan and positive surplus, then verify one whole-dollar proposal.

## Debrief · Test every customer against both offers

Originally, four hours cost S$10 + 4 × S$5 = S$30 on A and S$25 + 4 × S$2 = S$33 on B. The four-hour type prefers A and receives S$38 − S$30 = S$8 surplus. B would also be affordable, but it is not the preferred choice.

Twelve hours cost S$70 on A and S$49 on B. The twelve-hour type prefers B, with S$60 − S$49 = S$11 surplus; A would exceed its total value. Thus both intended choices and both participation conditions hold under the original menu.

## Debrief · Included hours change the comparison

With B’s fee still S$25 and four hours included, the four-hour customer pays S$25, below A’s S$30. Its surplus is now S$13. Twelve hours cost S$25 + 8 × S$2 = S$41 on B, giving S$19 surplus. Both types choose B: the original separation disappears.

That is not cheating or mistaken use of a high-use plan. B is open to everyone and has become the cheaper offer for both project types. Calling it high-use, hiding it from some customers or asking people to declare a type does not repair the voluntary-choice incentives.

## Debrief · Write the three constraints

With four hours included, B’s four-hour bill is F. A still costs S$30. Strict preference for A therefore requires **F greater than S$30**. The four-hour customer’s participation remains satisfied because its S$38 value exceeds A’s S$30 bill.

B’s twelve-hour bill is F + S$16. Strict preference for B over A requires F + S$16 less than S$70, or F less than S$54. Positive participation is tighter: F + S$16 must be less than S$60, so **F must be less than S$44**.

Combining the constraints gives **S$30 less than F less than S$44**. At S$30 the four-hour type is indifferent between plans; at S$44 the twelve-hour type has zero surplus and declines under the stated rule. The strict objective excludes both endpoints. Whole-dollar feasible fees are S$31 through S$43.

## Debrief · Verify one transparent repair

Choose F = S$35, for example. Four-hour customers compare A at S$30 with B at S$35, choose A and retain S$8 surplus. Twelve-hour customers compare A at S$70 with B at S$35 + S$16 = S$51, choose B and retain S$9 surplus. Both choices are strict.

Display B as S$35 for the month including four equipment-hours, then S$2 per additional hour. Show complete four-hour and twelve-hour examples alongside A. Do not call the included hours costless: a monthly fee is payable. This is a new proposed menu, not permission to alter an accepted customer bill.

## Debrief · Participation is not self-selection

Participation compares an intended plan with not renting. Incentive compatibility compares that plan with the other available plan for the same customer. Both matter: after the inclusion change everyone still participates, but they no longer sort as intended. A plan can be attractive enough to join and too attractive to the other type.

The S$35 proposal is feasible, not uniquely best. Other fees inside the range also work and distribute surplus differently. Without customer proportions, operating costs or a stated wider objective, neither the highest fee nor the separated menu is established as optimal, profitable or fairer than letting both types choose B.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

Original fictional rental service and deterministic project model. Types need exactly four or twelve hours to obtain total values S$38 or S$60; partial projects have no benefit and extra hours have no value. Values include non-price burdens; no uncertainty, splitting, pooling or resale. Everyone can choose either plan. Participation requires strictly positive surplus and intended plan choice must be strict, so boundary ties fail the objective. Original bills: four hours A 30/B 33; twelve A 70/B 49. Revised B includes four hours and costs F plus S$2 per excess hour. Feasible fee range is strictly between S$30 and S$44; S$35 gives chosen bills 30/51 and surpluses 8/9. All charges and tax included, no renewal or existing promise altered. No customer shares, provider costs, global optimum or welfare claim supplied. Sources explain concepts and transparent disclosure, not actual demand or legal clearance for this fictional offer.

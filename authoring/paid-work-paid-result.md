# Paid for the work. Paid for the result?

## A result is also a measurement agreement

Outcome-linked service models connect payment to a measured result rather than only to specified work. Research on such models highlights the part played by customer resources and changing conditions. A clear number is not automatically a clear attribution rule.

The fictional offer below is noncritical workflow advice. It is not a legal contract template, an accounting-recognition exercise or a recommendation to reduce employee pay.

## Opening decision

A two-person Hong Kong startup helps small craft studios organise nonurgent booking administration. Its next four-week pilot includes a workflow review, a reusable planning sheet, two paid staff workshops and a documented handover. Both fee options have identical scope, quality checks and support limits. Staff retain normal pay, breaks and safe workloads; hours released would be used for other tasks, not treated as cash payroll savings. Personal customer details stay with the studio; the startup receives authorised aggregate workload records only.

The startup estimates HK$8,000 in direct delivery cost, including paid founder time, for either offer. Fixed charges HK$10,000 for completed agreed work. Outcome-linked charges HK$3,000 plus HK$80 for each credited reduction hour, floored at zero and capped at 200 hours. Both are payable on the same date after completion; assume no taxes, financing differences or other pilot fees. These are project contributions before common overhead, not whole-business profit.

The preliminary outcome proposal compares total administration hours with a 400-hour prior month. At 100 credited hours the fee would be HK$11,000, but that is an illustration, not a forecast. No contract has been signed. The founder wants an offer that customers will value and the startup can repeat. An earlier unpaid demonstration’s workload and implementation records will arrive before the final quote; the customer has not yet agreed a detailed baseline-adjustment rule.

## Round two

The earlier demonstration recorded 80 comparable booking batches and 400 administration hours before the advice, then 64 batches and 300 hours afterward. A temporary local demand decline reduced batches. The studio also assigned only half its staff to the planned training because it controlled their roster. Neither fact establishes how much effect the advice had. There was no comparison studio, random allocation or validated workload model; batch difficulty could also differ.

A raw comparison credits 100 hours. A deliberately simple volume adjustment assumes five baseline hours per batch, giving 64 × 5 = 320 expected hours and a 20-hour difference. This is a sensitivity illustration, not a validated counterfactual or an amount already owed. The demonstration was unpaid; there is no invoice to rewrite.

The customer now requires a defensible written measurement and input-responsibility plan before accepting any outcome-linked offer. You may quote the fixed pilot now, or postpone the quote for a separately funded design exercise estimated to cost the startup HK$2,000. That exercise could specify the baseline, workload adjustments, training responsibilities and an agreed review process; it cannot guarantee agreement or prove past causality. No free redesign or automatically accepted hybrid is available. The customer remains willing to consider the fixed offer, not committed to buy it.

## Calculate a fee, not a causal effect

Fixed contribution is HK$10,000 − HK$8,000 = HK$2,000. Under the provisional formula, raw 100 hours produce HK$3,000 + HK$8,000 = HK$11,000 and HK$3,000 contribution. Volume-adjusted 20 hours produce HK$3,000 + HK$1,600 = HK$4,600 and a HK$3,400 loss against direct cost. Neither calculation identifies the startup’s true impact.

At zero credited hours the outcome fee is HK$3,000 and direct-cost contribution is −HK$5,000; at the 200-hour cap they are HK$19,000 and HK$11,000. No probabilities are supplied, so an expected profit or a best risk-adjusted price cannot be computed.

## Decide who supplies the result

The startup can control the quality of its advice and agreed workshops. The studio controls staff availability and daily adoption; workload depends partly on demand. The charging architecture must say how those inputs enter the measured result, rather than silently transferring every variation to either party. A stipulation can allocate risk without proving causality.

Released hours may have operational value but are not cash savings at unchanged pay. Do not speed up staff unsafely, suppress records or select only easy batches to improve a payment measure.

## A bounded next step

Quote fixed if the immediate aim is to deliver a defined learning pilot while the attribution method remains unready. This retains delivery and cost risk and may be less attractive to a customer seeking shared outcome risk. It does not mean the service has no value.

Postpone and spend HK$2,000 if establishing a repeatable outcome model is worth that additional at-risk design cost. Agree credible workload measures, customer responsibilities, quality floors, data access, adjustment triggers and review costs before pricing again. Do not subtract the design cost from an assumed signed pilot or promise that the customer will accept new terms.

## Instructor note

Allow 2 minutes for the initial choice, 3 for the two baseline calculations and 6–8 for the revised offer. Ask what the startup sells, what it controls and what the customer must contribute. Keep revenue architecture separate from revenue recognition. A volume adjustment is a sensitivity check, not a causal estimate. Both immediate fixed delivery and paid preparation of an outcome model can be defensible; the unresolved tradeoff is learning speed versus measurement capability.

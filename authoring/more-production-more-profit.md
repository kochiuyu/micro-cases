# More production. More profit?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 253 -->

ACC-22 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Absorption and variable costing · Inventory profit bridge · Normal-capacity overhead

![Two fictional adult factory colleagues discuss blank report pages beside a plain desk organiser and closed cartons in a small workshop office.](../more-production-more-profit-scene.jpg)

## A reporting policy worth reading

The LEGO Group’s 2025 annual report describes including direct production costs and an appropriate share of production overhead in inventory, with fixed overhead allocated using normal production capacity. This is a published accounting policy, not evidence that its profit was inflated or that it overproduced.

The small factory below is original fiction, unrelated to LEGO. Its question is how to explain a period’s result without confusing goods made with goods sold.

## Opening decision

**Fictional classroom situation.** You are controller of a small Hong Kong manufacturer making one standard desk organiser. The board is reviewing two completed months before approving next month’s production plan. Reported operating profit has risen from HK$18,000 to HK$22,000, while sales volume and price are unchanged. The operations lead asks whether the result supports maintaining the higher production pace. The sales lead wants to understand whether customer economics improved.

You have one main slide for the board, with a supporting schedule allowed. A sales-margin bridge starts with the economics of units sold; a production-and-inventory bridge starts with units made and held; a reconciled combination connects both. Choose the most useful explanation, not a more favourable accounting policy. The production-and-stock schedule has not yet reached your desk. Do not invent its quantities or a cause for the change.

The same absorption-based reporting policy applies in both months. All costs shown are actual and complete for this exercise. The HK$40 comprises all variable manufacturing costs per unit; there are no variable selling costs. The HK$10,000 covers all other period costs. There are no returns, discounts, tax, finance costs, abnormal losses, work in progress or raw-material inventory changes. Do not infer cash receipts or payments from these expense figures.

## Evidence

|---|---:|---:|

**Student question:** Which bridge would you lead with for the board: sales margin, production and inventory, or a reconciled combination? Explain what the summary already establishes and which missing schedule you need before attributing the profit rise.

## Round two

The checked production-and-stock schedule arrives. There is no missing sale or change in product mix. All completed units are identical and meet required quality standards.

| Units · same product | Previous | Current |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening finished goods | 0 | 0 |
| Completed production | 800 | 1,000 |
| Sold | 800 | 800 |
| Closing finished goods | 0 | 200 |

The approved normal-capacity basis is **1,000 units per month**, already assessed over normal operating conditions including planned maintenance. It does not change just because this month’s output changes. Budgeted and actual fixed production overhead are both HK$20,000 per month. The fixed-overhead rate is therefore **HK$20 per unit**. At lower output, charge any unallocated overhead to that month; do not increase the rate. Current output is normal, not abnormally high.

Under the supplied **absorption** policy, finished goods include the HK$40 variable manufacturing cost plus HK$20 allocated fixed production overhead per unit. Under the supplementary internal **variable-costing** view, finished goods contain only HK$40 per unit and the entire HK$20,000 fixed production overhead is expensed each month. Other period costs are expensed in both views. The 200 closing units are saleable; their estimated net selling proceeds exceed their carrying cost, so no write-down is required on the supplied facts.

Operations says some stock may cover a forthcoming delivery peak. No approved demand forecast or delivery schedule is supplied, so that explanation needs checking. Neither a genuine customer need nor deliberate overproduction has been established. The board can retain the existing reporting policy and request a reconciled performance view before deciding the next production plan.

**Student question:** What explanation and follow-up would you now recommend to the board? Reconcile both months under absorption and variable costing, and decide whether the higher reported profit proves better selling performance or justifies producing more.

## Begin with what customers bought

Both months earn HK$80,000 revenue from 800 units at HK$100. The variable manufacturing cost of those sales is 800 × HK$40 = HK$32,000. Sales contribution is therefore HK$48,000 in both months. Subtract fixed production overhead of HK$20,000 and other period costs of HK$10,000: the internal variable-costing operating result is HK$18,000 in each month.

Round one supports a production-and-inventory investigation, or a combined bridge led by the unchanged sales contribution. It does not support claiming that customers bought more, paid more or cost less per unit. A sales-only slide is incomplete, but it is a useful starting check.

## Previous month: do not hide idle capacity in unit cost

Normal-capacity fixed overhead is HK$20,000 ÷ 1,000 = HK$20 per unit. Previous production absorbs 800 × HK$20 = HK$16,000, leaving HK$4,000 unallocated and expensed immediately. All 800 units are sold: absorption cost of sales is 800 × HK$60 = HK$48,000. Profit is HK$80,000 − HK$48,000 − HK$4,000 − HK$10,000 = **HK$18,000**.

Dividing the full HK$20,000 by only 800 units would produce HK$25 per unit. That is not the supplied policy. A quieter month does not justify loading unused-capacity cost into inventory.

## Current month: follow the unsold units

Current production costs HK$40,000 variable plus HK$20,000 fixed, a total HK$60,000. Of 1,000 completed units, 800 are sold and 200 remain. Closing inventory is 200 × HK$60 = HK$12,000. Absorption cost of sales is HK$60,000 − HK$12,000 = HK$48,000. There is no unallocated overhead this month. Profit is HK$80,000 − HK$48,000 − HK$10,000 = **HK$22,000**.

Of the HK$12,000 closing inventory, HK$8,000 is variable manufacturing cost and HK$4,000 is fixed production overhead. Variable costing also carries the HK$8,000 variable component forward. Only the HK$4,000 fixed component explains the difference between the two profit views; do not treat all HK$12,000 as extra profit.

## Connect the two views

| HK$ | Previous | Current |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sales revenue | 80,000 | 80,000 |
| Variable cost of units sold | 32,000 | 32,000 |
| Sales contribution | 48,000 | 48,000 |
| Variable-costing operating profit | 18,000 | 18,000 |
| Absorption-costing operating profit | 18,000 | 22,000 |

Current absorption profit HK$22,000 = variable-costing profit HK$18,000 + HK$4,000 fixed overhead in closing stock − HK$0 in opening stock. Previous closing and opening stocks were both zero, so its difference was zero. The entire HK$4,000 month-to-month reported increase is accounted for by this timing difference under the supplied facts.

An alternative bridge starts at previous absorption profit HK$18,000: unchanged sales contribution and period costs add zero; the amount of fixed overhead carried forward rises by HK$4,000; current absorption profit is HK$22,000. The prior HK$4,000 unallocated charge and the current HK$4,000 in stock are two parts of the reconciliation, not two separate HK$4,000 benefits to add.

## Recommend an explanation, not a production target

Present both views with a stock roll-forward. Say: reported profit rose HK$4,000 while sales contribution was unchanged; current stock carries HK$4,000 of fixed production cost into a later period. This is consistent with the supplied reporting policy. It is not by itself a false profit, a cash gain, an efficiency improvement or evidence of misconduct.

Do not approve extra production merely to repeat that accounting effect. Check the delivery forecast, stock age, storage and financing needs, expected selling proceeds and service risks before choosing output. Equally, do not reject useful stock automatically: preparing for credible demand or disruption can be sensible. Keep the required financial-reporting basis and use the internal view as a clearly labelled supplement, not a substitute.

## When the units leave stock

As a separate illustration, suppose the next month opens with these 200 units, makes 800 and sells 1,000, leaving no stock. Keep the same HK$100 selling price, HK$40 variable cost, HK$20 normal overhead rate, HK$20,000 actual fixed overhead and HK$10,000 other period costs. No write-down or other change occurs. This is a demonstration, not a demand forecast.

Variable-costing profit is HK$100,000 − HK$40,000 − HK$20,000 − HK$10,000 = HK$30,000. Absorption cost of sold units is HK$12,000 opening stock + HK$48,000 production = HK$60,000; add HK$4,000 unallocated overhead and HK$10,000 other costs, giving profit HK$26,000. The earlier HK$4,000 difference reverses. Across the current and illustrative next month, each view totals HK$48,000. Timing changed, not the combined result under these assumptions.

## Instructor note

Use two minutes for the summary, two for choosing a board-slide approach, four for the reveal and reconciliation, and four for the recommendation. Ask students to name the missing schedule before revealing it. A combined bridge is strongest, but a production-led approach is defensible if it also acknowledges unchanged sales contribution. Ask why prior-period unallocated overhead must not be capitalised and why HK$12,000 stock is not HK$12,000 extra profit.

Keep the optional next-month demonstration until after the second decision. Do not infer an optimal production quantity without demand and operating evidence. Unlike ACC-21, this is not an incremental sell-or-process choice after split-off. Unlike ACC-15, it does not reconstruct cash flow; unlike ACC-19, it does not redesign investment-centre incentives. The exercise applies a supplied simplified reporting policy, not personalised accounting or tax advice.

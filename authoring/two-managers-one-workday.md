# Two reasonable managers. One impossible instruction?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 293 -->

PPL-25 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Role conflict · Matrix working · Priority decisions

![A fictional analyst and two calm team leads discuss blank folders at a shared desk and planning board.](../two-managers-one-workday-scene.jpg)

## Two reporting lines need a way to resolve priorities

Matrix structures combine different lines of responsibility, such as functional expertise and project delivery. This can improve coordination across boundaries, but it also requires a way to reconcile competing priorities. Two legitimate requests can conflict even when neither manager is unreasonable.

The people, assignments, hours and deadlines in this exercise are invented. Public management texts and workplace guidance inform the distinction between conflicting instructions, unclear authority and excessive workload. Overseas workplace guidance is not presented as Hong Kong law or as a diagnosis of any employee.

## Opening decision

Jo is an analyst in a fictional Hong Kong professional-services team. Jo reports to a functional lead for methods and quality and a client-project lead for delivery. Both leads have legitimate authority in their own areas. On Friday, each asks Jo to finish a separate assignment on Monday. Neither can cancel the other lead’s work, and Jo cannot independently move either deadline.

The methods assignment needs six productive task hours, including preparation and checking. The client assignment also needs six such hours and can be done independently. Jo has six productive task hours on Monday and six on Tuesday after meetings, breaks and other duties. Those hours already include all available capacity. Neither assignment can be compressed, delegated or stripped of its checks. Extra unpaid or after-hours work is not an option.

You are Jo’s team coordinator. You can arrange paid coordination time already outside those task hours. Route A lets Jo seek approval from both leads for an exception on this occasion, with the director deciding if the leads cannot agree. Route B establishes a shared two-day priority plan, approved jointly by the leads, with the director as the named tie-breaker. Both routes can be completed before Monday and both protect Jo from having to disobey one instruction alone. You do not yet know why each lead requested Monday or whether this conflict is recurring.

**Student question:** Would you use an employee-brokered exception or a shared priority plan? Explain who must authorise a change, what you need to learn about the deadlines, and why asking Jo to work harder does not resolve the conflict.

## Round two

The client assignment is needed for a Tuesday 09:00 meeting and must be checked and ready by the end of Monday. The methods assignment supports a Wednesday workshop. Its Monday deadline was an internal buffer, not an external dependency. The functional lead confirms it can be completed on Tuesday without omitting checks or disrupting another person’s work. The client task can therefore occupy Monday and the methods task Tuesday, if the responsible leads approve the revised plan.

Records show three similar priority clashes in the preceding four weeks. This is evidence of a recurring coordination problem, not a measured estimate of lost hours or proof that either lead is at fault. Both leads support their own legitimate objectives and are willing to agree on a resolution process. The director remains available as the tie-breaker; no reporting line or job is being removed.

**Student question:** How would you schedule the two assignments and assign responsibility for future priority conflicts? Compare the two routes after the reveal, and state what the schedule does not prove about workload or either manager.

## Separate capacity from authority

Monday contains six productive task hours but the two requested assignments need twelve. The Monday shortfall is six hours. Across Monday and Tuesday, the twelve available hours equal the twelve required hours, but that arithmetic alone does not authorise moving a deadline. Jo faces incompatible timing instructions from two legitimate authorities; an unresolved decision right is not a lack of effort.

## Ask what makes a deadline necessary

Before the reveal, either route can be defensible if it secures timely approval and a fallback decision. Ask what each deliverable enables, whether another person depends on it, and which lead can approve a change. Do not silently rank client work above methods work. The client’s deadline becomes decisive here because of the verified Tuesday meeting, while the methods lead confirms flexibility for this specific assignment.

## A feasible plan, not spare capacity

Schedule the client assignment for Monday: 6 of 6 available task hours. Schedule the methods assignment for Tuesday: 6 of 6. Both include their required checks. The total is 12 of 12 hours, leaving no spare task capacity in the stated schedule. The plan resolves the timing clash only once the leads approve it; it does not demonstrate that Jo can absorb further work or that every future conflict can be solved within two days.

## Who owns the next conflict?

A one-off employee-brokered exception can be quick and preserves local judgement. It still requires the leads’ approval and the director’s fallback authority. A shared plan makes the competing requests visible together, assigns approval to the leads rather than Jo, and provides a defined escalation point. With three similar recent clashes, route B is the stronger recommendation for preventing repeated negotiation by the employee. That is a reasoned design choice, not evidence of a quantified productivity gain.

## Keep the strengths of both reporting lines

The functional lead protects methods and quality; the project lead protects client delivery. The solution is not to declare either role universally superior or abandon matrix working. Record the agreed sequence, the owner of each deadline and the director’s tie-breaking role. New demands should trigger an explicit trade-off rather than a private instruction that overwrites the shared plan. Review whether clashes recur and whether decisions arrive in time; do not invent a percentage improvement.

## Instructor note

Use about 3 minutes for the opening, 5 for the reveal and schedule, and 7 for responsibility and transfer. Ask students to identify both the numerical constraint and the authority constraint. A participant who prefers route A can offer a sound answer if it includes both approvals and escalation; the recurrence evidence strengthens the case for B without making A inherently irresponsible.

Distinguish role conflict from role ambiguity and workload. The required actions conflict on Monday; the method for reconciling them was unclear. There are enough stated task hours over two days only after an authorised deadline change. Avoid diagnosing stress, blaming a personality, treating overtime as free, or claiming that a matrix always fails. Optional extension: ask what should happen if both deadlines truly are fixed. The leads or director would then need to change authorised scope, resources or commitments, rather than push an impossible choice onto Jo.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

All people, assignments, hours, deadlines and recurrence figures are invented. Six productive task hours per day are net of other duties; coordination is funded separately and not double-counted. Each six-hour task includes preparation and checks. Neither manager can override the other, and Jo cannot change deadlines. The reveal verifies specific flexibility, not a universal rule that client work wins. Overseas public guidance is not Hong Kong legal advice.

- [OpenStax · Principles of Management · 4.3 Organizational Designs and Structures](https://openstax.org/books/principles-management/pages/4-3-organizational-designs-and-structures). Matrix discussion explains dual authority, cross-functional integration and coordination challenges. Read substantive matrix section; conceptual source, not evidence about the fictional team.
- [Linn C. Stuckenbruck · The Matrix Organization · Project Management Quarterly 10(3), 1979, 21–33](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/matrix-organization-structure-reason-evolution-1837). Historical practitioner discussion of priorities, conflicting guidance and responsibility allocation. Used to frame explicit decision rights, not to establish a universal causal productivity effect.
- [UK Health and Safety Executive · Management Standards: Role · updated 11 May 2021](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/role.htm). Addresses understanding roles, compatible requirements and systems for raising conflicts. UK guidance, not a statement of Hong Kong legal duties.
- [UK Health and Safety Executive · Management Standards: Demands · updated 11 May 2021](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/demands.htm). Discusses achievable demands in relation to agreed working hours and employees’ capacity. Supports separating workload from role conflict; not a clinical diagnosis or a legal conclusion.
- [SafeWork NSW · Role conflict or lack of role clarity](https://www.safework.nsw.gov.au/hazards-a-z/role-conflict-or-lack-of-role-clarity). Public guidance on incompatible expectations and unclear responsibilities. Helps distinguish the concepts and prompts organisational clarification; not imported as Hong Kong law.

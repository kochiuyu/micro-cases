# Which share would you sell?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 491 -->

FIN-46 · Hong Kong · 15 minutes · Disposition effect · Behavioural finance · Realised gains and losses · Trading costs · Cash constraints

![A Hong Kong investor compares two holdings on a laptop beside a building repair bill and a calculator.](../which-share-would-you-sell-scene.jpg)

## When sale costs changed in Hong Kong

In November 2023, Hong Kong reduced the stamp duty rate on ordinary stock sales and purchases from 0.13% to 0.1% per side. That changed a transaction cost, not the original price a particular investor had paid. The exercise below uses fictional all-in selling costs, not a market fee schedule.

## Opening decision

A building repair bill is due at Yan’s Kwun Tong flat. She needs HK$9,900 in cleared cash by the deadline. In this exercise her only way to raise it is to sell one whole lot of either A or B. She must keep the other lot until the end of the month.

Both lots have an executable sale quote of HK$10,000. The broker quotes the same HK$40 all-in sale cost for each and confirms either sale will settle before the bill is due. Yan keeps any cash left over after the bill.

Use the two equally likely month-end values in the exhibit. Compare cash left over plus the value of the lot she keeps. Ignore interest, dividends and any tax on gains or losses. The two account views are alternative histories, not two consecutive trades.

## Evidence

**Student question:** Which lot should Yan sell in Statement 1? Record your answer before comparing it with Statement 2. What, if anything, would make you change the sale?

## Round two

Before Yan sends an order, the broker revises only the all-in sale cost for B from HK$40 to HK$120. Sale quotes, settlement timing and both month-end forecasts stay the same. Yan still cannot borrow or sell a second lot.

For the sensitivity check, let the surcharge on B vary above the original HK$40 cost while A’s sale cost remains HK$40.

**Student question:** With the new quote, which sale pays the bill on time? How large can B’s surcharge be before that sale no longer covers the bill?

## Debrief · Count cash, not the gain

Either original sale leaves HK$9,960 in cleared cash and HK$60 after the bill. In Statement 1 a sale of A records a HK$1,960 gain after selling costs and a sale of B records a HK$2,040 loss. Statement 2 swaps these gains and losses, not the sale proceeds.

## Debrief · Choose what to keep

A’s expected month-end value is (HK$9,200 + HK$11,040) ÷ 2 = HK$10,120. B’s is (HK$9,200 + HK$10,800) ÷ 2 = HK$10,000. Selling B keeps A, which matches B in the weak state and gives HK$240 more in the strong state. With HK$60 left either way, selling B adds HK$120 in expected month-end wealth.

A rule of always selling the winner selects A in Statement 1 and B in Statement 2. That switch illustrates the disposition effect when past purchase prices do not change taxes, future values or other constraints. One choice on its own does not establish a bias.

## Debrief · Apply the cash deadline first

At a HK$120 all-in cost, selling B clears only HK$9,880, short of the bill by HK$20. A still clears HK$9,960. Selling A is now the only feasible sale even though keeping B gives up HK$120 in expected value before comparing sale costs.

With surcharge s, B clears HK$9,960 − s. It pays the bill if s is no more than HK$60, including exactly HK$60. For any feasible surcharge, selling B still adds HK$120 − s in expected wealth. Here the cash deadline binds before the expected-wealth advantage disappears.

## Instructor note

Allow 15 minutes: 3 for a private vote using only Statement 1, 3 to swap histories and vote again, 4 for the cash deadline and 5 to compare arguments. On the first pass, ask students to cover the Statement 2 row.

Ask which figures belong on a sale ticket before the purchase column is revealed: net cash by deadline and the payoff of the position kept. Discuss one setting where purchase history would matter, such as a tax rule that changes the cash value of a realised loss.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

The investor, bill, prices, selling costs, settlement confirmation and forecasts are fictional. Only the dated change to stamp duty is historical. This case is a classroom exercise, not investment advice.

- [Stock Exchange of Hong Kong circular CT/158/23, November 2023](https://www.hkex.com.hk/-/media/HKEX-Market/Services/Circulars-and-Notices/Participant-and-Members-Circulars/SEHK/2023/ce_SEHK_CT_158_2023.pdf). Confirms the date and rates of the reduction in stock-transfer stamp duty.
- [HKEX, Hong Kong securities transaction charges](https://www.hkex.com.hk/Services/Rules-and-Forms-and-Fees/Fees/Securities-(Hong-Kong)/Trading/Transaction?sc_lang=en). Distinguishes brokerage, levies, the trading fee and stamp duty; the case specifies its own all-in costs.
- [Odean (1998), Are Investors Reluctant to Realize Their Losses?](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/Papers%20current%20versions/AreInvestorsReluctant.pdf). Finds that investors in the brokerage-account sample realised gains more readily than losses; also considers tax-motivated selling.
- [Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean (2007), Evidence from Taiwan](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers%20current%20versions/istheaggregateinvestorreluctant_efm_2007.pdf). Tests the disposition effect in historical Taiwan Stock Exchange trades; it is not a measurement for Hong Kong.
- [Frydman and Rangel (2014), Purchase-price salience and the disposition effect](https://www.rnl.caltech.edu/publications/pdf/frydman2014a.pdf). Reports a weaker disposition effect in a laboratory condition that did not display acquisition prices.

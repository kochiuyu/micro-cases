# A project ends. What does the team learn?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 341 -->

PPL-29 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · After-action review · Team reflection · Transferable learning

![An event team compares a venue sketch and retrospective notes after a completed event.](../project-ends-team-learning-scene.jpg)

## Successful outcomes still deserve explanation

Debrief research examines how teams learn by reflecting together on a completed experience. Practical guidance recommends comparing intended and actual actions, examining explanations and turning lessons into follow-up work. It does not establish that a longer meeting is always better. The event, constraints and evidence below are fictional.

## Opening decision

A Hong Kong company’s six-person events team has delivered an internal workshop on time. All planned sessions ran, access arrangements worked and no safety incident was reported. The next workshop uses a different venue in two weeks. The team has one 90-minute paid meeting tomorrow, with everyone available; no overtime or extra meeting is funded.

A allocates 15 minutes to a brief operational debrief and 75 minutes to planning the next event. The debrief captures obvious fixes, useful practices and action owners. B allocates 45 minutes to a deeper, nonpunitive after-action review and 45 minutes to planning. A trained team member will facilitate either version, and notes and the run sheet are already available. Both routes preserve a minimum 45-minute block for essential access, safety and booking decisions. B displaces 30 minutes of optional programme refinement, not mandatory checks.

The manager wants to learn without turning success into a blame exercise. A protects more forward-planning time and may be sufficient if delivery followed the intended process. B gives more room to compare the plan, actual actions, differing explanations and conditions at the next venue, but cannot investigate every detail. No one has yet established why the event finished on time. Staff performance ratings and pay are outside this review.

## Evidence

A: 15 minutes debrief + 75 minutes planning = 90. B: 45 minutes deeper review + 45 minutes planning = 90. Both protect 45 minutes essential planning; B displaces 30 minutes optional refinement. Six team members attend within paid time. On-time completion alone does not explain the process.

**Student question:** Would you choose A or B for tomorrow’s meeting? Explain what would make deeper reflection worth its planning-time cost after an apparently successful event.

## Round two

The run sheet intended equipment to arrive at the main room at 08:15, with setup completed by 08:45 before the 09:00 opening. A handoff log shows delivery at 08:35 after the booking confirmation was sent to the wrong internal queue. Staff improvised by using an adjacent preparation room, with the venue’s permission, to assemble materials in parallel. The equipment was ready at 08:55 and the opening remained on time. No unsafe access or unpaid work was used.

One colleague credits the preparation room; another credits fast communication; a third notes that the first session needed fewer materials than expected. The records establish the timing and actions, not how much each factor caused the result. The team cannot observe what would have happened without the workaround. However, the next venue has no adjacent preparation room and requires all assembly in the main room. Copying that exact workaround is therefore not a feasible fallback there.

The team has three possible topics: the late delivery and booking-confirmation handoff; the mechanism by which parallel preparation recovered time; and a change in decoration colour that generated no recorded delay or complaint. It can examine the first two as a connected sequence within B’s 45 minutes, while documenting the third for later if needed. The existing 45-minute essential-planning block can assign and schedule a venue-specific setup check within already funded preparation work. There is no extra meeting allocation or evidence that the next event will fail.

**Student question:** Would you keep or change the meeting allocation? Select the issue deserving deeper review, distinguish evidence from competing explanations, and name one transferable action that does not assume the same workaround will be available.

## Answer the first decision: match depth to learning value

A is reasonable when a short review can capture the relevant lesson and forward planning has higher value. B becomes worthwhile when a success masks uncertain coordination or a condition likely to change. Do not equate reflection with a long complaint session, or treat an on-time result as proof that the original plan worked. The decision is the value of focused inquiry relative to 30 minutes of optional refinement.

## Answer the revealed decision: choose B and narrow its scope

I would choose B, focused on the confirmation-to-delivery handoff and the recovery sequence. These are connected, consequential and relevant to the next venue. Defer decoration unless new evidence makes it important. Compare the 08:15 arrival plan with the 08:35 log and the 08:55 readiness record. Invite each role to explain what information it had, rather than nominate a person to blame. Record uncertainty instead of forcing agreement about a single cause.

## A counterfactual is a question, not a fact

Ask what would change without the adjacent room, with a later arrival, or with the full material requirement. Those alternatives expose assumptions but do not measure lost minutes or prove which colleague saved the event. The known lesson is that this particular fallback cannot transfer unchanged. The broader hypotheses concern reliable confirmation, timely escalation and feasible setup sequencing. Check them against the next venue, rather than canonising the improvisation.

## Turn reflection into a testable action

Assign one booking owner to obtain and record explicit receipt of the delivery instruction from the correct queue before the next venue’s agreed cutoff. Use the essential-planning block to schedule a setup check with the next venue’s actual assembly rules, within funded preparation work. Preserve the team’s quick communication, but define who escalates an unconfirmed handoff and when. At the next event, compare planned and actual arrival/readiness and whether escalation worked; do not promise that a review guarantees success.

## Instructor note

Keep the opportunity cost visible: B leaves exactly the essential 45-minute planning block. The point is selective collective reflection after execution, not collecting information before a decision or blindly transferring a founder’s routine. Suggested review agenda: 5 minutes intended outcome, 10 minutes factual timeline, 15 minutes competing explanations and transfer conditions, 10 minutes actions and owners, 5 minutes uncertainties and checks. The 45 minutes is a fictional allocation, not a research-validated optimum. Learning notes are not personnel assessments.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

All events, logs, venues, staff accounts and allocations are original fiction. 15+75 = 45+45 = 90 minutes. B uses 30 additional review minutes and removes 30 optional planning minutes, not essential safety/access/booking work. With six participants the meeting remains nine person-hours; reallocating 30 minutes shifts three person-hours from planning to review. Planned arrival 08:15 to actual 08:35 is 20 minutes late; readiness 08:55 is 10 minutes after the 08:45 plan and five minutes before opening. This arithmetic does not prove the workaround saved ten minutes: material demand and other factors differed. No counterfactual duration, causal contribution, failure probability or performance gain is supplied. Existing records precede the decision; subsequent checks use already funded preparation. Emergency-response guidance supplies general learning concepts, not event-safety procedures or proof of transfer. Review syntheses and related institutional guidance are not five independent trials.

- [Tannenbaum and Cerasoli: Do Team and Individual Debriefs Enhance Performance?, 2013](https://cebma.org/assets/Uploads/Tannenbaum-Cerasoli.pdf). Original meta-analysis. Discusses alignment and shared reflection; design limitations and limited moderator evidence preclude promising a specific benefit from this meeting.
- [Healthcare Improvement Scotland: After Action Reviews](https://hisengage.scot/engaging-communities/participation-toolkit/after-action-reviews/). Primary practice guidance on objectives, actual events, explanations, specific actions and nonpunitive facilitation. Not an experiment.
- [WHO: Guidance for after action review, 2019](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-WHE-CPI-2019.4). Primary handbook on scope, timelines, facilitation and actions with owners/resources. Public full text also read via an openly hosted copy; public-health methods are not operational instructions for this event.
- [Stoto and colleagues: Getting the most from after action reviews, 2019](https://extranet.who.int/sph/sites/default/files/document-library/document/Getting%20the%20most%20from%20after%20action%20reviews%20to%20improve%20global%20health%20security.pdf). Original scholarly review and practice analysis. Addresses learning value, multiple perspectives and system explanations; overlaps other debrief literature rather than supplying an independent event trial.
- [NASA: Lessons Learned](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-professionals/appel-lessons-learned/). Primary account distinguishes collecting, recording, disseminating and applying lessons. Supports following reflection through to practice, not a forecast of performance.

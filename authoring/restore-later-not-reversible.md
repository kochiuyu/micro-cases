# Restore later. Lose something now?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 322 -->

SUS-27 · Fictional community land trust · 15–20 minutes · Ecological irreversibility · Recovery time · Option preservation

![A community land steward and ecologist stand between a paved disturbed plot and mature habitat, with no clearing underway.](../restore-later-not-reversible-scene.jpg)

## Restoration starts a process, not a rewind

The Convention on Biological Diversity’s Target 2 guidance distinguishes effective restoration activity from completed recovery and warns against using restoration potential to justify further degradation. IPBES likewise describes the long timescales and limits of recovering damaged ecosystems.

The Society for Ecological Restoration’s 2026 standards use reference conditions, measurable attributes and time-bound goals. A 2012 wetland meta-analysis found different recovery trajectories for different functions; it does not predict the woodland in this case. FAO’s 2026 monitoring discussion also distinguishes land under restoration from land fully recovered. The site-specific findings below are invented.

## Opening decision

**Fictional classroom situation.** A community land trust can license one of two two-year outdoor-market projects. A uses an already-paved, disturbed plot with removable stalls and no excavation. B uses a mature habitat patch and would clear its woody vegetation and regrade the ground for a larger market. Both provide useful local trading space. Only one operating team is available, so combining them is not an option.

For this exercise, land rights, required permissions, protected species, protected areas, safety and mandatory conservation duties have been checked. Neither proposal trades away a prohibition or legal safeguard. No work or licence has been committed. Permission to consider B is not a finding that ecological loss is acceptable. Existing trust services and habitat-care obligations remain funded; the extra income would support discretionary improvements, not avert insolvency.

A’s independently assessed installation and removal leave the paved plot in its current condition and do not damage the habitat patch. B includes a funded plan to remove the market after year 2 and restore native habitat on the cleared area. Its promoters describe the land use as “temporary”. The trustees have not yet seen the ecological review of that promise.

The quoted net contributions are HK$80,000 for A and HK$160,000 for B over the two-year licence. They use the same cash basis and deduct all differing project costs, removal and the required ring-fenced funding for subsequent care and monitoring. Long-term funding and land control are stipulated as adequate; the decision is not about an unfunded maintenance bill. The figures do not value habitat losses or establish complete social benefit.

## Evidence

**Student question:** Would you provisionally choose A or B? What evidence would you require before treating B’s promised restoration as making the habitat conversion reversible?

## Round two

The fictional site ecologist distinguishes vegetation cover from habitat function. B’s funded methods are credible for starting recovery, but comparable woody structure is estimated to take **30–50 years after restoration begins**, not two years. The range is a site-specific teaching assumption, not a universal recovery rate or guarantee.

The existing patch supplies a continuous network of mature tree cavities used by a locally occurring wildlife assemblage. Clearance removes that function immediately. The ecologist cannot reliably promise that planting will recreate the same cavity network and associated use, even after canopy structure improves. No assessed substitute patch or proven artificial-cavity package is available in this exercise. No protected-species restriction has been waived; this is an ecological-function finding within the stipulated lawful options.

B’s restoration begins at the end of year 2. Even its estimated structural recovery would therefore occur roughly **32–52 years from today**. Maintenance money and monitoring cannot remove that interval or guarantee the missing function. The market structures may be removable while their ecological effects are not readily reversible.

A remains available at HK$80,000 and leaves the existing patch intact. Its market can end after year 2 without requiring ecological reconstruction of that patch, allowing the trust to reconsider future uses with more evidence. This preserves a choice, not a guaranteed future profit or protection against every natural disturbance. No reliable money value or probability for the cavity-function loss has been supplied.

**Student question:** Would you retain or change your recommendation? State the financial benefit you give up or pursue, the ecological loss and recovery interval you accept or avoid, and how you would preserve options without claiming restoration is certain or worthless.

## Answer the opening question with conditions

Before the review, B offers HK$80,000 more net cash and a funded restoration programme. That can justify considering it, but “temporary” must describe more than a licence term. Ask which habitat attributes return, how long recovery takes, what evidence supports the trajectory, what cannot be recreated and what outcomes monitoring will test. A provisional A choice is reasonable if the trust prioritises keeping habitat intact while those questions remain unresolved.

Financial provision removes one important delivery concern; it does not make ecological recovery immediate. Nor does legal permission supply the trust’s environmental values. Neither project can be called reversible solely because the stalls are removable.

## Separate the clocks and the functions

B’s two-year commercial period and 30–50-year recovery estimate are different clocks. Starting restoration after year 2 implies approximately **32–52 years from today** for comparable woody structure, if the estimated trajectory holds. The cavity function is separately uncertain. A restored appearance or equal planted area would not demonstrate that it has returned.

Even eventual full recovery could not supply the habitat function during the intervening years. Monitoring may reveal failure and guide further action; it cannot retroactively restore those lost years. The assessment does not imply all restoration fails: partial recovery and improvement can still be valuable where damage already exists.

## A defensible recommendation and its cost

Recommend **A** here to earn HK$80,000 while avoiding an immediate loss that B cannot reliably reverse. The opportunity cost is B’s extra **HK$80,000** net contribution, not the whole HK$160,000. This recommendation prioritises the current function and retaining future choice over extra discretionary income; it is not a calculated universal welfare optimum.

A recommendation for B must openly accept the long interruption and uncertain functional recovery. It cannot label the project “no lasting loss” or count a funded planting promise as equivalent compensation. Trustees would need a defensible reason for accepting that residual loss; no numerical valuation supplied by the case resolves it. The ecological review strengthens the case for avoidance rather than making the foregone income disappear.

## Preserve options and measure outcomes

Under A, specify the paved footprint, removable installation, habitat exclusion, care responsibilities and a review before any future change. Revisit new evidence without promising that a later destructive project will be approved. Option preservation means retaining the ability to decide later, not obtaining that flexibility for free.

For any already-damaged site being restored, define reference conditions, measurable structure and function, responsible managers, review dates and funded adaptive action. Keep financial security and ecological evidence separate. If B were chosen, report interim losses and uncertainty honestly; monitoring is not a substitute for the initial decision about whether to cause the loss.

## Instructor note

Take a provisional vote before the ecological review. Ask students to distinguish licence duration, removal of structures, restoration activity, structural recovery and functional continuity. Require both the HK$80,000 incremental cash benefit and the 32–52-year timeline; neither is a complete environmental valuation.

Unlike SUS-15, this case is not about planting counts or which layout best connects existing patches. It concerns whether a new loss can be undone and the value of retaining a choice. All site facts, permissions, cash amounts, ecological functions and times are invented. The wetland study supports caution about extrapolating recovery, not the fictional woodland estimate. Do not infer a legal exemption or that every restoration project is ineffective.

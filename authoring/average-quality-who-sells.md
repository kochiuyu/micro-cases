# The average quality. Who still sells?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 371 -->

ECO-31 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Adverse selection · Participation · Costly verification

![A fictional Hong Kong used-camera marketplace organiser and technician examine two outwardly similar cameras at a plain inspection bench, with no legible labels or prices.](../average-quality-who-sells-scene.jpg)

## Checking condition before setting a price

Used-camera business MPB describes checking equipment’s functionality and cosmetic condition before finalising its quote. That illustrates a practical role for inspection; it does not establish that inspection is free or infallible.

The marketplace, values and inspection service below are invented. Our simplified model makes quality verification perfect so that students can isolate who chooses to trade and whether verification is worth its cost.

## Opening decision

You design a one-day trading rule for fictional Hong Kong camera marketplace Lens Lane. There are 100 potential sellers, each with one outwardly identical camera: 50 are high quality and 50 are low quality. Low quality is still usable. Sellers know their own camera’s type. Buyers know the initial numbers and the value table, but cannot identify any individual camera’s type before buying.

There are enough identical, risk-neutral buyers to buy every offered camera. They compete up to their expected value of a camera from the participating pool, and buy at that value if indifferent. Sellers participate only when their net receipt is at least their reservation value, the amount they require to give up the camera; they accept at equality. There is no bargaining, repair, reputation signal or other fee.

A colleague proposes an uninspected price based on the initial 50–50 mix. Before adopting it, test whether both seller types would actually supply cameras at that price. No sale has occurred yet.

## Evidence

| Type | Potential sellers | Buyer value if type known | Seller reservation value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| High quality | 50 | 3,000 | 2,400 |
| Low quality | 50 | 1,000 | 600 |

A price must be consistent with the quality of cameras actually offered, not just the original stock. A seller who declines keeps the camera. Buyers cannot rely on an unverifiable claim of high quality.

**Student question:** Can an offer based on the original average support trade by both quality groups? Calculate the offer and identify which sellers would accept.

## Round two

At the proposed average-value offer, the high-quality sellers decline. Buyers understand this participation response before any sale; they must reassess the uninspected pool rather than continue using the original mix.

Alternatively, the organiser can offer a separate trading session in which **every camera offered for sale must be inspected**. For this exercise the service perfectly and credibly reveals the type to all buyers, cannot be forged and does not change camera quality. Each seller knows its outcome in advance. Buyers then bid the stated value of the verified type.

Inspection costs **HK$200 per inspected camera**, paid by its seller. This fee exactly covers real inspection resources; there is no subsidy, fixed cost, capacity limit or other charge. Sellers can decline the inspected session and keep their cameras, but there is no uninspected trading lane within that session. Compare this complete rule with the separate no-inspection rule, not with an unspecified optional-certification system.

**Student question:** Choose the uninspected rule or the all-inspected rule on total gains from trade after inspection costs. Calculate participation and net gains under each, and explain whether the uninspected market disappears entirely.

## Debrief · The average changes who participates

The initial average buyer value is (50 × HK$3,000 + 50 × HK$1,000) ÷ 100 = **HK$2,000**. Low-quality sellers accept because HK$2,000 exceeds HK$600. High-quality sellers decline because it is below HK$2,400. An offer computed from all 100 cameras therefore cannot keep all 100 in the pool.

The feedback is the mechanism: price affects participation, participation affects the quality mix, and that mix affects what buyers will pay. This is adverse selection, not merely uncertainty about a fixed selection of goods.

## Debrief · A smaller market survives

Only low-quality cameras remain in the uninspected pool. Buyers’ value is now HK$1,000 per camera. At that price all 50 low-quality sellers still accept, while high-quality sellers stay out. The low-only outcome is consistent with its own price and composition.

No common uninspected price can restore both groups: high-quality sellers need at least HK$2,400, but the original mixed pool is worth only HK$2,000 to buyers. Nevertheless, **the market does not disappear**. Fifty useful low-quality trades survive. The lost opportunities are the high-quality trades.

## Debrief · Verification is not free

With mandatory inspection of every offered camera, high-quality sellers receive HK$3,000 − HK$200 = **HK$2,800 net**, above their HK$2,400 reservation value. Low-quality sellers receive HK$1,000 − HK$200 = **HK$800 net**, above HK$600. All 100 sellers participate under the stated rule.

Inspecting a low-quality camera does not make it high quality. Verification makes the two types distinguishable before purchase. Different justified prices can then support both types of trade.

## Debrief · Compare gains, not sale proceeds

Without inspection, total gains are 50 × (HK$1,000 − HK$600) = **HK$20,000**. With inspection, gains are 50 × (HK$3,000 − HK$2,400 − HK$200) + 50 × (HK$1,000 − HK$600 − HK$200) = **HK$30,000**. Choose the inspected rule between these two alternatives: net gains rise by HK$10,000.

The extra high-quality trades create HK$30,000 of gross gains, but inspecting all 100 cameras costs HK$20,000. Do not count only the cost of the newly traded high-quality cameras. Prices transfer money between buyers and sellers; buyer value minus the seller’s value of keeping the camera measures the gross gain. The inspection resource cost is deducted once.

## Instructor note

Minutes 0–4: calculate the proposed pooled offer and vote. Minutes 4–8: ask who is actually willing to sell and rebuild the buyer’s offer. Minutes 8–13: introduce inspection, check both types’ net receipts and calculate total gains. Minutes 13–18: separate high-quality exit from total market collapse.

Under competitive bids at buyer value, buyers earn zero surplus and sellers receive the net trading surplus. The gain comparison is total surplus, not platform revenue or sales turnover. Low-quality sellers earn less with universal inspection than in the low-only market, but still prefer selling to keeping their cameras. Participation is voluntary within each alternative regime.

Perfect verification and known reservation values are teaching assumptions, not descriptions of real inspection. Ask how errors, fixed costs or other trading routes could change the result. Optional or targeted certification is a different mechanism and might save resources; this case does not claim universal inspection is globally optimal. ECO-13 studies a seller’s costly signal; here the central object is the composition of the trading pool.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

All camera counts, values, bidding and participation rules and inspection costs are fictional. Buyers are risk neutral and numerous, bid expected value, and trade at equality. Reservation values represent the value of keeping the item. Quality is fixed and known privately to sellers. No transaction occurs at the inconsistent initial pooled offer. Perfect public verification is stipulated, not attributed to MPB. The two alternative sessions do not operate simultaneously. The inspection fee equals resource cost, so it is subtracted once from total surplus. No full market collapse, universal benefit from information or globally optimal certification scheme is claimed.

Explains how offered terms change seller participation and market quality. Its multi-quality complete-unravelling example is not the outcome of our two-type model.
- [OpenStax, Principles of Economics 3e, section16.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/16-1-the-problem-of-imperfect-information-and-asymmetric-information). Distinguishes imperfect from asymmetric information and discusses participation and mechanisms for reducing uncertainty. Fictional values are not copied.
- [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, Markets with Asymmetric Information (2001)](https://www.kva.se/app/uploads/2001/10/globalassets-priser-nobel-2001-scibackeken01.pdf). Scientific background explains a two-quality market in which high-quality sellers exit when their reservation value exceeds pooled buyer value. Contextual explanation of Akerlof, not a claim to have read the original1970 paper.
- [Jonathan Levin, Information and the market for lemons, RAND Journal of Economics32(4),2001,657–666](https://web.stanford.edu/~jdlevin/Papers/Lemons.pdf). Introduction and model show why information changes require assumptions: better private information need not monotonically reduce trade. Used to bound generalisations, not as empirical camera evidence.

Describes specialist checks and possible quote changes. Supports the practical inspection context only, not perfect detection or the fictional HK$200 cost.

# More reviews. A more trustworthy picture?

![Two fictional guesthouse colleagues discuss a plain tablet and guest folders at a welcoming reception desk.](../review-history-current-offer-scene.jpg)

## A genuine review still describes a particular visit

Review publishers need to consider both authenticity and relevance. A genuine account of an earlier stay can remain valuable history without describing every feature of today’s offer. Transparent dates, sorting rules and visible critical feedback help readers interpret that difference.

The guesthouse and every review below are fictional. References illustrate review-presentation principles and different platforms’ approaches, not permission to remove criticism or a legal opinion about Hong Kong publishing.

## Opening decision

A small guesthouse is redesigning the guest-feedback section on its own website, clearly identified as operator-run. It has permission to publish 100 genuine guest reviews, each with one overall score from 1 to 5. Their scores sum to 380. There are no fake reviews, incentives, duplicates, privacy violations or unlawful content in this teaching dataset. Reviews are not a representative survey of all guests.

Choose A: a newest-first full feed; or B: a disclosed topic view that guests can select, such as rooms, breakfast or location. Topic assignment follows a published content rule, not rating or sentiment; a review may appear under more than one topic but counts only once in the archive. Both views show visit dates and include criticism. Both provide a prominent full-feed control, total archive count and clear active-filter label. No review is edited or deleted, and the implementation costs are equal and funded.

The team has not yet connected review dates to the guesthouse’s operating history. It wants a useful default display and a summary that does not confuse all-time feedback with a guarantee about today’s stay.

## Evidence

| Opening fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Complete verified review archive | 100 reviews; total 380 score points |
| Rating scale | 1–5; one overall score per review |
| Available views | Newest first; or guest-selected topic with disclosed rule |
| Publication safeguards | All genuine reviews retained; negative and positive treated equally |

**Student question:** Would you default to the newest-first feed or the disclosed topic view? Explain how guests can see both useful detail and critical feedback without mistaking a selected view for the whole review record.

## Round two

A documented change took effect on 1 July: management changed, and the breakfast room and equipment were replaced before reopening. The building location and the uphill pedestrian approach did not change. The archive contains 80 stays before 1 July with scores totalling 288, and 20 stays since reopening with scores totalling 92. These are exhaustive, non-overlapping groups with no duplicate reviews.

Some older reviews praise the former breakfast team; others criticise its limited breakfast provision. Both describe the old service accurately. Older complaints about the uphill approach remain relevant to the unchanged location. Recent reviews include praise and criticism, including complaints about breakfast waiting. Verified current facility descriptions and access information are available independently of reviews. No evidence establishes that the management change caused the score difference or that every current guest has a better experience.

**Student question:** How would you redesign the review display after the documented change? State which histories and counts remain visible, how you would describe current facilities, and why neither the pooled score nor the recent score proves current service quality.

## Put the periods beside the scores

All-time mean: 380/100 = 3.8 out of 5. Before 1 July: 288/80 = 3.6 from 80 reviews. Since reopening: 92/20 = 4.6 from 20 reviews. The all-time mean is weighted by review counts, not the simple average of 3.6 and 4.6. These are summaries of submitted reviews, not representative guest satisfaction estimates or a causal evaluation.

## Preserve history without pretending nothing changed

Show a conspicuous dated change note and the before/after counts beside any period summaries. Keep the complete chronological archive easy to find, with both favourable and unfavourable history intact. A date filter may help guests explore the new service, but label it visibly and do not present its 20 reviews as all 100. Do not erase old praise while keeping old criticism, or erase old criticism while borrowing old praise.

## Relevance is feature-specific

A changed breakfast operation makes old breakfast descriptions historical, not false. The unchanged uphill approach remains relevant regardless of when a review was written. Retain that access information prominently in the factual current description; do not require a guest to discover it by searching old comments. Show current breakfast waiting criticism alongside recent praise. A blanket “everything before July is irrelevant” rule would lose useful context.

## Choose a useful default, not a flattering one

Newest-first is simple and gives immediate access to recent accounts, but still needs the dated change note. A topic view helps guests with a particular concern, but its rule and subset must be visible, and mixed-topic reviews must not inflate total counts. Either can work if the full record is easily accessible and the default is not designed to hide criticism. No supplied evidence predicts which layout increases bookings.

## Instructor note

0–4 minutes: choose the initial display and explain safeguards. 4–8 minutes: reveal the operating-history split and compute three means. 8–12 minutes: sort the breakfast and uphill-access examples by current relevance, not positive or negative sentiment. 12–15 minutes: sketch a truthful summary with counts, dates and a full-archive route.

Do not solve this by deleting all old reviews or declaring 4.6 the true quality of today’s stay. This differs from IS-11’s nonresponse mechanism: the task here is historical relevance within an existing archive. Nonrepresentativeness remains a limitation, not the new reveal. Third-party platforms have their own policies; this exercise changes only the fictional operator’s own display.

# Many possible users. One first market?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 187 -->

ENT-16 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Beachhead market · Buying process · Customer discovery

![Two founders compare plain customer folders and print samples at an office table.](../one-first-market-scene.jpg)

## A growing startup landscape, an original market choice

On 26 January 2026, the Hong Kong Government reported 5,221 startups in its 2025 survey. That describes an ecosystem, not demand for any particular product. The founders, segments and buying processes below are fictional.

## Opening decision

Two Hong Kong founders have built a shared application for tracking print-proof approvals. The same standard product can serve two potential segments: A, hotel groups coordinating materials across properties; or B, independent design studios handling repeated client proofs. Neither route requires a separate customer version or a different business model.

Their fictional desk-research lists contain 500 possible organisations in A and 80 in B. These are candidate organisations, not qualified leads, paying customers or estimates of market spending. Early conversations suggest a genuine coordination problem in both groups, but nobody has ordered.

The founders can fund either complete 12-week discovery-and-onboarding plan, including its stated technical preparation, but cannot run both properly with their current team. Six months of operating runway is already funded independently of sales. They must choose a first segment, not reject the other forever. They want evidence of a repeatable way to reach, onboard and serve paying customers.

## Round two

A buying-process map adds detail. In A, three hotel-group teams have agreed to discovery meetings. A local user can champion the tool, but central procurement and IT must approve purchase and live use; the expected review takes eight to 12 weeks and may take longer. The standard connector’s setup and review preparation are included in A’s funded plan. A sponsor can arrange a synthetic-data demonstration before approval, not authorise live deployment or guarantee a purchase. Each eventual approved account could support repeated work across several properties.

In B, six studio owners have agreed to discovery meetings. Each can decide on a standalone subscription without an external integration; their stated decision process takes one to two weeks. Those studios handle client-proof cycles weekly. If they find the tool useful, they are willing to discuss an introduction to another studio, but no referral or purchase is promised. Smaller accounts may generate less revenue each; prices, conversion rates and margins have not yet been established.

Both are feasible learning routes. A offers a route into larger organisational accounts and early process learning even before purchase. B offers the prospect of observing more complete buying-and-use cycles sooner. The small, deliberately contacted groups do not establish how every organisation in either segment behaves. Consent, data protection and required approvals remain in place; the team will not bypass them to accelerate a trial.

**Student question:** Would you retain or change your first segment after seeing the buying-process map? Set a 12-week learning milestone for the chosen route and a specific result that would make you revisit the other segment, without treating meetings or introductions as sales.

## Size is a starting clue

Five hundred candidate organisations is 6.25 times 80, but that is a ratio of list counts, not revenue potential or reachable demand. Customers differ in account value, approval effort, needs and access. A smaller first segment can support faster learning; a larger one can justify patient development. Neither list supplies a conversion forecast.

## Trace the buying journey

For A, distinguish the user, internal champion, purchasing authority and technical approval. Synthetic-data feedback can clarify the problem and procurement requirements without proving live adoption. For B, a shorter approval route and weekly use create an opportunity to test onboarding and repeated use, not proof of retention or willingness to pay.

## Choose a learning sequence

A team choosing A could require documented approval steps, an agreed evaluation scope and a credible next decision date by week 12. A team choosing B could seek evidence of an actual authorised purchase and repeated use at a sustainable support effort. Track refusals and failed assumptions too. Define in advance what missing evidence would trigger reconsideration; a first market is not a permanent exclusive commitment.

## Instructor note

Take an initial segment vote, then map each route from first contact to purchase and repeated use. Ask A advocates how they will learn during procurement, and B advocates why rapid small-account learning can lead to an economically viable business. Accept either argued sequence. Keep the product model fixed, unlike a bespoke-contract decision. Meeting counts and stated review times are bounded fictional observations, not representative surveys, guaranteed sales dates or evidence that introductions always travel.

# An incident declared. Who is directing the response?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 237 -->

GVR-20 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Incident governance · Delegated authority · Escalation

![Two distribution-office managers discuss a plain response folder beside a telephone and closed laptop.](../one-response-clear-authority-scene.jpg)

## Reliability also requires governance

In a Legislative Council reply on 8 May 2024, Hong Kong’s Government described stronger governance and independent testing for large or high-risk public IT systems. It did not prescribe the command structure in this case. Our distributor and service interruption are fictional; the exercise concerns organisational authority, not repairing software.

## Opening decision

A fictional Hong Kong office-supplies distributor’s customer order-status page is unavailable. Ordering, payments and fulfilment run on separate verified systems and continue normally. The technical team currently finds no indication of data loss, intrusion or physical risk; it continues checking rather than treating absence of evidence as certainty. Existing delivery promises and customer rights remain protected. A staffed phone service can provide verified status information without disclosing it to unauthorised callers.

The company has declared a non-safety service incident. Its trained operations manager and an experienced executive director are both available and qualified to coordinate it. The technical lead alone directs authorised engineering work; neither proposed coordinator performs unreviewed changes. The board retains oversight and decisions outside delegated limits. A communications lead gives verified updates, with no promised restoration time before evidence supports it. All roles have paid capacity, deputies and an agreed handover.

Choose a single coordinator: M delegates incident command to the operations manager, freeing the executive director for board decisions and external obligations. E names the executive director as incident commander and delegates ordinary board liaison to another director. M is closer to daily workflow; E has broader organisational authority but must avoid becoming a technical bottleneck. Both use one incident log, one task-allocation channel and the same escalation rules. Neither means allowing every director to issue instructions. The board has not yet made this appointment.

## Round two

Before the appointment is confirmed, one director asks an engineer to restore the status page immediately, while another asks the same engineer to produce a board chronology first. The technical lead is supervising a necessary verification step. The engineer has not acted on either competing request and records them in the incident log. The next decision is not which senior request to obey privately: the designated coordinator must sequence them with technical advice, while an available scribe prepares the chronology without interrupting verification.

The approved incident delegation permits total incremental response commitments up to HK$20,000. HK$12,000 is already committed. A qualified supplier offers additional support at HK$15,000, bringing the total to HK$27,000. This exceeds the delegation; no order has been placed. A quorate board decision can be obtained within ten minutes under the company’s established procedure. The coordinator may recommend the package but cannot approve it by splitting the purchase. Existing paid response capacity and safe phone support continue meanwhile; no emergency exception is assumed.

Updates are due every thirty minutes and immediately when a material change occurs. The next update must distinguish confirmed impact, work under way, unresolved facts and decisions required. Any sign of intrusion, data-integrity loss, a threatened contractual obligation or a risk to people triggers the established specialist and board escalation process immediately, rather than waiting for the next scheduled update. The scenario does not ask students to determine legal reporting deadlines. Specialist advice and applicable notifications remain mandatory where relevant.

**Student question:** Would you keep your chosen coordinator after the conflicting requests? Allocate the verification and chronology work, decide who can approve the supplier package, and specify the next update and escalation triggers.

## One command channel is not one person doing everything

The coordinator aligns priorities and removes obstacles. The technical lead controls authorised engineering; a scribe can assemble the board chronology from the common log. Directors retain their governance role but channel operational requests through the coordinator. The appointment, deputies and any handover must be explicit to everyone responding.

## A spending limit applies to the incident total

HK$12,000 plus HK$15,000 equals HK$27,000, HK$7,000 above the HK$20,000 delegation. Only HK$8,000 remains within the original limit; that does not authorise a HK$15,000 commitment. Seek the available board decision with a reasoned recommendation. Do not split the invoice or invent an emergency override. Approval, refusal and alternatives should be recorded.

## Report uncertainty without losing control

A useful update states known impact, checks under way, owners, next review time and the specific board decision needed. Do not turn a requested restoration time into a verified forecast. Escalation is immediate for the specified material risks, even between scheduled updates. The board should receive information that permits challenge, not a stream of conflicting technical orders.

## Instructor note

M and E can both work if responsibilities and capacity are clear. Seniority alone does not settle command suitability; delegation does not erase board accountability. IS-03 concerns technical recovery, while this case concerns coordinating authority during an interruption. The technical response itself is deliberately outside scope. Cyber-specific references provide role-design analogies, not a claim that this fictional outage is an attack or that UK reporting rules apply in Hong Kong.

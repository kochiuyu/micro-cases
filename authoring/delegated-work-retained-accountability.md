# The supplier promises compliance. Who remains accountable?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 357 -->

GVR-30 · Fictional repair-service firm · 15–20 minutes · Outsourcing governance · Assurance boundaries · Proportionate oversight

![An administrative service owner examines a three-party handoff at a quiet office desk.](../delegated-work-retained-accountability-scene.jpg)

## Oversight must follow the service chain

US banking guidance treats outsourcing as a change in delivery, not an escape from responsibility. Its 2023 framework permits periodic or continuous monitoring proportionate to risk; the 2024 community-bank guide adds practical questions about evidence, contractual access and orderly transitions. Operational-resilience guidance adds dependency mapping and tested alternatives.

ICO guidance separately explains ongoing controller oversight and subcontracting safeguards in UK personal-data processing. These sector- and jurisdiction-specific sources inform a governance exercise, not a legal conclusion about the fictional firm. The ICO pages are marked under review; local legal and privacy advice would still be necessary.

## Opening decision

**Fictional classroom situation.** Harbour Repairs will outsource appointment confirmations to ClearDesk. Harbour retains the service promise, internal owner Nia, accessible complaints and correction routes, and all applicable customer remedies. No payment, eligibility, safety triage or repair decision is delegated. Customers can always contact Harbour directly. Only the minimum authorised contact and booking details may be used; privacy/security requirements are mandatory under either design.

Nia must choose the principal oversight pattern for a six-month pilot. Periodic uses monthly independent reconciliation of every booking received and confirmation delivered, plus a monthly sample of 30 records checked against authorised instructions. Its complete additional oversight cost is HK$12,000 for the pilot. Exceptions uses continuously operating status checks, alerts for bookings unacknowledged after two hours, daily review of the alert queue and a weekly sample of 10 records. Its complete additional oversight cost is HK$18,000. Both costs include staff and systems, and both fit the HK$20,000 oversight budget. They exclude the identical service fee and mandatory controls, which are separately funded.

Both designs require immediate reporting of suspected privacy incidents, complaints and material service failures, plus prior approval of any subcontracting or material process change. Neither is permission to wait for a monthly meeting when a customer reports trouble. Nia owns escalation, correction and a tested in-house fallback. The fallback has capacity to handle the full pilot volume without missed appointments or extra customer charges.

ClearDesk supplies a compliance statement referring to its own confirmation platform and staff. Nia has not yet validated the statement’s date, detailed scope, evidence or exclusions. No failure probabilities or expected monetary losses are supplied. A faster status signal is not necessarily evidence that the right message reached the right person.

## Round two

During pre-launch mapping, ClearDesk discloses that RelayPrint will match booking identifiers to recipient details and dispatch confirmations. This is a critical accuracy and privacy step. No live customer data has been transferred and no customer has yet been harmed. ClearDesk’s statement expressly excludes RelayPrint; the status feed records only that ClearDesk handed a job onward. A green status therefore cannot establish correct delivery.

The proposed subcontracting has not been approved. ClearDesk is willing to provide the subcontractor’s controls, instruction chain, access arrangements, incident reporting and continuity evidence for review. That willingness is not proof that the controls work. Harbour can postpone the outsourced launch and run the fully funded in-house fallback without disrupting appointments.

A revised assurance scope can reconcile Harbour’s booking input to verified delivery and sample recipient matching across both providers. No revised price is yet available. The HK$20,000 oversight limit remains; any plan exceeding it requires a separate authorised budget decision. Contracts must preserve access, change approval, escalation, correction and workable exit across the chain, subject to legal review. Outsourcing does not make Harbour the only party with duties: each provider retains its own obligations.

**Student question:** Would you launch the outsourced service now? Design a proportionate assurance and escalation arrangement across Harbour, ClearDesk and RelayPrint, including what happens if adequate evidence or access is unavailable.

## Answer the first decision

Either principal pattern can be justified before the reveal. Periodic costs HK$12,000 and leaves HK$8,000 of the oversight budget; Exceptions costs HK$18,000 and leaves HK$2,000. The extra HK$6,000 buys earlier status visibility and a different sampling cadence, not demonstrated loss reduction. Weekly and monthly samples are not automatically comparable coverage without volumes and selection methods.

Prefer Exceptions provisionally if timely confirmation is the priority, but retain substantive checks. Validate statement scope, exclusions, date, independence and evidence; map every handoff and confirm access. Continuous reporting is not continuous assurance. Both retain immediate incident escalation, customer remedies and Nia’s accountability.

## Answer the revealed boundary

**Do not launch the outsourced chain yet.** Use the ready in-house fallback while Nia assesses and approves the proposed change. The excluded matching/dispatch step is precisely where status-only monitoring is blind. This is a pre-launch evidence gap, not proof that RelayPrint has already breached an obligation.

Require end-to-end reconciliation and samples that test recipient accuracy, timeliness and authorised handling across both providers. Test alert completeness, missing records and false green statuses. ClearDesk must manage its subcontractor and provide evidence; Nia must assess whether it meets Harbour’s requirements. Providers do not become duty-free because Harbour retains an accountable owner.

## Make responsibility operational

Nia signs off the risk assessment and launch decision, with privacy/legal specialists approving their areas. ClearDesk reports issues and coordinates RelayPrint’s corrective work. Harbour remains the customer-facing correction route and tracks closure, rather than sending customers down the chain. Specify named deputies, response deadlines, evidence retention and authority to invoke fallback.

Combine risk-based periodic evidence checks with prompt exception and change reporting where justified. Do not simply add both quoted prices: a revised combined scope requires a new costed proposal. If access, controls or evidence remain inadequate, keep the service in-house or assess another provider. Termination and migration must protect records, continuity and customer rights.

## Instructor note

Ask what each assurance artefact actually covers, not whether a supplier has a reassuring label. No detection probabilities, legal liability allocation or benefit-cost optimum can be calculated. Sampling counts alone do not establish confidence levels. The budget arithmetic is headroom, not money saved after adopting an unpriced redesign.

GVR-07 addresses remedy after a known supplier failure; this case is prospective assurance of a newly disclosed chain. Accept Periodic with strong risk-based reasoning before disclosure. After disclosure, require a launch gate and sufficient coverage, not a reflex demand for maximal surveillance or immediate destructive termination.

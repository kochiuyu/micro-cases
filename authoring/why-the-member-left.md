# The customer left. Which offer deserves a return?

## The reason for leaving changes the remedy

Customer win-back research distinguishes the reason a relationship ended from the offer used to restart it. A discount and a repaired service barrier are not interchangeable. The studio, records and campaign below are fictional; research supplies the question, not a prediction that any former member will return.

## Opening decision

You manage fictional Harbour Step, a Hong Kong fitness studio. One hundred and twenty adults ended their monthly membership during the last four weeks. Each voluntarily recorded one main non-medical cancellation reason. The business may use the retained reason for the stated service-improvement and permitted marketing purposes; no health information or sensitive profile is collected.

Twenty have opted out of all marketing and must receive none. The other one hundred have valid, current permission covering one studio-offer email this month. Permission, secure handling and suppression checks are already funded. Membership cancellation is not treated as marketing consent or withdrawal: the separate permission record governs contact.

You can prepare D, one identical email advertising 20% off the first returning month, or M, one campaign with reason-matched message versions and a no-message branch when no relevant remedy exists. Neither route sends more than one email per person. Both preserve opt-outs and ordinary membership access, without pressure to give new consent.

The regular monthly price is HK$600; the proposed first-month discount makes it HK$480. There is no automatic renewal, joining fee or minimum term in either offer. Any later month requires a fresh purchase at the disclosed normal price. A discount costs HK$120 per redemption before considering other costs; no return rate is known.

D costs HK$1,500 to prepare; M costs HK$3,000. Either fits the campaign budget, but M uses HK$1,500 that could otherwise fund optional signage. Staff have not yet reconciled cancellation reasons with contact permissions or the approved timetable. Nothing has been sent, and neither plan may claim a service change that has not actually been approved.

## Round two

The reconciled records show eighty left after the studio removed its 6 p.m. classes; twelve of them opted out. Thirty cited the monthly price; six opted out. Ten moved beyond practical travelling distance; two opted out. These categories are mutually exclusive main reasons, not a claim that people can never have several motives.

The sixty-eight contactable timetable leavers recorded that 6 p.m. was their feasible weekday window; the current later classes did not fit. The twenty-four contactable price leavers recorded that available times worked for them. For this bounded planning exercise those constraints remain current. They are not inferred from age, occupation, health or clicks.

Operations has now approved and funded restored 6 p.m. classes on Mondays and Wednesdays from next month, with sufficient staffed capacity for all sixty-eight timetable leavers if they chose to return. Existing members keep their bookings and access. This schedule is available under either campaign; its cost is identical and separate from the preparation budgets.

The eight contactable relocated people cannot use the studio from their new locations under the supplied facts. There is no online service, nearby partner or planned branch to offer them. No one has promised to return, and the studio has no experimental evidence about either email. Updated personal circumstances can be volunteered without changing anyone’s contact rights.

**Student question:** Calculate the contactable group sizes and design M’s message or no-message branch for each. Explain why D’s discount alone misses the timetable issue, even though both campaigns can link to the same public schedule. What would you measure before calling M a profitable win-back strategy?

## Debrief · Start with the remaining audience

Timetable: 80 − 12 = 68. Price: 30 − 6 = 24. Relocation: 10 − 2 = 8. These sum to one hundred authorised contacts; the twenty opt-outs stay suppressed. Timetable leavers are 68% of that authorised audience, not 80%. Counts describe this fictional cohort, not the fitness market.

M need not use every available permission. It can send one relevant email to each of sixty-eight timetable leavers and twenty-four price leavers, ninety-two messages at most, and send none to the eight relocated people. Any further opt-out before dispatch reduces those counts. A smaller send total is not itself evidence of higher effectiveness.

## Debrief · Repair and communicate the relevant barrier

For timetable leavers, lead with the genuine change: “6 p.m. classes return on Mondays and Wednesdays next month. View the timetable if that suits you.” Disclose the HK$600 monthly price and normal booking terms. Do not claim a discount alone creates a feasible class, or imply the recipient must explain their personal circumstances.

For price leavers, M can offer the approved HK$480 first returning month, with the later HK$600 price, fresh-purchase requirement and full offer terms clear. The current class times already fit their stated window. A temporary reduction may help an initial purchase; it does not establish affordability or retention after the discount ends.

## Debrief · Sometimes there is no relevant invitation

Do not invent an online class or nearby branch for relocated people. No message is the appropriate M branch when the studio cannot address their supplied practical barrier. Keep any necessary suppression record protected; neither a “last chance” pitch nor a marketing survey is a way around an opt-out.

The records support a reason-matched hypothesis, not permanent labels. Someone may later volunteer a different need or return without an email. Use only authorised information and allow correction. Do not interpret every lapsed member as price-sensitive or collect medical details to make this campaign more granular.

## Debrief · Compare the whole campaign honestly

M is better aligned with the supplied reasons and avoids offering a price remedy where timing or location is the obstacle. D remains cheaper to prepare and might still inform people through its schedule link. The evidence does not establish M’s incremental return, or prove that every D recipient would misunderstand or ignore the offer.

The restored timetable is a real service change under both plans, not a benefit caused by segmentation. M’s extra HK$1,500 preparation cost and any redeemed HK$120 discounts must remain visible. No wage, service-margin, response-rate or later-purchase data support a profit calculation. Do not multiply ninety-two messages by an invented conversion rate.

## Debrief · Distinguish a return from an incremental return

Track authorised recipients, delivered messages, actual returning purchases, discount redemptions and later full-price purchases separately by recorded reason. A click is not a renewed membership. A return after an email is not necessarily caused by it: the public restored timetable could attract people who were never contacted.

A properly authorised future test could randomise relevant versions or a no-email comparison within reason groups while keeping timetable access and rights identical. Define the outcome window and costs before testing, and honour withdrawals. No test is run here. Report uncertainty rather than calling a descriptive cohort difference a causal effect.

## Instructor note

MKT-18 changes communication cadence; MKT-30 separates shopping occasions within people. This case holds the one-contact limit fixed and asks whether a reactivation remedy addresses the reason a relationship ended. Require group-specific copy, a defensible no-message branch and an explicit distinction between service repair and promotional pricing.

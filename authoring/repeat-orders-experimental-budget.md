## A purchase and an adoption decision

A paid corporate trial is a real purchase. It need not mean that the operating business has approved routine use or budgeted recurring orders. Corporate-startup guidance and venture-client programmes distinguish prototype purchasing, validation and subsequent supplier arrangements. The fictional decision below asks which buying process has actually been completed.

## Opening decision

Fictional FoldWorks, a Hong Kong packaging venture, has delivered three paid batches to one corporate customer in three consecutive months. Each batch sold for HK$90,000 and met its agreed specifications. Payment is genuine; there are no unpaid invoices or outstanding remedies. These are three purchases by one organisation, not three independent customers.

The founders are considering a permanent production team, H, rather than buying bounded production capacity from an established independent manufacturer, T. No employment offers or new manufacturing commitments have been made. Both arrangements can meet identical quality, safety, working-condition and delivery requirements for the next six months.

H costs HK$240,000 over the six-month comparison for onboarding, wages and all related employment costs, plus HK$20,000 materials and fulfilment per batch. T costs HK$60,000 per completed batch, including all production, materials and fulfilment, without a standing fee. Both can supply up to twelve batches in that period.

Both options are affordable within earmarked funding; existing obligations and protected reserves are separately covered. H is genuinely permanent employment, not automatic dismissal at month six. Any later continuation or lawful exit obligations require separate funded planning. T is a professionally reviewed manufacturing contract, not disguised employment or a device to remove workers’ rights.

The founders’ worksheet assumes eight further batches over the next six months at HK$90,000 each. It extrapolates from the recent relationship; it is not an eight-batch order. Both suppliers can honour the delivery dates under consideration. No forecast probabilities or other customer orders are supplied.

## Round two

The customer confirms that all three past orders came from its innovation team’s finite experimental budget. That team has authorised two more batches at HK$90,000 each for the next six months. Those purchases are firm on the stated quality and delivery terms. Nothing beyond those two batches is committed.

The operating procurement unit has not approved routine supply. It is willing to evaluate the next two batches against written packing-line compatibility, damage and delivery criteria. The operating manager will review results with procurement and finance before deciding whether to allocate an operating budget and negotiate a routine supply agreement.

The criteria, responsible reviewers and review meeting after the second batch are confirmed, but success does not guarantee budget approval, supplier appointment or any volume. T is permitted for these batches and does not obstruct the review. No certification can be borrowed, no assessment bypassed and no future order assumed from the meeting date.

**Student question:** Which capacity commitment now fits the evidence? Compare costs for the two authorised batches and identify the additional evidence needed before hiring. Explain why the innovation purchases remain meaningful without proving a repeatable operating sales process.

## Debrief · The eight-batch projection

At eight batches, H costs 240,000 + 8 × 20,000 = HK$400,000. T costs 8 × 60,000 = HK$480,000. H would save HK$80,000 within this six-month comparison if all eight occur. Projected revenue is HK$720,000 for either, but the projection is not a purchase commitment.

The cost crossover solves 240,000 + 20,000n = 60,000n, giving n = 6 batches. H is cheaper above six, T below six, and they tie at six. This is a conditional cost threshold, not an approval criterion or evidence that seven batches will be sold.

## Debrief · Reassess at the authorised scope

At two batches, H costs HK$280,000 and T HK$120,000. T commits HK$160,000 less to the specified work. Both produce HK$180,000 sales on successful delivery. Sales minus these listed costs are negative HK$100,000 for H and positive HK$60,000 for T, before separately budgeted common costs and tax.

Choose T for the authorised batches while pursuing the operating review. It preserves the promised quality and delivery without making the larger standing commitment now. This is not a finding that permanent employment is undesirable: its cost advantage requires volume evidence not yet present, and the longer-term decision needs a wider budget.

## Debrief · Payment is evidence, not every kind of evidence

The completed purchases show that an authorised innovation buyer paid for delivered batches and accepted the agreed specifications. They are stronger than compliments or unpriced interest. They do not show that the operating unit has adopted the product, secured recurring funding or approved the venture for routine supply.

Two further authorised batches strengthen the near-term workload. Repeated payments from the same experimental budget are not independent confirmations of an operating buying process. Nor does the budget boundary prove that adoption will fail: the customer has supplied a credible evaluation route and named decision functions.

## Debrief · Turn the route into a checkpoint

Fulfil the two batches and preserve the agreed evidence on line compatibility, damage and delivery. Ask the operating manager, procurement and finance to document results, remaining supplier requirements, the responsible budget holder and the decision timetable. A technically successful batch and an approved operating budget answer different questions.

Before expanding capacity, obtain an authorised supply arrangement with meaningful volume, timing, price and conditions, and recalculate costs and cash requirements. Supplier registration alone need not promise purchases. If approval is delayed or refused, do not silently produce unrequested batches or present further experimental orders as routine adoption.

## Debrief · Keep the commitment honest

T’s higher cost per batch is a genuine tradeoff if volume later rises. It buys a bounded commitment today, not free future capacity: its twelve-batch capacity is only available within the stated period and terms. Hiring later may require new lead times and quotes, which must be checked rather than assumed away.

This is not a six-month lifetime valuation of permanent employment. H’s continuation, training benefits, retention and any lawful exit obligations beyond the comparison need separate assessment. No employee is being dismissed in this decision. Do not improve the calculation by removing safety checks, lawful pay or existing customer obligations.

## Instructor note

First separate three observations: paid historical batches, two authorised future batches and eight forecast batches. Then draw the customer’s innovation team, operating manager, procurement and finance as different decision functions within one buyer. The learning problem is the budget and approval transition, not whether payment occurred.

ENT-02 moves from free prototype interest to an initial paid trial. STR-10 weighs customer concentration and a bounded commercial minimum. Here payment and repeated delivery already exist, but routine organisational adoption remains unapproved. Programme sources illustrate possible processes, not conversion probabilities or rules governing this fictional customer.

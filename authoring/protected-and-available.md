# One bank. Two kinds of safety.

Case 98 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Finance · Cash concentration · Payment access

![An older man in a sage shirt listens as a woman in a terracotta cardigan gestures between two open folders at a print-shop desk, with paper stacks and printing equipment behind them.](../protected-and-available-scene.jpg)

## In the news

On 1 October 2024, Hong Kong raised its Deposit Protection Scheme limit to HK$800,000 per depositor per member bank.

That is not a separate allowance for each account or a promise of immediate spending access. The company, banks and rates below are fictional.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. Harbour Print Limited is deciding where to keep HK$1.2 million of its own operating reserve. It can keep everything at Bank A or divide it equally between Bank A and Bank B. Both relationships and permissions can be arranged in advance; no covenant requires a single bank.

The company is an eligible depositor. These are conventional on-demand Hong Kong-dollar deposits at Hong Kong offices of two confirmed, separate Deposit Protection Scheme member banks. There are no other company balances there or restrictions on the funds.

Keeping the reserve together offers more illustrated interest and less administration. Dividing it reduces concentration. The task is to choose a policy, not to open accounts or move real money.

## Evidence

| Planning comparison | Keep together | Divide equally |
|---|---|---|
| Starting allocation | HK$1.2m at Bank A | HK$600,000 at each bank |
| Fictional annual rates | A: 3% | A: 3%; B: 2.8% |
| Annual interest illustration | HK$36,000 | HK$34,800 |
| Additional annual administration cost | None | HK$1,800 |
| Starting principal within protection ceilings | HK$800,000 | HK$1.2m across both banks |

Interest assumes unchanged balances for a year, not actual receipts after withdrawals. Common costs are unchanged. Dividing sacrifices HK$1,200 of illustrated interest and adds HK$1,800 cost: **HK$3,000 annually in this comparison**.

Accrued interest counts within the protection limit, not on top of it. The HK$600,000 allocations leave headroom. Keeping HK$400,000 above one bank’s ceiling does not mean it is automatically lost if that bank fails; recovery would be uncertain. No failure probability is supplied.

**Student question:** Keep the reserve together or divide it? What would justify the extra HK$3,000 annual comparison cost, and what evidence could reverse your choice?

## Round two

A treasury review confirms that Bank B can pay the company’s suppliers directly using its already-authorised mandate, documented beneficiary details and available signatories. It need not send money back through Bank A.

Now assess this hypothetical interruption at the starting balances: payments from Bank A are unavailable until the next business day. Bank B, the relevant settlement routes, company access and signatories, and recipient banks remain operational. An essential HK$200,000 supplier payment must arrive today. No borrowing, other cash pool or third-party funding is available.

Under the split arrangement, Bank B’s HK$600,000 was deposited before the interruption; it cannot be moved out of A afterwards. Bank A’s money still exists and neither bank is assumed to have failed. This is a scenario to examine, not an allegation or forecast about any bank.

**Student question:** Which arrangement can fund today’s HK$200,000 payment in this scenario? Does deposit protection itself solve the access problem, and does this change what you would pay for the reserve arrangement?

## Keep the return comparison separate from the protection question

Keeping HK$1.2m at 3% gives HK$36,000 in the unchanged-balance illustration. Splitting gives HK$18,000 at A plus HK$16,800 at B, or HK$34,800. Including the extra HK$1,800 administration cost makes the comparison difference HK$3,000.

That is a visible cost of the alternative arrangement, not a guaranteed loss of profit. Actual interest changes as balances and rates change. No supplied probability lets us calculate an expected loss that proves one policy optimal.

## Locate the money that can actually make the payment

Under the split policy, Bank B can send HK$200,000 directly and retain HK$400,000. HK$600,000 remains at A, temporarily unavailable through the stated payment route. Total cash after paying the supplier is HK$1m.

Under the together policy, the full HK$1.2m remains at A but cannot fund the deadline through the available arrangements in this scenario. That is an access problem, not a loss of the whole balance. Deposit protection is not an outage-payment service.

## Choose the exposure deliberately

The split policy meets this particular payment requirement while increasing the starting principal inside separate protection ceilings. Its value depends on the business’s need for reliable payment access and its willingness to incur the stated ongoing cost.

Keeping funds together retains the better illustrated return and simpler administration, but also the described exposure. A policy recommendation must address whether management can accept that interruption risk within its obligations; it is not permission to disregard a due payment.

A shared-network interruption, unavailable signatories or a changed balance could defeat the second route too. Two banks do not guarantee continuity in every situation.

## Review the arrangement as balances and needs change

Confirm the legal member-bank identities, actual deposit eligibility and total claims including interest, rather than counting account numbers. Review both the money available for essential payments and the people and routes authorised to send it.

Keep the three questions distinct: how much is protected, when can it be used, and what does the arrangement cost? Revisit the answer when rates, balances, payment needs or operating arrangements change, without inferring any named bank is unsafe.

## Instructor note

Take a provisional policy vote before the interruption scenario. The reveal establishes an independently usable payment route, not merely a second account or a successful token transfer. The split is already a priced option; do not add a free hybrid or an unstated emergency loan.

The annual return calculation is a separate unchanged-balance illustration. The interruption starts with HK$600,000 at each bank under the split policy, before the HK$200,000 payment. Do not subtract that payment twice or count the temporarily inaccessible A balance as lost.

For the exit question, replace the two member banks with two accounts at A. The company still has one HK$800,000 ceiling there and the same payment-channel dependence. Accrued interest also counts within the ceiling. No bank-failure probability, recovery rate or compensation deadline has been supplied for numerical optimisation.

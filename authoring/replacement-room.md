# A room is ready. Is the guest’s trip restored?

Case 25 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Service recovery · Guest needs

![A hotel duty manager and receptionist discuss replacement-room arrangements with a waiting traveler and suitcase.](../replacement-room-scene.jpg)

## In the news

On 15 June 2026, Hong Kong’s Consumer Council described a guest moved to another building because the booked guesthouse was full. The substitute lacked the advertised lockable locker. The complaint does not establish deliberate overbooking, but it raises a service question: when is a replacement actually equivalent for the person using it?

## Opening decision

You are the duty manager of a fictional Hong Kong hotel on an event night. A booking-system error has left one confirmed guest without a room. The guest has arrived. No room can be made available in your hotel tonight. You apologize, take responsibility and must propose a workable replacement now.

Two nearby hotels have each confirmed one suitable room and will hold it for 15 minutes. Both meet the booked room type and quality, with no accessibility requirement or other special requirement yet disclosed. Your hotel will pay the replacement and transfer costs; the guest’s agreed original price does not increase. You may approve either package within your authority, but cannot treat silence as consent. If neither works, escalate while continuing to help the guest.

## Evidence

Two confirmed options · fictional recovery packages

| Item | Hotel A | Hotel B |
|---|---|---|
| Replacement room paid by your hotel | HK$900 | HK$1,300 |
| Transfer paid by your hotel | HK$100 taxi | No cost: next door |
| Immediate hotel outlay | HK$1,000 | HK$1,300 |
| Additional gesture | HK$500 future-stay voucher | No voucher |

A is 20 minutes away by taxi tonight; B is next door. Voucher is only for a later stay, not cash, and expires after a year. Face value is neither guaranteed guest value nor expected hotel cost. Original booking receipt is unchanged.

**Student question:** Which package would you offer first? Before recommending it, what would you ask the guest about the rest of their trip?

## Round two

The guest is leaving Hong Kong tomorrow on a 6am train from the station beside your hotel. They want to reach the station by 5.30am, prefer not to depend on a taxi at that hour, and do not expect to return within a year. You verify that Hotel B is a five-minute walk from that station. From A, the receiving hotel estimates a 35-minute taxi trip at that hour; you would arrange and pay it, but cannot guarantee an exact arrival time.

Both rooms remain held. Hotel B has confirmed early checkout. The guest says B would solve the immediate travel problem. No other room or price fact changes. This is a fictional service-recovery choice, not the Consumer Council’s reported case.

**Student question:** Would you change your recommendation? Explain the extra cost you would authorize, then give the guest a clear offer and confirm the practical arrangements.

## Debrief · Repair the trip, not the headline

Restore the trip, not just a room category. Before the reveal, A’s lower immediate cost and larger-looking gesture can seem attractive. Yet a replacement is useful only if it fits the guest’s plans. The guest’s early departure makes location, checkout and transfer reliability part of the service being restored. B now fits the stated preference better.

Separate price, cost and value. B costs your hotel HK$300 more immediately: HK$1,300 rather than HK$1,000. The comparison excludes any additional morning taxi for A, whose price is not supplied. The HK$500 voucher is not cash and is unlikely to help a guest who will not return before it expires. It should not be added to the HK$1,000 outlay as though it were an immediate HK$500 payment or a guaranteed HK$500 benefit.

Make an informed arrangement, not an imposed move. Offer B without an extra charge to the guest, explain the early-checkout arrangement and obtain agreement. Confirm the reservation with the receiving hotel, the named reception contact and how luggage will be moved. Stay responsible until the guest is checked in. A sincere apology and follow-up support the practical remedy; neither replaces it.

If B were unavailable or the guest rejected both packages, escalate rather than promising a room that is not secured. A different guest with flexible plans might genuinely prefer A and the voucher. The case does not establish a universal compensation amount or allow staff to displace the least valuable customer automatically.

## Back to the evidence · Useful to whom?

The Consumer Council’s complaint illustrates how changed facilities can undermine a booking even when a room is provided. RTHK’s report of the same release emphasizes clear responsibilities and proactive communication; it is not a second independent complaint sample. Hong Kong hospitality research and teaching guidance also emphasize matching recovery to the customer’s circumstances.

A published hotel-overbooking study’s public abstract found different reactions to cash and voucher compensation, without a corresponding significant improvement in return intentions from overcompensation. That is a reason not to equate a larger gesture with guaranteed loyalty. It is not a formula for what this fictional guest must accept. No legal entitlement or real-hotel policy is being asserted.

## Instructor note

Minutes 0–3: ask which option students would offer first and require one question for the guest. Minutes 3–7: reveal the train and stated preference; compare the HK$300 immediate difference with usefulness to the guest. Minutes 7–12: role-play a concise apology and offer, then ask the guest to repeat the agreed arrangements.

Challenge “the voucher is worth HK$500” with its restrictions and the guest’s plans. Challenge “same room standard means equivalent” with the purpose of the stay. Do not assume A’s morning taxi is free: the hotel pays it, but the amount is unknown. Ask what would happen if the receiving hotel’s hold expired during the conversation. This is recovery after a failure, not a lesson endorsing overbooking or a compensation-rights exercise.

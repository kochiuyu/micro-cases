# Keep it secret. For how long?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 68 -->

ENT-06 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Entrepreneurship · Innovation protection · Launch readiness

![Two founders discuss a compact folded display stand and an unmarked notebook in a modest prototype workshop.](../visible-secret-scene.jpg)

## In the news

On 22 December 2025, HKPC announced that Hong Kong’s first Technology and Innovation Support Centre had commenced operation, describing patent search, training and consultation support for startups and small businesses.

The announcement illustrates local attention to innovation protection. It does not establish that any particular invention will obtain protection or succeed commercially. The folding-stand startup below is fictional.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong startup has developed a compact folding display stand for market stalls. A mechanical locking arrangement makes setup easier. The founders plan their first public launch in four weeks. They have not publicly disclosed the mechanism or sold samples.

Drawings are access-controlled. Employees and the prototype supplier have confidentiality commitments. One founder wants to rely on secrecy and spend scarce funds on launch: keeping know-how confidential avoids disclosing it in a patent application and may preserve it for a long time.

The other founder wants confidential professional assessment of patent options before release. Neither patentability, ownership, relevant markets, useful protection, cost nor third-party rights has been assessed. The choice is what to investigate before launching, not whether students should draft or file a legal document.

## Evidence

| Approach to investigate | Possible benefit | Unanswered question |
|---|---|---|
| Rely on secrecy | Keep valuable know-how undisclosed | Can the information remain secret after sale? |
| Obtain confidential patent advice | Assess whether useful protection is available | Would eligibility, scope, territory, cost and timing fit the business? |

**Student question:** Which protection approach should the founders investigate first before the public launch? What would make that approach credible for this particular product?

## Round two

In a confidential internal test, an engineer who has never seen the drawings examines and dismantles one finished sample and works out the locking arrangement. The engineer is working under confidentiality; the test has not publicly disclosed the mechanism.

For this classroom situation, the test establishes that ordinary buyers receiving a unit could discover the core mechanism from the product itself, without anyone leaking the drawings. It does not show that all production know-how is visible, that a competitor intends to copy it, or that a patent would be available.

**Student question:** Would more staff confidentiality agreements address the exposure revealed by this test? What should change in the pre-launch plan, and what would you do if useful protection is unavailable or uneconomic?

## Identify how the information becomes known

The original controls address access to drawings and unauthorised disclosure. The test exposes a different route: learning from the finished product. More employee promises do not make an observable mechanism undiscoverable.

Independent discovery is not the same event as stealing drawings or breaching a confidence. Do not label the engineer’s finding theft. Equally, this case does not give general legal clearance to copy products: other rights, contracts and circumstances may matter.

## Protect the information that can still remain hidden

The reveal weakens a secrecy-only plan for the visible mechanism, not every secret in the business. Production methods, settings or other genuinely hidden know-how may still deserve proportionate confidentiality measures.

Secrecy is not costless simply because it lacks a patent registration fee. Maintaining controls takes effort. Patents and confidentiality can also serve different information or stages rather than being mutually exclusive for the whole company.

## Use the remaining pre-launch window

Before releasing the mechanism publicly, obtain qualified confidential advice about available protection, ownership, third-party rights, relevant markets, useful scope, timing and cost. Public disclosure can jeopardise patent options, so establish the sequence before treating the launch date as fixed.

This is not an instruction to file automatically, nor to wait for a granted patent before any launch. An application is not a guaranteed grant, and a patent does not provide worldwide exclusivity or blanket freedom to operate. Any right has a defined scope, territory and limited duration.

## A viable business may not rely on exclusivity

If useful protection is unavailable or uneconomic, the founders can deliberately consider competing through reliable production, service, brand, speed or continuing improvements. Those advantages must be developed; the case does not promise that they will defeat imitation.

The important revision is to stop budgeting or positioning as though the mechanism will remain a durable secret once buyers can inspect it. Assess commercial viability under realistic exposure rather than assuming either perfect secrecy or inevitable failure.

## Instructor note

Before the reveal, invite students to defend conserving launch funds and obtaining advice. Afterward, ask them to distinguish a drawing leak from discovery through a sold product. The confidential test leaves the pre-launch decision open.

A strong answer changes the protection investigation without declaring the mechanism patentable. It keeps genuinely hidden know-how separate, seeks appropriate confidential assessment before public release, and has a commercial fallback if effective protection is unavailable.

The product, founders and test are invented. Confidentiality commitments are scenario facts, not a finding that particular contracts are sufficient or enforceable. This is a classroom discussion, not legal advice or an instruction to submit an application.

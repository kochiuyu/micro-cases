# One customer. Which channel earns the sale?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 243 -->

MKT-21 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Omnichannel retail · Channel incentives · Sales credit

![An adult adviser and shopper compare chairs beside a small collection counter, plain carton and laptop in a home-office furniture shop.](../one-customer-which-channel-scene.jpg)

## One purchase can cross channels

IKEA’s US service page describes ordering and paying online before collecting at a physical location. That familiar journey crosses an organisational boundary even though the customer sees one retailer. It does not tell us how IKEA rewards staff.

Academic research and practitioner accounts identify a related management problem: channel-specific rewards can overlook work performed elsewhere in the same journey. Desk Corner, its four orders and every commission rule below are fictional, not IKEA’s practices or a reported dispute.

## Opening decision

**Fictional classroom situation.** Desk Corner sells home-office chairs through one shop and its own website. Customers may try chairs in store, pay through either channel, and collect or receive delivery. The store team provides demonstrations and collection; the online team maintains product information, checkout and home-delivery coordination. No medical benefit is claimed.

You are designing a future four-week team commission pilot. Existing pay, agreed commissions and service entitlements remain protected. Staff representatives will review transparent rules before the pilot starts; today’s records are a simulation, not a retrospective pay change.

The pilot has a fixed HK$800 demonstration budget across four completed, equal-price orders: HK$200 per order, shared between the two teams. One order receives only one HK$200 pot. Prices, margins and refunds are identical across channels; all four example orders are kept. Individual staff pay is outside this exercise.

**Checkout-only:** give the whole pot to the payment channel. This is simple and verifiable. **Logged assistance:** give half to the store for a verified product-selection consultation before online payment, and half to online; otherwise use checkout-only. Logged assistance requires a short order-linked record and occasional review. That administration is funded separately but uses staff time.

## Evidence

| Journey | Product selection | Payment and delivery |
|---|---|---|
| A | Store adviser compares chair sizes; customer then orders from home | Paid online; home delivery |
| B | Customer chooses using website information, no store advice | Paid online; home delivery |

First two of four simulated orders. A has verified pre-payment advice; B has no store contact. No covert tracking; neither rule permits withholding service or forcing checkout channel.

**Student question:** Which rule would you pilot, checkout-only or logged assistance? Allocate each HK$200 pot for journeys A and B, and name the behaviour your rule might encourage or discourage.

## Round two

The remaining two records show a different direction of travel: the purchase starts online, then the store does work. These are additional simulation journeys, not a measured effect of either commission rule.

| New journey | Verified record | What stays the same |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C | Paid online for collection; at pickup, store adviser compares sizes and helps choose a different size at the same price | One order, no extra sale or second commission pot |
| D | Paid online for collection; store staff prepare and hand over the chosen chair, with no selection consultation | Collection work is real, but not selection advice |

In C, the customer freely requests advice; no pressure to switch product or repurchase is permitted. Staff link the changed size to the original order. In D, efficient collection is the service wanted. The initial logged-assistance rule recognises advice only before payment, so neither C nor D qualifies for its advice split.

**Student question:** How would you revise or retain the rule after journeys C and D? Show team allocations for all four orders within HK$800, and add one safeguard against creating unnecessary advice or extra transactions just to earn credit.

## Separate the payment from the journey

Under checkout-only, A and B each give online HK$200 and store zero. Under the initial assistance rule, A gives HK$100 to each team; B still gives online HK$200. Total commission remains HK$400 for those two orders under either rule.

Checkout-only is easy to audit, but may make a useful in-store consultation invisible to the incentive. The shared rule acknowledges that work, but can invite claims for minor interactions. These are incentive risks, not observed staff misconduct or proven sales effects.

## The reveal tests the boundary

Both original rules give C and D entirely to online: both were paid online, and C’s advice came after payment. Yet C combines online ordering, substantive store advice and store fulfilment. D combines online ordering and store fulfilment without advice. Changing where payment occurs would not necessarily change what the customer needs.

A revised rule can distinguish product-selection help, processing the transaction and completing fulfilment. It should not assume every pickup requires advice or that every advice session causes a sale.

## One defensible pilot, not a proven optimum

For illustration, allocate 100 credit points per order: **40 for selection support, 40 for checkout, 20 for fulfilment.** Give selection points to the store only when an order-linked, substantive consultation is verified, whether before or after payment; otherwise give them to the payment channel. In these four records, online provides that fallback through its self-service product information. Checkout follows payment; fulfilment follows home-delivery coordination or store collection.

These are chosen policy weights, not measured causal shares or payment for minutes worked. Under this pilot every point is worth HK$2 from the order’s HK$200 pot. Another coherent set of weights or a shared team pool can be defended; do not claim this one maximises profit.

| Order | Store points | Online points | Store / online commission |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 40 | 60 | HK$80 / HK$120 |
| B | 0 | 100 | HK$0 / HK$200 |
| C | 60 | 40 | HK$120 / HK$80 |
| D | 20 | 80 | HK$40 / HK$160 |
| Total | 120 | 280 | HK$240 / HK$560 |

## Make cooperation easier than claiming credit

Use one order ID and at most one selection-support claim. Define substantive advice with a short record of the customer’s question and the comparison supplied, not duration or a forced product change. Routine greetings and collection checks are not selection advice. Review disputed records through a stated process; do not make the customer arbitrate staff pay.

Pay and service do not depend on customers accepting unnecessary advice. No cancellation-and-repurchase route creates another pot. Do not collect new personal data simply to prove a journey; use the minimum authorised order-linked information. Treat disputed or missing evidence under a declared rule, not an improvised favour.

## Judge the retailer, not the winning column

Moving commission from one team to the other creates no new customer value by itself. Before wider adoption, examine unwanted channel redirection, collection waiting time, unresolved advice requests, disputed claims and total retailer contribution after fulfilment and administration costs. Keep customer choice and existing service standards intact.

These four constructed records test whether a rule handles known journeys consistently. They do not show how many sales the store caused, forecast conversion gains or establish the best commission split. A future pilot needs a credible comparison and staff/customer feedback.

## Instructor note

Allow two minutes for the opening and vote, three for A/B allocations, three for the reveal and revised rule, then four for incentive and evidence limits. Keep later sections hidden until the second vote. Require each proposed allocation to add to HK$200 per order and HK$800 across all four.

Accept checkout-only when students defend simplicity and propose a credible way to recognise service separately; accept shared rules when they define qualifying work and controls. The worked 40/40/20 scheme is a testable proposal, not the correct percentage. Ask why C is still one order and why D should not be turned into an unnecessary consultation.

MKT-12 diagnoses a conversion-stage problem; MKT-13 estimates relationship contribution; MKT-11 demonstrates service expertise to customers. This case concerns internal channel cooperation. MKT-22 will distinguish advertising attribution models; do not import ad-click weighting or causal-credit estimation here. Employment terms, individual performance judgements and legal pay advice are outside scope.

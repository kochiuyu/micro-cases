# One forecast. Three strategies?

Case 90 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Strategy · Scenario planning · Strategic flexibility

![Two adult trainers consider a substantial course folder and separate thin lesson booklets in a sunlit teaching studio.](../more-than-one-future-scene.jpg)

## In the news

On 15 May 2025, HKEX held an ESG Academy seminar on climate strategy, scenario planning and analysis. Its publicly hosted presentation distinguished exploring plausible futures from predicting one future and connected that reasoning to strategic choices.

Our fictional case applies the general idea to customer buying patterns, not climate reporting. The seminar did not discuss this training business or endorse these invented figures.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong provider of business presentation-skills training is choosing next year’s service-development focus. It will choose one development focus rather than fully develop both separate offers. Its qualified trainers can deliver either proposed route, with appropriate learner support.

A: develop a distinctive full-length corporate cohort programme for central learning teams. B: develop short stand-alone modules for department managers buying help with specific needs. The choice concerns the offer and its buyers, not whether short courses teach as well as long ones.

Both routes are affordable, including the stated downside. No client has committed and no probability estimate favours either future. The manager wants a strategy that fits the customer patterns the business may encounter.

## Evidence

Two contrasting classroom scenarios for the same coming year: **Central programmes**, where clients consolidate learning purchases into substantial cohort programmes; and **Department choices**, where budgets remain available but departments buy smaller units as needed. These are not forecasts, equally likely outcomes or every possible future.

| New offer · HK$000 | Central programmes | Department choices |
|---|---:|---:|
| A · Full-length cohort programme | 300 | −60 |
| B · Stand-alone catalogue | 100 | 180 |

Each entry is a fictional one-year incremental cash surplus after all incremental development, marketing and delivery cash costs. Unchanged existing business is excluded; financing and tax effects are outside this simplified comparison. Thus −60 means a HK$60,000 loss on this initiative, not necessarily a loss for the whole firm.

**Student question:** Would you provisionally choose A or B? Explain which customer future matters most to your choice and what evidence would make you reconsider.

## Round two

The trainers complete a paid design-feasibility exercise. Core practice activities can become reusable modules, sold separately or joined with a deliberately designed facilitator-led opening, synthesis and follow-through into a coherent cohort programme.

This makes a third route feasible before commitment: C, a modular core with facilitated combinations. Sequencing, assessment and learner support still require design. Its estimates include the extra architecture and adaptation work, marketing and delivery; flexibility is not free. All three routes remain affordable.



| One-year incremental cash surplus · HK$000 | Central programmes | Department choices |
|---|---:|---:|
| A · Full-length cohort programme | 300 | −60 |
| B · Stand-alone catalogue | 100 | 180 |
| C · Modular core with facilitated combinations | 220 | 140 |

The same cash-cost basis and exclusions apply. C’s added integration cost and less specialised offer limit its upside. Design feasibility is not proof of customer demand, equal educational outcomes or guaranteed receipts.

**Student question:** Choose A, B or C now. What upside are you willing to give up, and which observable customer signal would trigger a review of your choice?

## Test the offer, not just the preferred story

A fits central buyers seeking a substantial cohort experience; B fits departments buying smaller stand-alone units. The same option must be examined under both customer futures, not only the one that makes it look best.

No probabilities are supplied. Averaging the two columns would silently assume equal weights. A can be defended by a strategic priority for central programmes and tolerance for its stated loss; B can be defended by departmental demand or a preference for its stronger result in the weaker displayed scenario.

## Price the flexibility rather than assuming it is free

C gives up HK$80,000 compared with A under central programmes and HK$40,000 compared with B under department choices. It is not best in either column.

Its smaller result across the two displayed scenarios is HK$140,000, compared with HK$100,000 for B and −HK$60,000 for A. That makes C attractive if the manager values performance across these contrasting conditions enough to sacrifice specialised upside. It does not prove C is everyone’s best choice.

## Keep the boundary around the scenario table

HK$140,000 is C’s minimum in this table, not a guaranteed cash floor or a global worst case. Other customer patterns, competitor moves or delivery difficulties could produce worse outcomes. The numbers are assumptions for strategic comparison, not verified Hong Kong demand forecasts.

The feasibility exercise adds a genuine option; it does not show that buyers want it. A, B or C can be chosen with a stated strategic rationale and tolerance for the outcomes. There is no supplied expected-value ranking.

## Connect the strategy to a review signal

Choose a named review owner and examine qualified enquiries and actual procurement commitments monthly: are customers increasingly seeking whole programmes or separate modules? Check conversion and delivery feedback as well, rather than treating every enquiry as a sale.

Agree what sustained pattern would prompt a review, which assumption it would challenge and what response would be considered. One unusual request is not a new future. Changing an offer later may still require time and expenditure; C does not remove every adaptation cost.

## Instructor note

Take a provisional A-or-B vote before introducing C. Ask students to name the customer assumption behind their choice, not to guess a hidden correct probability. Both scenarios retain budgets; the uncertainty concerns how clients buy training, not simply a good or bad economy.

After the reveal, ask what C protects and what it sacrifices. The displayed minima are −60, 100 and 140 in HK$000. Do not call 140 a guaranteed floor, prescribe a risk preference or introduce an equal-weight average without an explicit new assumption.

All routes are feasible and fundable. This is service-development strategy, not room capacity, cash timing, payback or a claim about teaching efficacy. The one-year classroom stress test is deliberately simpler than the fuller, often longer-horizon institutional scenario methods in the references.

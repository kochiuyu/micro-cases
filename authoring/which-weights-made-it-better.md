# Better results. Which weights made them better?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 442 -->

SUS-37 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Multi-criteria decision analysis · Weight sensitivity · Dominance and tradeoffs

![Adult Hong Kong community committee members compare three abstract project models and separate blank criterion cards at a meeting table; no project is marked as the winner and no numerical results are shown.](../which-weights-made-it-better-scene.jpg)

## A ranking has assumptions

A project ranking combines evidence with choices about what matters. Sensitivity analysis can show when plausible priorities change the leader, without changing any project. This fictional exercise separates a transparent conditional ranking from a claim that arithmetic has resolved a community’s values.

## Opening decision

You advise fictional Harbour District’s community committee in Hong Kong. It can fund one of three optional projects: A, a low-carbon community building; B, a neighbourhood access hub; or C, a smaller mixed upgrade. All meet mandatory safety, legal and accessibility conditions. Required services remain separately funded. Each has the same complete funded cost and implementation horizon.

An agreed assessment gives each project three preference scores: emissions reduction, additional community access and resilience. Higher is better. Zero and 100 are fixed agreed worst and best reference outcomes for each criterion, not the worst and best current projects. Intermediate scores represent proportional value between those endpoints, not tonnes, resident counts or probabilities.

For this exercise, all relevant optional benefits are covered, no benefit is counted twice, and the committee accepts adding the three weighted scores. Mandatory conditions cannot be offset by extra points. Scores, participants, costs and timing stay fixed throughout. Removing an option would not change the scales. These simplifying assumptions are stipulated, not independently measured findings.

The published appraisal rule gives emissions a weight of 0.50, access 0.30 and resilience 0.20. Multiply each score by its weight, then add the three results. Weights express tradeoffs between the defined endpoint improvements, not simply which topic sounds important. The committee must test plausible weights before making an award; no winner has been promised.

**Student question:** Which project leads under the provisional weights? Calculate all three totals. Would you describe that project as unconditionally best, or best under a stated set of tradeoffs? Explain what must remain visible in your recommendation.

## Round two

Before the award, the same stakeholder group agrees two further weight sets are reasonable sensitivity tests. Climate emphasis uses emissions 0.60, access 0.20 and resilience 0.20. Access emphasis uses emissions 0.20, access 0.60 and resilience 0.20. Both sum to one. The group has not selected either as the final rule.

Stakeholders accept the scores and endpoint definitions. Their unresolved disagreement is how much access improvement to trade for emissions improvement. Both sensitivity sets are approved for testing, not as simultaneous binding award rules. The published process permits a documented final weighting discussion before the decision. It does not permit secretly changing weights to favour a preferred project.

One funded meeting remains before the deadline. It can resolve or openly record the tradeoff and produce the authorised committee decision without losing any project’s availability. No new proposals, participation barriers or later benefit dates have appeared. You must recommend a route, not pretend that another decimal place will settle the disagreement.

**Student question:** Calculate the rankings under both sensitivity sets. Which option can be ruled out within this model? Recommend retaining an unconditional winner, making a conditional recommendation, or reopening the unresolved weighting choice. State what the final decision record should explain.

## Debrief · Reproduce the first ranking

Under the provisional rule, A scores 90 × 0.50 + 50 × 0.30 + 70 × 0.20 = 74. B scores 60 × 0.50 + 90 × 0.30 + 70 × 0.20 = 71. C scores 40 × 0.50 + 40 × 0.30 + 50 × 0.20 = 42.

A leads by three points under that rule. This is an index difference, not three percent more welfare, three tonnes saved or a measured probability of success. Reporting the score without its scales and weights would conceal the judgments responsible for the ranking.

## Debrief · Test the reversal

With climate emphasis, A scores 54 + 10 + 14 = 78; B scores 36 + 18 + 14 = 68; C scores 24 + 8 + 10 = 42. A leads. With access emphasis, A scores 18 + 30 + 14 = 62; B scores 12 + 54 + 14 = 80; C again scores 42.

B leads under access emphasis. No project became better or worse, and no new evidence changed its assessed performance. The preference tradeoff changed. Neither tested result is an arithmetic error. An unconditional claim that A is best across the accepted priority sets is therefore unsupported.

## Debrief · Separate dominance from preference

C has lower scores than both A and B on every criterion, at the same cost and horizon. With non-negative weights summing to one, C must have a lower weighted total than either. It is dominated within the stipulated model. This is stronger than merely coming third under one chosen weight set.

A and B do not dominate each other: A offers more emissions value, B more access value, and their resilience scores are equal. Excluding C does not resolve that tradeoff. Keep the original scale anchors and scores rather than recalculating them from only A and B.

## Debrief · Make a conditional recommendation

Recommend A under the provisional or climate-emphasis weights and B under access emphasis, then use the permitted meeting to settle the final weighting choice transparently. This combines a useful conditional recommendation with reopening the unresolved value judgment. Do not declare a robust winner, average the weight sets without authority, or quietly select the rule that produces your favourite.

The final record should show the scores, fixed scales, weights, calculated rankings and reason for the adopted tradeoff. Record material disagreement and who had authority to decide. If disagreement persists, the authorised committee must make and explain the choice under its published process; the analyst cannot manufacture consensus.

## Debrief · Where the boundary lies

Keeping resilience at 0.20 leaves 0.80 for emissions and access. Let the emissions weight be w, so access is 0.80 minus w. A’s advantage over B equals 30w minus 40(0.80 minus w), or 70w minus 32. The scores tie when w equals 32/70, about 0.4571.

Above that threshold A leads; below it B leads. This boundary describes only the specified additive model with fixed resilience and scales. It does not tell stakeholders which side they should prefer. Different criteria, interactions, uncertain scores or non-negotiable obligations would require a different analysis rather than blind reuse of this threshold.

## Instructor note

Minutes 0–5: calculate the provisional ranking. Minutes 5–10: reveal the alternative weights and identify the reversal and dominated option. Minutes 10–15: write a conditional recommendation and a decision-record sentence. Ask whether a numerical total is evidence, a value judgment, or a combination of both.

SUS-18 concerns who can shape the available ballot; SUS-29 concerns discounting benefits across time. Here participation, alternatives and timing stay fixed. The exercise tests sensitivity to stated value tradeoffs. All projects and scores are fictional; the model is not a real appraisal or an instruction to trade away legal, safety or access duties.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

All projects, committee rules, scores and weights are fictional. Same complete cost and horizon; mandatory conditions and existing services protected. Fixed cardinal preference scales, stipulated additive validity and no double counting. Weight changes are authorised sensitivity tests before an award, not covert retrospective manipulation. C is strictly dominated in this model. A/B ranking changes with tradeoffs, not measured performance. No probability, welfare percentage, empirical impact or legal conclusion is inferred.

Official guidance explains preference scales, swing weighting, dominance and sensitivity. The classroom uses stipulated fixed endpoints and simplified additive scores, not a completed Green Book appraisal.

Its composite-indicator setting informs model limitations; it does not validate these fictional project scores.

Official engineering guidance distinguishes mandatory criteria and documented decision preferences, assumptions and sensitivities. Its mission procedures are not Hong Kong community-governance requirements.

The agency report explains structured decision making through objectives, alternatives, consequences and tradeoffs, including preference scales and objective weights. This short methodological section is not an empirical evaluation of these projects.
- [ACT Treasury · Multi-Criteria Analysis approach](https://www.treasury.act.gov.au/capital-framework/prove/detailed-technical-guidance/multi-criteria-analysis/multi-criteria-analysis-approach). It is cited as a contrasting caution, not as endorsement of this classroom scoring rule or a locally applicable procedure.

# An invoice issued. Cash likely?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 157 -->

ACC-14 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Receivable impairment · Expected recovery · Allowance versus write-off

![A design-studio owner and accountant consider three blank scenario cards beside an invoice folder and design samples.](../an-invoice-not-a-receipt-scene.jpg)

## Industry context

Design Capital’s 2025 annual report, available through HKEX, separately presents trade receivables, loss allowances and amounts written off. Its accounting note describes using historical experience and forward-looking information when estimating expected credit losses.

These are ordinary financial-reporting disclosures, not evidence of misconduct. The small Hong Kong design studio, customer and all amounts and probabilities below are original fiction, not Design Capital’s accounts or collection experience.

## Opening decision

A fictional Hong Kong design studio has completed and delivered an accepted project. An undisputed HK$100,000 invoice is overdue. Revenue recognition and the invoice amount are not in question; no refund, credit note or price concession is due. No cash has been collected. The gross receivable remains HK$100,000 and the studio has an existing HK$10,000 loss allowance, giving a net carrying amount of HK$90,000.

The customer proposes paying HK$20,000 in two weeks, HK$40,000 two weeks later and the final HK$40,000 after another two weeks. That is a statement of intended payment, not cash or a guarantee. The studio has not agreed a change to contractual rights or forgiven any balance. Collection and customer contact continue regardless of the accounting estimate.

For today’s internal review, finance supplies the three mutually exclusive recovery scenarios below. Treat them as the complete, reasonable and supportable estimate set for this exercise at this date. They already incorporate the customer’s proposal and current collection evidence. The amounts are total eventual recovery on the existing invoice, not extra receipts or individual instalments. Ignore immaterial discounting for this short-horizon classroom calculation; this is not a general rule for delayed receipts. No collateral, insurance proceeds, fees or other recoveries are omitted from the supplied amounts. The exercise uses a simplified lifetime expected-loss model, not a complete compliance assessment for every small business.

## Evidence

| Total eventual recovery | Today’s probability | Cash shortfall against HK$100,000 |
|---|---:|---:|
| HK$100,000 | 80% | HK$0 |
| HK$60,000 | 15% | HK$40,000 |
| HK$20,000 | 5% | HK$80,000 |

**Student question:** Would you keep the HK$10,000 allowance, increase it or remove it because the customer promises full payment? Calculate the scenario-weighted recovery and explain how an expected shortfall differs from the amount still owed.

## Round two

At a separate internal review just after the first proposed instalment date, no HK$20,000 payment has arrived. The customer says its own expected customer receipt was delayed. Finance reviews the missed payment together with updated collection information and supplies a revised scenario set. The probabilities are given assumptions supported within this fictional exercise; they are not calculated mechanically from one missed instalment.

| Total eventual recovery | Updated probability |
| --- | --- |
| HK$100,000 | 60% |
| HK$60,000 | 30% |
| HK$20,000 | 10% |

No cash has been received between the reviews. The gross receivable and contractual claim are still HK$100,000. No debt has been forgiven, contract modified or balance written off. The updated scenarios replace, rather than add to, the old set. The recovery amounts still describe all eventual receipts from the original invoice. Finance has not concluded that there is no reasonable prospect of recovering a specific part of the debt. The short-horizon discounting and other modelling simplifications remain unchanged.

**Student question:** What allowance and net receivable should the studio report now, and what is the change from the existing estimate? Explain why the update neither records a cash payment nor tells the collection team to stop pursuing the invoice.

## A promise is one input, not the estimate

Initially, expected recovery is 80% × HK$100,000 + 15% × HK$60,000 + 5% × HK$20,000 = HK$90,000. The corresponding expected shortfall is HK$10,000. Keeping the existing allowance is supported by the supplied first-review evidence. The full-payment scenario is the most likely, but using only that scenario would ignore the other possible outcomes. Increasing the allowance without contrary evidence is not automatically better accounting.

## Update the estimate, not the invoice

After the review, expected recovery is 60% × HK$100,000 + 30% × HK$60,000 + 10% × HK$20,000 = HK$80,000. The allowance becomes HK$100,000 − HK$80,000 = HK$20,000. The required increase is HK$10,000, not another HK$20,000 on top of the original allowance. Under this simplified model, the increase is an impairment expense and the net receivable falls from HK$90,000 to HK$80,000.

## Four different numbers

The gross invoice and claim remain HK$100,000. The loss allowance is HK$20,000. The net carrying amount is HK$80,000. Cash received is still zero. The HK$80,000 is a weighted estimate, not a promised receipt or one of the three stipulated recovery outcomes. A reporting estimate does not change the customer’s obligation or create money for paying the studio’s bills.

## Allowance is not forgiveness

The studio continues its documented collection process while reporting the updated estimate. Forgiving debt changes rights; an allowance estimates expected loss without doing that. Writing off a balance is a separate accounting judgement about recovery expectations, not simply the act of increasing an allowance. A write-off also should not be casually equated with legal forgiveness. This case supplies no basis for a write-off, contract modification or new credit note.

## Instructor note

Ask students to calculate expected recovery and, independently, weighted shortfalls. Initially: 15% × HK$40,000 + 5% × HK$80,000 = HK$10,000. After the update: 30% × HK$40,000 + 10% × HK$80,000 = HK$20,000. Keep a four-line bridge for gross receivable, allowance, net receivable and cash. Unlike a preference vote, not every accounting answer is equally supported once the complete evidence set is stipulated. Do not invent pessimistic probabilities to appear prudent or wait for a final default before recognising expected loss. The later review is a new internal measurement date, not a lesson about adjusting subsequent events. In practice the reporting framework, evidence quality, timing, discounting, modifications and write-off criteria require separate assessment. No external reading is needed to solve the supplied model.

# Two sellers merge. Which competition disappears?

![Two delivery-business colleagues discuss a plain research sheet beside document envelopes.](../the-rival-customers-would-choose-scene.jpg)

## An acquisition can face a competition question

On 25 July 2024, Singapore’s competition authority said it had ended its assessment of Grab’s proposed Trans-cab acquisition after the parties withdrew their plans. The earlier decision was provisional, not a completed merger clearance. This is regional context, not the same competitive mechanism as our fictional two-seller delivery example.

## Opening decision

Two fictional Hong Kong delivery firms, A and B, are considering common ownership. Both currently compete independently for business document deliveries. Their managers estimate HK$120,000 annual operating savings from consolidating depot administration. An accountant has checked the calculation’s inputs, but implementation and realisation remain uncertain. The estimate is net of planned annual integration costs; it is not a customer price reduction or a valuation of competition. There is no evidence yet about how much would reach customers.

Three other firms also advertise document delivery. The proposal’s first presentation therefore says customers would still have several options. A’s board must decide whether to spend an affordable HK$10,000 on a bounded, independent customer-substitution and efficiencies study, or stop the current acquisition proposal and continue operating independently. The study could recommend redesign or abandonment; funding it does not authorise a transaction. No binding acquisition commitment, operational integration or coordination of competitive prices has occurred.

Proper legal advice, any required review and protected due-diligence arrangements are funded separately. The parties continue independent commercial decisions; no sensitive customer identities or pricing plans are shared in this classroom exercise. Neither business is failing, and existing contracts, paid work and delivery obligations continue. The learning question is what rivalry customers might lose, not whether students can grant legal approval. What evidence would make the list of five firms meaningful?

## Evidence

| Initial claim | Known | Still needed |
|---|---|---|
| Operating savings | HK$120,000 annual estimate | Realisation, alternatives and customer benefit |
| Customer choice | A, B and three advertised rivals | Usable substitutes for each delivery need |

**Student question:** Would you fund the bounded independent study or stop the current proposal? Explain what evidence is missing about customer alternatives and who would benefit from the claimed savings.

## Round two

A preliminary, independently administered interview exercise covers sixty selected A customers with one specified delivery need each. It is not a random or market-representative sample. Asked what they would do if A were unavailable, forty name B as their usable first alternative, ten name C, and ten would postpone the delivery rather than use another listed provider. These stated choices are not observed switching after a price increase and do not estimate a market-wide diversion ratio.

The service checks explain the pattern. Thirty of the forty naming B need collection after 4pm with delivery before 6pm; only A and B currently offer confirmed capacity for that requirement in the specified area. The other ten naming B and ten naming C have flexible collection windows. The ten postponing do not identify an acceptable substitute for their particular needs. D and E advertise delivery but cannot meet these interviewees’ required windows and coverage. Their future expansion is possible, not evidenced or guaranteed. The exercise does not establish a legally defined market or future price rise.

Depot savings still require verification of timing, implementation costs and whether a practical less restrictive arrangement could achieve them. No customer-benefit commitment or evidence of pass-through has appeared. Both board options remain available: commission the independent study with these sharper questions, or stop the proposal now. Keeping separate brand names after acquisition would not by itself preserve independent ownership incentives. Any redesign would require separate commercial and legal evaluation, not a classroom promise that a remedy works.

**Student question:** Given the selected customers’ alternatives, would you still commission the study or stop the proposal? Identify the lost competitive constraint, the limits of the evidence and what would be needed to support an efficiency claim that benefits customers.

## Count meaningful alternatives

Forty of sixty selected respondents name B, including thirty with a narrow late-collection need. Common ownership removes A and B as independently controlled alternatives even if both signs remain. D and E’s presence on a broad list does not show they constrain this service. But sixty selected statements do not establish market shares, a general switching probability or the magnitude of harm.

## Savings and customer benefit are different claims

The HK$120,000 is a projected operating saving, not verified realised money or automatic consumer benefit. Ask whether it is timely and credible, requires the merger, and could instead arise through a practical lawful alternative. Then ask whether remaining competition and incentives would transmit benefits through price, quality, choice or service. Do not subtract an unmeasured competition cost from the saving and declare a welfare result.

## Study a change in rivalry

The counterfactual is two viable independent firms, not one disappearing. The proposed study should examine service-specific alternatives, actual switching and bidding evidence where lawfully available, capacity, entry conditions and substantiated efficiencies. A staged decision can preserve options without prejudging approval. Stopping now is also defensible if the board does not want to pursue the identified risk.

## Instructor note

This is an economics exercise, not a merger filing or legal opinion. The Singapore hook concerns a different transaction and mechanism. UK and US guidelines illustrate economic reasoning, not Hong Kong legal tests. The cited 2015 Hong Kong guideline expressly has a carrier-licence scope; do not treat it as a general delivery-merger approval rule. STR-05 examines buyer value; ECO-20 examines the independent alternatives customers would lose.

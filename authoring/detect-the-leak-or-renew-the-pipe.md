# Detect the leak sooner, or renew the old pipe?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 515 -->

SUS-42 · Hong Kong · 15 minutes · Water conservation · Maintenance response · Asset renewal · Service interruption

![A Hong Kong estate manager and engineer discuss an old communal pipe; a small sensor sits unattached on the workbench.](../detect-the-leak-or-renew-the-pipe-scene.jpg)

## Hong Kong plans to renew higher-risk aged water mains

In its September 2026 Policy Address, Hong Kong announced plans to replace or rehabilitate 350 kilometres of higher-risk aged water mains over five years. It also proposed linking continuous monitoring to leak detection and maintenance. Those plans concern the public network. The committee and communal pipes in this case are fictional.

## Opening decision

Jo advises the property committee of a fictional Hong Kong estate. An engineer identifies one communal fresh-water pipe section for preventive work. There is no known active leak at decision time. Either option uses qualified professionals and leaves emergency response arrangements in place.

Monitor adds sensors and a repair contract, but keeps the present pipe. Renew replaces the identified pipe section and retains conventional detection. The contractor expects one residual incident under Renew, not zero. The table gives one-year forecasts. All incidents are treated as separate and all leaks in the model eventually get repaired.

The committee will spend at most HK$50,000 and will accept at most 50 cubic metres of expected leakage in the year. Among options meeting both limits, it wants the lower direct spending. Report interruptions separately because residents feel them even when an option meets the water target. For the exercise, count each household’s outage hours separately and include planned work.

## Evidence

| Fictional annual forecast | Monitor | Renew |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annual fixed cost | HK$25,000 | HK$44,000 |
| Cost per leak incident | HK$2,000 | HK$2,000 |
| Number of incidents | 6 | 1 |
| Volume lost per day per incident | 2 m³ | 2 m³ |
| Time from onset to confirmed repair | 3 days | 6 days |
| Water service interruption | 40 household-hours | 90 household-hours |

**Student question:** What annual leakage and spending would each option produce? Which should the committee choose under its two limits, and what does it give up on water conservation or service interruption?

## Round two

Before the vote, the monitoring contractor estimates that work queues will extend the average time from onset to confirmed repair to eight days, even though sensors still flag leaks just as quickly. Monitor’s forecast interruption rises to 100 household-hours. All other exhibit values, including the fixed and per-incident costs and Renew’s assumptions, stay the same.

**Student question:** Which option now meets the limits? Ignoring any further change in interruption, what is the longest average onset-to-repair time that would keep Monitor within the leakage limit? What should the committee measure to see if Monitor can meet it?

## Debrief · Use the whole time the water is leaking

Multiply the incident count by days of leakage per incident and cubic metres lost per day. Monitor originally loses 6 × 3 × 2 = 36 m³; Renew loses 1 × 6 × 2 = 12 m³. Renew saves 24 m³ relative to Monitor despite a slower response to its residual incident, because it has fewer leaks.

## Debrief · Apply the committee’s rule

Monitor costs HK$25,000 + 6 × HK$2,000 = HK$37,000. Renew costs HK$44,000 + HK$2,000 = HK$46,000. Both meet the budget and water limits. Choose Monitor under the stated lower-spending rule: it saves HK$9,000 and causes 50 fewer household-hours of interruption, but loses 24 m³ more fresh water.

## Debrief · A faster alarm is not a faster fix

The longer delay raises Monitor’s forecast loss to 6 × 8 × 2 = 96 m³, above the 50 m³ limit. Its HK$37,000 cost still fits, but lower spending cannot override the water limit. Renew remains within both limits at 12 m³ and HK$46,000, and now also has 10 fewer household-hours of expected interruption.

## Debrief · Make the response target measurable

Monitor would need an average of at most 50 ÷ (6 × 2) = 4⅙ days from onset to confirmed repair under the stated rate and event count. Estimate onset from validated flow records and log detection, dispatch, repair and closure separately. If incident rates or flow rates differ materially, calculate total loss from the observed incidents rather than assuming the same 4⅙-day threshold.

## Instructor note

Use 2 minutes to read, 4 to compare both packages, 4 to process the delay and 5 to defend a decision. Ask students why faster detection can coexist with longer water loss. This simplified one-year comparison does not value every outage, model bursts or certify a real pipe or repair plan.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

All estate conditions, incident counts, rates, losses, prices and interruptions are fictional expected planning inputs. Only the dated public-network announcement is real. This is not plumbing instruction, a water tariff analysis or an assessment of any real pipe.

- [HKSAR Government, 2026 Policy Address, Clean Environment](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202609/16/P2026091600280.htm). Announces a risk-based 350-kilometre programme and the plan to link monitoring, detection and maintenance. These are plans, not realised outcomes.
- [HKSAR Government, LCQ14: Handling water mains leakage](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202505/28/P2025052800623p.htm). Discusses risk-based intervention and notes that missing master meters limit the government’s ability to measure private estate leakage.
- [Water Supplies Department, Water Matters: Water Distribution](https://www.wsd.gov.hk/en/water-matters/water-distribution/03/). Describes continuous monitoring in district metering areas alongside risk-based pipe replacement and rehabilitation.
- [Water Supplies Department, Guideline on Leak Detection on Underground Communal Service of Housing Estates](https://www.wsd.gov.hk/filemanager/en/content_1720/Guidelin_on_Leak_Detection_in_Housing_Estates.pdf). Describes the underground communal-service setting and how concealed leaks can occur inside estates. Not a determination for the fictional site.
- [Water Supplies Department, Fresh Water Plumbing Maintenance Guide](https://www.wsd.gov.hk/filemanager/en/share/pdf/FWPMGe_a.pdf). Describes maintenance of internal freshwater plumbing for service reliability and to address leaks and nuisance. It does not supply any case estimate.

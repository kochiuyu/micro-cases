# A cheerful greeting. At what emotional cost?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 329 -->

PPL-28 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Emotional labour · Display rules · Professional service

![Visitor-desk advisers with varied natural expressions attend calmly to a guest.](../cheerful-greeting-emotional-cost-scene.jpg)

## A service standard also defines emotional work

Emotional-labour research examines how required expressions relate to employees’ feelings and effort. Studies distinguish changing an outward display from changing an inner feeling, and neither can be inferred reliably from a smile alone. The following desk and feedback are fictional.

## Opening decision

A Hong Kong cultural venue is choosing a four-week visitor-desk service standard. A requires a broad smile, an upbeat tone and the same enthusiastic opening at every routine greeting. B requires prompt acknowledgement, respectful tone, attentive listening and clear information, while permitting a calm expression and varied natural wording. Both preserve courtesy, accessibility, accurate directions and complaint escalation. A also permits calm handling of distress and complaints; its rigid enthusiasm requirement concerns routine greetings, not emergencies.

Both options fit funded training and normal staffing. Paid breaks, supervisor support and relief after difficult encounters remain available under either. No employee must tolerate abuse, disclose private feelings or accept reduced pay. The manager wants a welcoming, reliable service, not a test of employees’ personalities. The proposed standard will guide coaching during the trial, not automated facial analysis, secret monitoring or disciplinary scoring.

A may make expectations easy to teach and recognise. B may allow professional expression that fits the encounter without requiring everyone to perform the same mood, but managers must still define what respectful service looks like. No measured comparison of customer outcomes or employee effort is yet available.

## Evidence

A: broad smile, upbeat tone and fixed enthusiastic opening for routine greetings. B: prompt acknowledgement, respectful tone, listening and clear information, with varied calm expression allowed. Both retain courtesy, accessibility, accurate directions, complaint escalation, paid breaks and supervisor support.

**Student question:** Which standard would you trial, rigid enthusiasm or respectful service with varied expression? Explain which behaviour must be consistent and what you cannot infer about a colleague’s feelings from the outward greeting.

## Round two

Before either trial, six advisers voluntarily discuss examples in a confidential group session. One says the upbeat greeting usually matches how they feel. Another says it feels easy during ordinary enquiries but takes effort after several tense encounters, even when the next visitor is polite. A third says they can listen warmly and explain clearly with a calm expression, while the broad smile feels performed. These are illustrative self-reports, not diagnoses, staff rankings or evidence that anyone provided poor service.

In a separate convenience sample of twelve departing visitors, nine name clear directions and attentive listening as their main priorities; three also mention enjoying an enthusiastic welcome. No one asks for identical expressions. The question allowed visitors to describe preferences, not choose between tested service designs. The sample is small, non-random and may omit dissatisfied guests. It does not establish that expression never matters or that B improves satisfaction.

The manager confirms that B can retain the same required service steps and accessible communication, and neither plan changes queue capacity, breaks, escalation or support. No new training budget has been spent. The decision remains how narrowly to prescribe the outward emotion, with a plan to check courtesy and employee effort rather than assume that one expression costs everyone the same.

**Student question:** Would you keep or change your choice after this feedback? Distinguish surface acting from naturally felt professional expression, then specify a service check and a staff-support practice without diagnosing or ranking employees.

## Answer the first question

B is a sound provisional choice: define a consistent standard of acknowledgement, attention, clarity, respectful language and appropriate escalation rather than equate welcome with a fixed smile. A has a legitimate training advantage because its display rule is concrete, but that does not establish that its exact emotional appearance is needed. Observing a greeting tells the manager about visible behaviour, not whether the employee genuinely feels it or how much effort it takes.

## Answer the second question

The feedback strengthens the case for a bounded trial of B, not a claim of proven superiority. Surface acting changes the outward display while feelings remain different, as when someone performs enthusiasm they do not feel. Naturally felt professional expression occurs when the displayed concern or warmth fits what the employee feels; it need not be exuberant. Deep acting involves attempting to change inner feelings to fit the required display. The manager should not require staff to make themselves feel cheerful as the solution to a poorly chosen rule.

## Different encounters can create different demands

The same employee can experience different effort across encounters, and two employees can perform the same greeting with different effort. Do not label a naturally expressive colleague insincere or a calm colleague disengaged. B does not eliminate emotional labour: remaining respectful after a difficult encounter can still require regulation. Nor does authenticity mean expressing hostility or abandoning courtesy. The aim is a proportionate behavioural standard, not unrestricted expression or enforced happiness.

## Check service and support the work

Use announced, proportionate observations of whether visitors are acknowledged, heard, given correct accessible information and offered escalation when needed. Seek optional visitor feedback about clarity and respectful treatment, not whether every face looks cheerful. Invite private, voluntary staff feedback on which encounter demands are difficult, provide a clear supervisor handoff and keep funded relief and breaks usable. Discuss work design and resources without requiring personal explanations or collecting inferred emotional profiles.

## Learn without overstating the evidence

Review the four-week trial using service omissions, complaint handling, access needs, queue observations and voluntary staff feedback. Preserve a route to revise B if courtesy becomes unclear or a useful greeting aid is missing. The nine of twelve visitors are 75% of this convenience sample, not 75% of all guests. The six advisers’ examples reveal possible differences in effort, not a prevalence estimate or proof that the script causes exhaustion. The three visitors who enjoy enthusiasm are a reason to permit it naturally, not require it universally.

## Instructor note

This case changes the emotional display rule, not the visitor’s entitlement or the choice of optional hospitality gestures. It also differs from teaching an already agreed routine through role models. Ask students to name a behavioural requirement and a support practice without inferring mental health or commitment from expressions. The research includes experiments, field observations, a meta-analysis and a narrative review with overlapping evidence. Do not turn them into a diagnosis or a numerical benefit for the fictional desk; HSE guidance is UK work-design context, not Hong Kong legal advice.

Nine of twelve visitors equals 75% of a convenience sample; the remaining three also value enthusiasm. No tested outcome comparison or causal effect is supplied.

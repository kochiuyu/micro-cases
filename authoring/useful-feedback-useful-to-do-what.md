# Useful feedback. Useful to do what?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 245 -->

PPL-21 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Performance feedback · Task diagnosis · Development conversations

![An experienced adult designer and a studio lead calmly compare printed layout variants beside a laptop and colour swatches.](../useful-feedback-useful-to-do-what-scene.jpg)

## A result is not always a next step

CIPD’s September 2022 evidence review cautions that feedback does not help merely because it is frequent or detailed. Its usefulness depends on the information, the task and the conversation. Research also distinguishes knowing a result from learning what to change next.

Our studio, designer, work record and 20-minute meeting are fictional. They let you choose the focus of one development conversation without assuming that an error reveals a character flaw or that a coaching technique guarantees improvement.

## Opening decision

**Fictional classroom situation.** You lead a small Hong Kong graphic-design studio. Mei is an experienced designer using a familiar workflow. An internal check caught a missing decorative border on a square promotional image. The main rectangular layout was correct. The border is a noncritical brand requirement, not a safety warning, legal disclosure or accessibility element; nothing incorrect was released.

The agreed brief required the border on both versions. The present question is how to use a private **20-minute paid feedback meeting** before the next comparable job. Work has been scheduled to allow it without overtime. The lead is competent with this workflow. Existing independent release checks remain under either route; this is development, not a pay, promotion or disciplinary decision.

Two prepared feedback routes are available. A’s checklist and anonymous benchmark use comparable briefs and the same checked criteria, so they are legitimate evidence, not a public league table. B’s files are available and the designer can explain her sequence. Both include listening to Mei and agreeing a next action. You cannot give each route a full 20 minutes; choose the main emphasis.

## Evidence

| 20-minute feedback route | What it provides | What it leaves less time for |
|---|---|---|
| A · Review the results | Discuss the same five checklist items across recent comparable jobs, a private anonymised peer benchmark, and one priority | Watching how a draft becomes final variants |
| B · Reconstruct the work | Trace one recent job with the designer; compare intended checks with actual files and try a small revision | Discussing the full recent-job pattern and benchmark |

Five checklist items include the border. The initial summary identifies the omission, not where it occurred. No personality or motivation judgement has been made.

**Student question:** Which feedback route would you emphasise in the 20 minutes, A or B? Explain what useful information it could add and what evidence could make you change the emphasis.

## Round two

Before the meeting, the lead and Mei inspect the actual versions and a short workflow note. The note records work steps, not a judgement about effort. The repeated omission is narrower than an overall poor-performance label.

| New information | What is actually observed |
| --- | --- |
| Understanding | Mei accurately explains that both versions need the border and identifies it in an acceptable example |
| Recent work | Two comparable jobs have the border on the main layout but not on the square variant; all other checklist items pass |
| Mei’s account | She checks the master layout, creates the square version afterwards, then checks only that the files open |
| Practice available | A harmless copied sample can be used during the meeting; the next job still receives normal independent release checks |

Mei is willing to examine the sequence but has not yet tested a different final-file check. Her accurate explanation shows that repeating the standard alone would add little; it does not prove that the proposed change will work. The 20-minute allowance and all release safeguards stay the same.

**Student question:** With the new record, how would you use the 20 minutes now? Write a brief task-focused feedback message, specify one action to try, and state what the next comparable job would show if that action is helping.

## Results tell you where to ask, not always what to do

A can make a pattern visible and anchor discussion in agreed standards. B can expose a mismatch between an intended process and the sequence actually used. Neither is automatically better in every situation. Before the reveal, it is reasonable to want either broader pattern evidence or a closer look at one known omission.

The new record strengthens B here: Mei already understands the requirement, and the omission appears in the later-created variant. A further ranking discussion does not directly test that point in the sequence. Keep the accurate checklist as a reference rather than discarding it.

## Say something the recipient can use

One possible message: “The main layout and the other brief requirements passed. The square versions in these two jobs lack the border required in the brief. You checked the master before making the square version. Can we compare the final files with the brief and try a check after both versions exist?”

This separates an observation, a possible process explanation and a proposed test. It does not say Mei is careless, unmotivated or incapable. Invite correction if the reconstruction misses a constraint; the manager’s first explanation is not automatically fact.

## Use the same paid time differently

| Minutes | Use | Evidence produced |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | Confirm the brief, recognise what passed, hear Mei’s account | Shared description of the gap |
| 5 | Compare master and final variants against the brief | A specific place to test a different check |
| 8 | Try checking both final variants on a copied sample | Observe what the check catches and what prompts are needed |
| 4 | Agree a short check cue and follow-up on the next comparable job | One next action and a review point |

The schedule adds to 20 minutes, not 20 minutes for each route. It is an illustrative allocation, not a researched optimal dose. If the sample reveals a wider software or workload problem, stop claiming a simple check will solve it and agree the support needed. Do not remove independent release checking to make the trial look successful.

## Make the next action observable

A concrete cue could be: after all required versions exist, open each final file and compare the specified requirements with the brief. On the next comparable job, note whether Mei uses the cue, whether the border is present in both versions before the independent check, and whether other requirements remain satisfied.

The existing release checker can record these observations in the normal check record. A corrected file today or a confident “I understand” is not evidence of transfer. One later success is encouraging, not proof of a lasting causal effect; review further comparable work without inventing a new quota or sanction.

## Feedback is information, not a guaranteed treatment

The research does not establish that all ranking feedback is harmful, that every mistake needs a rehearsal or that more detail always helps. Information can be redundant, attention can be pulled toward social comparison, and a poorly designed process intervention can disrupt work that was already effective.

The reason for a focused reconstruction here is the supplied gap between known requirements and the point of checking. It is not a general claim about designers. Keep recognition accurate, discuss the actual omission plainly, and test whether the next action provides useful information.

## Instructor note

Use two minutes for the first vote, two to compare the information each route supplies, three for the reveal and a short feedback message, then four to examine the follow-up test. Do not reveal the proposed schedule before students allocate the meeting themselves.

Keep A credible: private, valid evidence, employee voice and a next action are already included. The choice is not respectful coaching versus humiliation. Credit a combined plan if its total remains 20 minutes and it explains what broad discussion is deferred. Ask what would falsify the simple final-check explanation.

This case concerns the informational content of feedback to an established employee on a familiar task after a specific, contained error. No real employee judgement or treatment prescription is supplied.

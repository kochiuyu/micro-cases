# Publish the score. Can rivals copy the proof?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 511 -->

STR-42 · Hong Kong · 15 minutes · Competitive signalling · Credibility · Imitation · Open methodology

![Two electronics repair managers compare a public scorecard with records beside a phone repair bench.](../publish-the-score-or-prove-it-scene.jpg)

## What makes a repair claim comparable?

In March 2024, Hong Kong’s Consumer Council found that repair charges and warranty terms varied among the smartphone brands it surveyed. Separately, new EU labels began displaying repairability grades for covered phones and tablets in June 2025. Those grades concern devices. This fictional Hong Kong scorecard rates repair shops instead.

## Opening decision

A Hong Kong electronics repair chain rates its outlets on quoted-price accuracy, turnaround and availability of parts. Manager Yuki must choose whether to keep the method internal or publish it so local community repair advisers can compare outlets and refer customers.

Publishing lets rival shops reuse the method. Some could display high self-reported scores without keeping the evidence that Yuki’s team collects. Referral loss is the margin the chain would otherwise earn from referred customers but loses to competing claims.

Use expected first-year contribution in HK$ thousands, relative to having no scorecard. All options use the same repair service. Ignore effects after the first year.

## Evidence

| First-year estimate | Internal only | Publish method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Operating and referral gain | 300 | 520 before publication cost and referral loss |
| Publication cost | 0 | 100 |
| Referral loss | 0 | 60 |

**Student question:** Would you publish the method on these estimates? How large could referral loss get before keeping the method internal would be just as good financially?

## Round two

Advisers learn that competitors are more likely to copy the score badge than expected. Without checks, referral loss is now estimated at 40 if copying is limited or 320 if widespread, with a 50% chance of each.

For a fee of 90 to the chain, an independent reviewer would check sample records. Any outlet may apply under the same rules, but only checked outlets may display the verified mark. Yuki’s referral losses fall to 10 or 40 in the same states because advisers distinguish checked from unverified claims. Other gains and costs stay fixed.

**Student question:** Which option now has the highest expected contribution: internal, published without checks, or published with independent checks? What is the largest fee that leaves checked publication at least as good as keeping the method internal?

## Debrief · Calculate the value of opening up

Published contribution is 520 − 100 − 60 = 360, versus 300 internally. The options tie at referral loss of 120. Publishing the method gives advisers a basis for comparison but makes it easier for rivals to present similar scores.

## Debrief · Cost unverified imitation

The revised expected referral loss is (40 + 320) ÷ 2 = 180. Publishing without checks now contributes 520 − 100 − 180 = 240, below the internal 300.

## Debrief · Evaluate independent checks

Checked publication has expected referral loss of (10 + 40) ÷ 2 = 25. Its contribution is 520 − 100 − 90 − 25 = 305; it narrowly beats keeping the score internal. At a fee of 95, checked publication and keeping the method internal each contribute 300.

## Takeaway · Rules can travel faster than proof

An independent check helps advisers recognise evidence-backed claims without excluding a capable rival. In the widespread-copying state, checked publication contributes 290 after the 90 fee, below the internal 300. Its lead in expectation does not guarantee a win in both states.

## Instructor note

Allow 15 minutes: 3 to read the pricing model, 4 for the initial decision, 5 to compare options, and 3 to defend the slim lead. Ask how an adviser could test whether customers recognise the verified mark and whether competing shops could earn it legitimately.

The chain, scoring method, independent checks, referral estimates and financial figures are fictional. Historical consumer research and device labels provide context only, not rules for Hong Kong repair shops.

# Better ratings. The same business?

ENT-12 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Pivot or persevere · Customer problem · Behavioural evidence

![Two founders compare illustrated examples of a shopkeeper planning alone and colleagues handing over work beside a neutral software prototype.](../better-for-whom-scene.jpg)

## Industry context

HKSTP’s Ideation application guidance dated 22 August 2025 asks founders to define a customer problem and plan milestones for testing problem–solution fit.

This is programme guidance, not evidence that a particular venture succeeded. The founders, application, trials and every number below are fictional; no HKSTP participant is being described.

## Opening decision

A fictional Hong Kong venture built a simple work-planning application for solo shop operators. Its original hypothesis: operators need a clearer personal daily task list and will repeatedly use one without reminders from the founders. A free four-week trial included 20 volunteer business accounts: 16 solo operators and four small teams. All received the same application and initial demonstration; there was no founder prompting after onboarding.

One designated user from each account rated ease of use after the demonstration and again after four weeks. All 20 answered both times. The mean rose from 3 to 4 on a five-point scale. The dashboard celebrates the improvement. Several team users also mentioned passing unfinished work to colleagues, rather than planning their own day.

The founders have HK$30,000 and six weeks available for one development-and-learning cycle. A keeps the solo-operator proposition and simplifies daily planning and onboarding. B redirects this cycle toward a shared handover prototype for small teams. Either uses the full HK$30,000 envelope, including development, recruitment and evaluation; they cannot deliver both properly. Existing trial support is funded separately and remains available. Neither option is a full launch or a commitment to abandon the old product permanently. No paid demand, market-size estimate or revenue forecast is supplied.

**Student question:** Use the next HK$30,000 cycle for A, simpler solo planning, or B, team handovers? State the customer problem you would bet on and what the higher ease-of-use rating does not tell you.

## Round two

The usage audit defines sustained use as completing the intended workflow in at least three of the four trial weeks. Two of 16 solo accounts met that threshold for personal planning; all four team accounts met it for handovers. The six sustained accounts are six of the original 20, not six additional recruits. Accounts with no qualifying use remain in their group denominator. The four teams used the same basic task list to leave status and next-action messages for colleagues.

Follow-up conversations with all accounts suggest different explanations. Ten solo operators say their existing paper routine already works. Six solo operators, including the two sustained users, say setup effort remains a barrier; this is a possible explanation, not proof that better onboarding would change behaviour. All four teams describe avoiding forgotten handovers as the main reason to return. These are volunteers, the team group is tiny, and no comparison group establishes the application caused a benefit. There were no access outages in the trial.

The founders can recruit ten additional accounts from whichever group they choose for the next cycle. Recruitment access is available for both groups, not evidence of future market size. The six weeks allow two weeks for preparation and onboarding followed by four weeks of observation. All ten new accounts must remain in the reported denominator, including withdrawals. Any continuation threshold must be written before observing results. Pricing and sustainable delivery costs still need later investigation.

**Student question:** Keep A or redirect to B after the usage audit? Write one testable customer-problem hypothesis for the ten new accounts, a behavioural continuation rule and a result that would make you change course.

## Improvement is not the same as problem–solution fit

The mean ease-of-use rating among the same 20 designated respondents increased. That is useful evidence about perceived usability, not proof of repeated need, willingness to pay or a viable business. There is no missing-response correction to perform here: everyone answered. The mismatch is between what was measured and the business hypothesis.

## Name the customer and the job

Solo sustained use is 2 ÷ 16 = 12.5%; team sustained use is 4 ÷ 4 = 100%; overall sustained use is 6 ÷ 20 = 30%. These describe this volunteer trial, not population retention rates. The two groups used the tool for different jobs, so their percentages are not a controlled comparison of product effectiveness.

The team signal concerns transferring responsibility for unfinished work, not simply another attractive button. Redirecting toward that problem changes the target customer and core value proposition. That is a candidate pivot. Simplifying onboarding for the original solo-planning problem is perseverance with an improved implementation.

## Make a bounded choice, not a victory announcement

B is a defensible next learning priority because observed repeated use and the reported job align. But four teams do not validate a market. A remains defensible if founders judge onboarding to be a plausible, testable obstacle worth one bounded cycle; ten solo accounts saying paper already works makes that a demanding claim.

Choose which uncertainty deserves the same limited budget. B forgoes an immediate chance to improve solo adoption; A postpones investigation of the handover signal. Neither choice authorises neglecting existing trial support, which remains funded. Do not silently turn either option into a commercial rollout.

## Write a rule that can fail

An illustrative B hypothesis: small teams with recurring shift handovers will use the prototype to transfer unfinished tasks without founder reminders. For the ten new teams, record whether a task with a next action is handed over and acknowledged by a colleague in at least three of four observation weeks. A possible managerial continuation rule is at least six of ten teams meeting that definition, with interviews checking that the repeated activity addresses handover work rather than demonstration activity.

Six of ten is an example chosen before results, not a scientific validation threshold. Report every account and any departures; do not count initial training as repeated use. If fewer than six qualify, revisit or stop this hypothesis rather than relabel clicks as success. Meeting the rule supports a further bounded investigation, not a claim of profitable demand. An A test should instead predefine repeated personal planning after simpler onboarding and allow evidence that the original problem is weak. Neither uncontrolled test alone identifies the causal effect of the redesign.

## Instructor note

Allow 3 minutes for the opening vote, 3 for the group usage calculations and 5 for a one-sentence hypothesis and continuation rule. Ask: what exactly changes between A and B—customer, problem or merely interface? Accept a well-defended A or B; challenge certainty about the four-team result.

Keep ease of use, sustained behaviour, customer problem and commercial viability separate. This is not a nonresponse weighting exercise or a stated-interest versus payment case. All thresholds and costs are classroom assumptions. Sources supply regional context and entrepreneurial reasoning; they do not corroborate the invented results or guarantee that pivots improve performance.

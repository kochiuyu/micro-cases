# More reminders. More relationships?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 207 -->

MKT-18 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Relationship marketing · Contact frequency · Customer permission

![Two homeware-shop colleagues discuss communication plans beside a tablet and plain planner.](../permission-is-not-a-message-quota-scene.jpg)

## Permission is the starting point

Hong Kong’s Privacy Commissioner revised its Guidance on Direct Marketing in April 2023. It addresses consent, the permitted use of data and people’s ability to stop marketing. That historical guidance supplies the local context, not a universal recommended email frequency. Our shop, choices and activity logs below are invented.

## Opening decision

A fictional Hong Kong homeware shop sends one product update each week to members who have validly consented to this subject matter and channel, with a stated maximum of one marketing email weekly. Consent records, notices, secure handling and immediate opt-out suppression are already in place. No purchased lists, sensitive profiles or new personal-data fields are proposed. Transactional messages stay separate. Customers retain normal shopping access and prices if they decline marketing.

The manager must set next month’s communication policy. A keeps the familiar weekly default and continues to honour every individual request for less contact or none. B adds a simple self-service choice of weekly updates, one monthly digest or no marketing. Nonresponse leaves the existing authorised weekly default unchanged; it is not new consent. Every chosen limit is binding, and opting out is immediate without completing a preference form. Either policy can honour requests through the existing service channel.

Both fit the same funded content and administration budget: four weekly product stories are already prepared. B assembles a digest from them but displaces a nonessential homepage refresh. Product availability, prices and service do not change. A brings new items to attention sooner; a monthly digest may arrive after an item sells out. Current availability remains visible on the public website. Neither policy is promised to increase sales.

## Evidence

| Policy | Benefit sought | Tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| A: weekly default | Timely product awareness; familiar operation | Repeated contact may feel irrelevant |
| B: visible cadence choice | Members choose contact rhythm | Digest may miss items; refresh postponed |

**Student question:** Which communication policy would you choose, and what would you measure besides how many emails you send?

## Round two

The manager opens the last four weeks’ aggregate logs. Members had previously volunteered their main shopping interest: 100 mostly replenish household supplies and 100 mostly buy occasional gifts. These interests are not frequency instructions. Everyone received four successfully delivered weekly emails. All 14 opt-outs in this simplified log occurred after the fourth email and were immediately suppressed; there were no other list changes. No opted-out member may receive another marketing message under either policy.

| Last four weeks | Replenishment | Occasional gifts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Members at start | 100 | 100 |
| Delivered emails | 400 | 400 |
| Members with any product click | 60 | 20 |
| Members with no product click | 40 | 80 |
| Opt-outs after final email | 2 | 12 |

A product click means a recorded, valid product-link interaction, counted once per member over the whole month. No click does not prove an email was unread or unwanted. The click and opt-out counts may overlap, so do not add them as separate groups. The groups were not randomly assigned, and nobody has yet tried the monthly digest. These descriptive records establish neither the cause of opt-out nor the effect of changing frequency. They also do not reveal purchasing or profit.

Next month begins with 98 replenishment members and 88 gift members still subscribed. Do not automatically label every gift shopper a monthly subscriber. Under B, individuals select their cadence themselves; actual selections are not yet known. Under A, individual requests still override the default. Forecast message counts only under explicit assumptions, and do not contact opted-out people to ask why they left.

**Student question:** Would you keep or change your policy after these logs? Explain the remaining audience, the limits of the evidence and how you would judge the next month without overriding anyone’s choice.

## Debrief: deliveries are not relationships

The log contains 800 delivered emails but only 200 people. There were 80 unique members with a product click, or 40% of the starting audience; 120 had none. The group click proportions were 60% and 20%, not proof that either group valued every email. Opt-outs were 2% and 12% of each starting group. The remaining audience is 98 + 88 = 186, not 200. Four emails to each remaining member would mean 744 sends only if nobody changed preferences or opted out during the next month.

## A reason to offer choice, not a causal forecast

B is defensible because the groups show different engagement and opt-out patterns, and members can express their own cadence rather than being assigned one from a shopping label. The evidence does not demonstrate that B will retain all 186 people or recover lost sales. A remains defensible if timely product awareness matters and the manager accepts the observed contact burden while honouring individual limits. The postponed refresh and missed time-sensitive updates are real tradeoffs, not hidden costs that disappear in the debrief.

## Track the relationship on honest denominators

Keep separate counts of authorised active subscribers, delivered messages, unique product clickers, frequency selections and opt-outs. Report outcomes by initial cohort as well as current recipients; a higher click rate after disengaged people leave is not necessarily improvement. If lawful aggregate purchase measures are available later, treat them separately from clicks and do not attribute every sale to an email. Stop sending when permission ends. A lower send total alone is not success, just as an email opt-out alone does not prove the person stopped buying.

## Instructor note

Ask for a policy and an evidence plan, not a fabricated lifetime-value calculation. No randomised comparison, future selection rate, revenue or margin is supplied. Reward students who separate permission, preferred frequency, observed engagement and customer value. Both policies obey the same rights baseline. This case concerns how often to use an already agreed channel; GVR-05 concerns what data to collect. Do not ask students to send real messages, upload a contact list or visit a source to solve the case.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

All shop facts, counts, timing and costs are original classroom assumptions. Shopping-interest categories are voluntary non-sensitive descriptions, not consent to infer a contact frequency. Every person starts with valid weekly permission; all opt-outs occur after the fourth delivered message solely to make the denominator transparent. Click and opt-out memberships may overlap. No customer-level records are supplied. The same content budget covers either policy; the nonessential homepage refresh is the explicit opportunity cost of B. Consent, first-use notices, immediate suppression and request handling are stipulated safeguards, not a complete legal checklist. Source research does not establish a Hong Kong effect size or an optimal cadence.

- [PCPD: Guidance on Direct Marketing, April 2023 revision](https://www.pcpd.org.hk/english/publications/files/GN_DM_e.pdf). Hong Kong consent, scope and opt-out context; not a recommended marketing cadence or complete case-specific legal opinion.
- [PCPD: Exercising your right of consent and opt-out, January 2013](https://www.pcpd.org.hk/english/news_events/media_statements/files/opt_out_e.pdf). Historical consumer explanation read alongside the later guidance; same regulator and framework, not an independent study.
- [Baek, Chen, Ma and Mitrofanov: Balancing Customer Engagement and Annoyance in Online Retail](https://www.columbia.edu/~wm2428/papers/customer_engagement.pdf). Author-hosted working paper: a North American retailer’s field trial plus separate historical analysis and modelling. No effect sizes or lifetime-value predictions are transferred to this shop.
- [Baymard Institute: Allow Users to Choose the Frequency of Newsletter Emails, 13 December 2022](https://baymard.com/research-articles/newsletter-frequency). Public account of US shopper survey and usability research; not access to the underlying dataset or causal evidence for this shop.
- [Mailchimp: My unsubscribe rate is really high](https://mailchimp.com/help/my-unsubscribe-rate-is-really-high/). Practitioner guidance on relevance, expectations and frequency choices; commercial advice, not an experiment. US legal references are not applied as Hong Kong law.

# A more efficient device. Less total energy?

SUS-21 · Hong Kong · 12–15 minutes · Energy efficiency · Rebound effect · Service and resource use

![Fictional adult colleagues in a community activity room discuss lighting and a plain room schedule beside a worktable.](../efficient-device-total-energy-scene.jpg)

## Efficiency changes the cost of using a service

Energy-efficiency research distinguishes engineering savings at unchanged use from what happens when people change how much they use a service. Lower running costs can encourage additional use, offsetting some savings. That does not mean efficiency is pointless, or that every extra hour of service is undesirable.

The community room, equipment, costs and responses below are invented. No real retrofit result or universal rebound rate is being reported.

## Opening decision

**Fictional classroom case.** You run a community activity room inside a Hong Kong centre. A qualified assessment confirms that a replacement lighting bank supplies the same suitable illumination using 0.60 kW instead of 1.20 kW while on. Electrical safety, access and emergency lighting are separately maintained. You are not dimming occupied work areas or restricting required services.

The room previously used its activity lights for 2,000 hours per year. The centre is already safely staffed and open for 3,000 hours; the room was otherwise left available but not opened for optional activity because the operator limited its lighting budget. All existing 2,000 core activity hours remain protected. Other premises use, staffing and non-lighting loads are stipulated unchanged in this simplified comparison, including any heating or cooling interactions.

Both options install the same lighting for HK$8,000, including qualified installation and first-year servicing. A leaves optional use flexible with normal staff oversight. B adds a transparent, agreed room-use schedule and monthly review for HK$600 over the first year, including all extra administration. No new fee or unpaid labour is assumed. Both are affordable; neither commits to later-year funding.

Electricity costs a constant fictional HK$1.50 per kWh. There are no changed fixed charges, demand charges or other incremental costs. The engineering estimate assumes unchanged 2,000-hour use. Choose provisionally before seeing the room team’s usage plans; the objective balances metered energy, cost and useful access, without a fixed ranking.

## Evidence

| Measure | Existing lights | New lights at unchanged use |
|---|---|---|
| Power while on | 1.20 kW | 0.60 kW |
| Annual on-hours | 2,000 | 2,000 |
| Lighting electricity | 2,400 kWh | 1,200 kWh |
| Electricity cost | HK$3,600 | HK$1,800 |

Energy = power × hours. Same illumination; 50% lower power. The engineering saving of 1,200 kWh assumes unchanged hours. Both options additionally cost HK$8,000 for installation and first-year servicing; B adds HK$600 for the first-year usage programme.

**Student question:** Would you provisionally choose the new lights alone (A) or add the usage programme (B)? Explain what the 50% figure does and does not promise, and what use information could change your choice.

## Round two

After learning that each lit hour will cost HK$0.90 rather than HK$1.80, the room team proposes longer optional activity sessions. For this worked comparison, assume the lower running cost causes the added use: with old lighting and unchanged policy, use would remain 2,000 hours. There is no independent demand growth, new membership or change of room scope. This is a stated counterfactual, not a causal conclusion inferred from before-and-after meter readings.

| Measure | A: flexible use | B: managed use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New-bank power | 0.60 kW | 0.60 kW |
| Annual on-hours | 3,000 | 2,400 |
| Protected core hours | 2,000 | 2,000 |
| Additional optional hours | 1,000 | 400 |
| Usage-programme cost | HK$0 | HK$600 |

Treat these exact hours as planning scenarios for the arithmetic, not forecasts proven by research. A accommodates more requested optional drop-in time. B preserves every core session and adds 400 optional hours through an accessible, published schedule, but forgoes 600 optional hours compared with A. Those hours have real potential value; they are not labelled waste. Residents can book or ask for help in person, and no one is denied a promised session or necessary access.

B switches lights off only when the room is unoccupied and no scheduled session needs them; staff retain manual control. Safety and emergency lighting are unaffected. Staff monitor actual energy, operating hours, unmet optional requests and core access monthly. If core access is impaired, correct the schedule rather than insist on its energy number. A retains normal safe oversight and the same basic meter records, but not B’s extra scheduling and review work.

The HK$8,000 installation is common to both options. B’s HK$600 is the complete additional first-year programme cost. No carbon factor, value per activity hour or lifetime environmental assessment is supplied. Compare the first-year plans and their tradeoffs; do not invent a cash value for community use.

**Student question:** Which first-year plan would you authorise now? Calculate lighting energy and total first-year cash cost for A and B, state the optional access you gain or forgo, and name one measure you would review alongside energy.

## Rebuild the energy saving

At unchanged use, the new lights would consume 0.60 × 2,000 = **1,200 kWh**, versus the old 2,400. A instead uses 0.60 × 3,000 = **1,800 kWh**: a saving of 600 kWh, or 25% of the baseline. B uses 0.60 × 2,400 = **1,440 kWh**: a saving of 960 kWh, or 40%.

Both plans retain a genuine energy reduction in this scenario. The devices still use half the power for the same illumination. Extra hours offset part of the fixed-use engineering saving; they do not make the equipment secretly less efficient.

## Identify the rebound mechanism

For this lighting exercise with unchanged illumination per operating hour, the fraction of engineering savings offset is (engineering saving − realised scenario saving) ÷ engineering saving. For A: (1,200 − 600) ÷ 1,200 = **50%**. For B: (1,200 − 960) ÷ 1,200 = **20%**. These are fractions of the potential saving, not percentages by which total energy rose.

The stipulated mechanism is lower cost per lit hour leading to more hours of the same service: direct rebound. Actual energy remains below baseline, so this is partial offset, not backfire. Extra use for unrelated reasons would require a different causal explanation. These invented percentages are not estimates for Hong Kong community centres or lighting generally.

## Compare costs on the same boundary

A’s electricity costs 1,800 × HK$1.50 = **HK$2,700**. Adding the common HK$8,000 installation gives **HK$10,700** for the first-year package. B’s electricity is 1,440 × HK$1.50 = HK$2,160; plus HK$600 administration and HK$8,000 installation gives **HK$10,760**.

Relative to A, B saves 360 kWh and HK$540 on electricity, but costs HK$600 to operate: **HK$60 more overall**. B is not the cheaper first-year option. Its extra programme would just break even on these electricity savings if it cost HK$540, holding use unchanged. That threshold is conditional, not a market quotation.

These are first-year cash comparisons, not lifetime net-present-value or carbon rankings. Do not subtract the installation twice or compare one plan’s electricity-only bill with the other plan’s complete cost.

## Protect service, not just the target

**A can be defended** for greater optional flexibility at slightly lower total cash cost, while retaining a 25% scenario energy reduction. It accepts more energy use than B and must not advertise the unchanged-hours 50% saving as its total reduction.

**B can be defended** for a larger 40% energy reduction at a small additional cash cost, while preserving core access. It explicitly gives up 600 optional activity hours relative to A. A smaller energy number does not by itself prove greater community welfare.

No supplied value for optional access or energy priority makes one plan uniquely best. Explain the priority, preserve safety and agreed core services, and review both resource use and unmet optional requests. Do not ration essential service simply to reproduce an engineering estimate.

## What the meter cannot establish alone

Record lighting kWh, on-hours, suitable illumination, core sessions delivered and optional requests accommodated or declined. Compare like periods and explain changes in membership, weather, other loads and schedules in real evaluations. A before-and-after total alone does not establish that cheaper use caused the difference.

This exercise isolates one lighting bank and direct usage response. It does not calculate equipment manufacture, disposal, other purchases funded by saved money or economy-wide effects. It also does not convert kWh into greenhouse-gas reductions. Wider environmental claims require a wider evidence boundary.

## Instructor note

Use 12–15 minutes: three for the provisional choice, four for the usage reveal and energy calculation, three for costs, and two to five for the access tradeoff. Keep the debrief hidden until the second decision. Students can multiply kW by hours; no elasticity estimation is needed.

Ask: what exactly increased, and why? Distinguish lower power, longer use, remaining energy savings and service value. Challenge both “half the watts means half the annual bill” and “rebound means efficiency never works”. Do not use the invented 50% and 20% as empirical estimates.

SUS-12 concerns shifting electricity timing and emission factors; GVR-04 concerns the scope of an intensity claim without a supplied causal account. SUS-08 concerns landlord–tenant incentives. Here the central mechanism is the operator’s usage response to cheaper service, and whether to pay for a transparent access-preserving management plan.

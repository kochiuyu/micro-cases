# A commuter pass. Did it replace a drive?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 568 -->

SUS-47 · Hong Kong · 15 minutes · Transport demand · Vehicle movements · Subsidy design

![Two colleagues review a commute survey beside a Hong Kong transit platform.](../the-pass-did-it-replace-the-drive-scene.jpg)

## Passenger journeys and vehicle movements are different measures

Hong Kong’s Transport Department uses travel surveys to describe personal trips and separately publishes transport patronage and road traffic statistics. A Hong Kong employer can face a similar measurement question: whether a funded trip changes what a vehicle does. The employer and all figures below are fictional.

## Opening decision

Kit runs travel benefits for a fictional Hong Kong hospital’s office staff. Broad offers the same public-transport credit to any interested worker. Target offers a higher credit to workers who volunteer for a programme aimed at replacing previous car travel. The exhibit is for one representative week, with equivalent workdays and no shift changes.

Each solo-drive replacement removes one car trip. Two participating carpool riders travelling in the same car remove one car trip if the lift is cancelled; neither removes a car trip if the car still runs. An already-existing public-transport trip does not remove a car trip. All counts are one-way trips and all are in those three nonoverlapping categories.

Prefer the programme that removes the most car trips with weekly spending no higher than HK$1,300 and at least 90 credited worker trips. All added passengers fit existing capacity. The exhibit assumes the relevant carpool lifts will be cancelled. Vehicle distance, travel time and emissions have not been measured.

## Evidence

| Fictional weekly pilot | Broad | Target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Already public-transport trips | 120 | 30 |
| Solo-drive trips replaced | 30 | 50 |
| Carpool rider trips replaced | 40 | 20 |
| Weekly programme cost | HK$1,000 | HK$1,250 |

**Student question:** For each pilot, count credited passenger trips and removed car trips. Which meets the rule best, and why would a comparison of all credited trips suggest a different answer?

## Round two

After checking lift logs, Kit finds that Target’s ten affected shared car movements still take place because a third occupant, the driver, continues to make each trip. Also, 18 of Target’s 50 reported solo-drive replacements were already using public transport. The verified 18 still receive the credit; no other estimate changes, and Broad’s affected shared lifts are all cancelled.

**Student question:** Which should Kit choose after the verification? Holding the other revised Target counts fixed, how many additional confirmed solo-drive replacements would Target need to beat Broad on car trips removed?

## Debrief · Start with the right denominator

Broad credits 120 + 30 + 40 = 190 passenger trips; Target credits 30 + 50 + 20 = 100. Both fit the budget and participation floor. Broad removes 30 + 40 ÷ 2 = 50 car trips; Target removes 50 + 20 ÷ 2 = 60, so Target initially wins.

## Debrief · Check what vehicles actually do

Target still credits 100 passenger trips, but removes 50 − 18 = 32 solo-drive trips and no carpool vehicle trips. Broad still removes 50, so it wins. Target needs 19 additional confirmed solo-drive replacements to strictly beat 50, holding the other outcomes fixed.

## Debrief · Separate three useful counts

Track credited trips, previously driven trips among participants and verified removed vehicle movements. Estimate distances and additional service before making a carbon claim. A credit supporting affordably accessible transport can still have value even when it does not reduce a single car movement.

## Instructor note

Use 2 minutes to read, 4 to compare units, 4 to reconcile the logs and 5 to discuss measurement without collecting individual sensitive travel histories. This model does not forecast emissions or service crowding.

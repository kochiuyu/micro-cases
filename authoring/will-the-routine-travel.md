# It worked in the last business. Will the routine travel?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 211 -->

ENT-18 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Experiential learning · Transfer of routines · Causal ambiguity

![A desk-organiser founder and colleague discuss a prototype beside a plain catalogue.](../will-the-routine-travel-scene.jpg)

## Experienced founders can still be learners

On 17 February 2022, Singapore Management University announced a new Business Innovations Generator cohort that included seven serial entrepreneurs. Its account described experienced founders seeking feedback and support. This is regional context, not evidence that one programme or selling routine improves performance. The founder and all figures below are fictional.

## Opening decision

A Hong Kong founder previously sold standard stationery replenishment packs. She now offers modular desktop organisers to small design studios. The product, price and one target customer group are fixed for a two-week selling trial. Her old routine used a short catalogue conversation followed by a quotation. In the last 20 recorded sales meetings in the old business, 16 led to orders. She wants to reuse that routine rather than start from nothing.

She has 24 funded founder hours and HK$4,000 all-in for one trial format. A adapts the old catalogue: four hours of preparation, twenty half-hour conversations and ten hours of follow-up. B develops a fit-and-use demonstration: eight hours of preparation, eight one-hour conversations and eight hours of follow-up. Both fit the allowance; preparation and scheduling prevent combining the formats within this trial. B explores customer questions more deeply but reaches fewer people.

Twenty adult studio decision-makers have voluntarily agreed to a trial conversation. A can meet all twenty; B uses eight selected by a pre-agreed rotation, with no claim of a random experiment. Neither format has been sold or promised to those people, and either invitation can be declined. Required operations, product checks, staffing and existing commitments are funded separately. Demonstrations use the venture’s own safe prototypes and synthetic examples, not customer documents. No sales forecast or comparable margin forecast is supplied.

## Evidence

| Format | Preparation | Conversations | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: catalogue + quote | 4 hours | 20 × 0.5 = 10 hours | 10 hours |
| B: fit-and-use demonstration | 8 hours | 8 × 1 = 8 hours | 8 hours |

**Student question:** Which selling format would you choose for the two-week trial, A or B? Explain which part of the founder’s past experience you expect to transfer and what you sacrifice with that choice.

## Round two

A closer reading separates the old 20 meetings into three non-overlapping groups. The 16 orders were not all first purchases by unfamiliar customers.

| Old sales meetings | Meetings | Orders |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Repeat buyers | 12 | 11 |
| New buyers introduced by a trusted intermediary | 6 | 5 |
| Unfamiliar buyers without an introduction | 2 | 0 |

All twenty current prospects are first-time buyers from this founder, without an introduction from the old intermediary. None has yet explained whether a catalogue is enough to assess the new organiser or whether a demonstration would change a purchase decision. A can still communicate a clear standard offer efficiently. B may expose fit questions and build understanding, but a longer conversation does not guarantee trust or an order. Two unfamiliar old meetings are too few to establish that A cannot work.

Not every old routine needs replacement. The founder has checked that her stock-count checklist, order confirmation and cash-record process fit this product’s workflow; both alternatives use them. Product checks and truthful descriptions are also unchanged. The decision is which selling routine to test, not whether to discard experience, change customer segment or recreate the old relationships. The same time and cash allowances remain.

**Student question:** Would you retain or change your trial choice after separating the old meetings? Identify what can transfer, what depended on the old setting, and what evidence would justify using the chosen selling routine again.

## Separate the routine from its setting

The old total is 16 / 20 = 80%, but 18 meetings involved repeat buyers or an introduction. Eleven repeat-buyer orders plus five introduced-buyer orders account for all sixteen orders. Familiarity and endorsement are possible explanations alongside the catalogue routine. This record does not identify their causal effects, and the two unfamiliar meetings do not estimate a reliable future conversion rate.

## Adapt selectively

Experience can save effort without supplying a ready-made answer. Preserve checked operating routines while testing whether the sales conversation travels to first-time buyers. A gives broader exposure to a simple offer. B uses scarce hours to investigate fit and explanation. Choosing B does not prove the old routine was bad; choosing A does not justify forecasting sixteen new orders.

## Specify the next learning decision

Record what prospects understood, unresolved fit questions, reasons for declining and any actual authorised orders and follow-up effort. Judge whether the routine produces an understandable offer and a feasible route to paid business. No conversion comparison between unequal, non-random trial groups is available here. Before another round, decide whether the evidence supports keeping the format, changing its explanation or reconsidering the offer itself.

## Instructor note

Ask students to distinguish a transferable action from a missing enabling condition. Accept A if students value reach and explicitly reject the old 80% as a forecast; accept B if they value deeper diagnosis and acknowledge its opportunity cost. ENT-13 concerns network access to missing resources; ENT-16 chooses a first customer segment. This case holds the segment fixed and examines a founder’s interpretation of prior success. No outreach or sale is performed by the classroom exercise.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

All founder, product, customer, budget and meeting facts are original fiction. A uses 4 + 20 × 0.5 + 10 = 24 hours; B uses 8 + 8 × 1 + 8 = 24 hours. Both use the same all-in HK$4,000 envelope, with essential operations separately funded. Old groups total 12 + 6 + 2 = 20 meetings and 11 + 5 + 0 = 16 orders. Relationships and product differ between ventures, so 80% is descriptive only. No counterfactual or causal effect is identified. Current prospects are first-time buyers with valid voluntary contact permission; a rotation for eight conversations is not random assignment. No confidential customer documents are required. Five sources provide institutional context and differently bounded research, not five demonstrations of this fictional routine.

- [SMU: BIG January 2022 cohort, 17 February 2022](https://news.smu.edu.sg/news/2022/02/17/smus-business-innovations-generator-welcomes-its-january-2022-cohort-0). Dated regional context includes serial founders seeking feedback. Institutional announcement, not an outcome evaluation.
- [Simon Parker: Do serial entrepreneurs run successively better-performing businesses? (2013)](https://effectuation.org/hubfs/Journal%20Articles/2017/05/Do-serial-entrepreneurs-1.pdf). Longitudinal US PSID study of performance trajectories. Its temporary-benefit finding is not a conversion forecast for this venture.
- [Gottschalk, Greene and Müller: habitual experience and new firm closure (online 2016)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11187-016-9780-3). German firm study discusses difficulty attributing outcomes to actions. Closure outcomes do not identify whether this catalogue routine works.
- [HBS Working Knowledge: entrepreneurial skills and behaviours, 6 June 2016](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/skills-and-behaviors-that-make-entrepreneurs-successful). Institutional account of assessment research distinguishes skills and behaviours across venture contexts. Self-assessment is not causal proof of success.
- [Gompers and colleagues: Skill vs. Luck in Entrepreneurship and Venture Capital (2006 working paper)](https://haas.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/w12592.pdf). VC-backed serial-entrepreneur study uses a different success outcome. Persistence in that setting does not validate this sales script or imply experience is worthless.

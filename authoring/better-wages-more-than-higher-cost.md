## A wage changes more than an expense line

Economists study employer wage-setting power when workers do not respond to each firm as if all jobs were interchangeable. Search difficulties and differences in location, schedules or other job features can matter. A firm need not be the only employer for its own hiring supply to rise with the wage it offers.

The fictional schedule below asks how a posted wage and total payroll can change by different amounts. The actual pay coverage is confirmed later. It is a classroom model, not an estimate of any Hong Kong employer’s market power or advice to suppress pay.

## Opening decision

A fictional Hong Kong event-services planner is preparing a voluntary one-shift crew for an optional new client event. No offers have been made, no existing worker’s pay will be reduced and no accepted client commitment depends on taking this event. Qualified people may decline freely. All quoted wages and conditions are stipulated to meet applicable legal and contractual requirements; benefits and other employment obligations are separately funded and unchanged.

The exhibit gives the minimum posted shift wage that attracts each crew size, from one to five equally qualified workers, under this simplified forecast. Each wage is per person for the same paid shift, not an hourly rate. “Added contribution” means the extra event revenue after non-wage variable costs produced by adding that worker. The figures are invented, certain within this exercise, and already account for the equipment and task constraints. Work remains safe at every listed crew size; fewer workers mean accepting a smaller event scope, not unpaid overtime.

The planner is deciding provisionally between three and four workers. The fourth adds HK$1,440 before labour pay and is quoted HK$1,280 for the shift. A colleague says that leaves HK$160 extra. However, the pay administrator has not yet confirmed whether that quote changes the rate paid to the other three workers. Do not make offers until the rule is confirmed. There are no recruitment fees, extra taxes or changing benefits in the comparison; other fixed costs are already covered.

## Round two

The administrator confirms a common-wage rule: every person in the chosen crew receives that crew size’s posted rate. Thus a four-person crew pays all four HK$1,280, not three at HK$1,120 plus a fourth at HK$1,280. The table describes alternative complete plans, not a sequence of signed individual contracts. No pay cut or unequal arrangement is an available workaround.

The planner can select zero through five workers, with zero meaning decline the optional event and earn no event contribution. Larger crews are unavailable this shift. There is no additional productivity, retention or future-sales effect beyond the supplied figures. Total event contribution before pay is the sum of the added contributions through the chosen crew size. Subtract the entire crew payroll to compare the employer’s contribution towards already-covered fixed costs and surplus.

For this calculation only, the objective is to maximise that employer contribution. This does not establish what workers should earn, whether the wage-setting situation is desirable or what policy should apply. Worker preferences, alternatives, bargaining conditions and broader social effects require separate evidence. Keep rights, voluntary participation and safe conditions intact.

**Student question:** After confirming the common-wage rule, which crew size maximises the supplied employer contribution? Reconcile the fourth worker’s full payroll effect, then explain why this calculation alone does not establish a fair wage or a socially best outcome.

## Build the whole payroll

At three workers, payroll is 3 × HK$1,120 = HK$3,360. At four, it is 4 × HK$1,280 = HK$5,120. The increase is HK$1,760: HK$1,280 for the additional worker plus 3 × HK$160 = HK$480 of increases for the others. The new worker’s wage is therefore not the marginal hiring expenditure, meaning the total payroll increase caused by expanding the crew.

## Compare like with like

The fourth worker adds HK$1,440 before pay but raises payroll by HK$1,760. Employer contribution falls by HK$320. The opening HK$160 claim left out HK$480 paid to the other three workers. The larger crew produces more event contribution before wages, while its extra payroll costs more than that increase. Higher output and higher employer surplus are different claims.

## Why expenditure rises faster than the wage

Under a common posted rate, moving up this firm’s labour-supply schedule raises pay for the whole chosen crew. Incremental payroll for workers one through five is HK$800, HK$1,120, HK$1,440, HK$1,760 and HK$2,080. Compare these increments with the corresponding added contributions, not with the five individual wage quotes. This result depends on the stated common-rate rule; it is not a rule for every employment arrangement.

## What the model leaves open

Monopsony describes employer-side buying power in a labour market; modern uses include more than a literal single employer. This exercise stipulates one firm’s wage-and-availability schedule. It does not measure actual outside options or prove misconduct. Concentration statistics alone also do not establish the size of wage-setting power: job definitions, geography, worker mobility and market conditions matter.

Empirical studies and richer search models investigate those issues using evidence and assumptions beyond this table. For example, one research study used selected US online vacancies and posted wages, not every worker’s final pay; a separate theoretical model studies information and search frictions. Neither supplies our fictional numbers or makes this employer-only objective a social recommendation.

## Instructor note

First ask learners to identify the missing pay coverage rather than reward an unconditional hiring answer. After the reveal, have pairs build total payroll and the fourth-worker increment before comparing all crew sizes. Accept a provisional four-person preference only if explicitly conditional on the unconfirmed rule. The final numerical answer is three workers for the stipulated employer objective, not a recommendation for real wage suppression. Ask what additional evidence would be needed to evaluate worker welfare and actual bargaining conditions without inventing a policy answer. Distinguish this mechanism from a pay-fairness or employee-retention case.

# Four times the reports. Four times the mistakes?

Case 51 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · People · Employee voice · Learning culture

![Three print-shop colleagues discuss a sample proof around a table in a calm team meeting.](../more-reports-scene.jpg)

## In the news

Airport Authority Hong Kong’s April 2024 newsletter reported a Work Improvement Team Convention held on 11 March 2024, where staff presented improvement projects.

That is an example of organised employee participation, not evidence of suppressed reporting or a quality problem at the airport. Our separate print-shop situation below is fictional.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong print shop introduces a short weekly discussion where staff can flag order mistakes without being publicly singled out. Its existing quality requirements and checks remain unchanged.

The dashboard counts distinct completed orders that staff reported as containing a mistake: four in the previous month and sixteen in the current month. Multiple reports about the same order count only once.

The owner worries that the new approach has weakened standards. She proposes replacing the discussion with compulsory supervisor approval of every order. The team has not yet compared reporting with an independent review of completed work.

## Evidence

| Dashboard measure | Previous month | Current month |
|---|---:|---:|
| Distinct completed orders staff reported as containing a mistake | 4 | 16 |

Duplicate reports about an order count once. Report counts do not establish all mistakes or severity.

**Student question:** Would you introduce approval of every order, retain the discussion, or investigate first? What do the two dashboard figures actually measure?

## Round two

For this classroom exercise, assume an independent, complete and consistently performed review of retained order records identifies every completed order meeting the same mistake definition in both months. Real reviews may miss problems; completeness is a simplifying assumption here.

There were 1,000 completed orders in each month. The review identifies 20 orders containing a mistake in each month. The four and sixteen staff-reported orders are verified subsets of those respective twenty, with no duplicates or false reports.

No information about mistake severity, underlying causes or other changes in the shop is supplied.

**Student question:** How should the manager respond now? Compare reporting coverage with the error rate, then propose one action that preserves both employee voice and quality standards.

## Separate visibility from the underlying rate

Reporting coverage among the reviewed mistaken orders rises from 4/20 = 20% to 16/20 = 80%.

The reviewed order-error rate stays at 20/1,000 = 2% in each month. Sixteen divided by four is four, but four times the reports does not mean four times the mistaken orders.

These figures do not establish that the discussion caused the reporting change, that everyone feels safe speaking up, or that quality improved. Equal counts also do not establish equal severity or cost.

## Thank the signal; correct the problem

A defensible response is to retain a usable reporting channel while investigating recurring causes and assigning corrective actions. Thank people for surfacing a problem, not for making the mistake.

Keep quality expectations and proportionate checks. Whether every order needs supervisor approval requires evidence about error types, risk, effectiveness and workload; the rise in reports alone does not justify it.

Psychological safety means people can raise concerns and admit uncertainty without undue interpersonal fear. It does not remove responsibility for careful work or following through on improvements.

## Close the feedback loop

For each important recurring issue, name an action owner and a review date. Tell staff what was investigated and changed, without unnecessary public identification of individuals. Offer another appropriate route when a group discussion is not suitable.

Track independently reviewed error rates, severity and repeat problems alongside reporting coverage. In real work, also test the reliability and consistency of the review itself.

Do not set “zero reports” or a reporting-volume quota as the success measure. More reports are useful when they help the team understand and address problems, not simply because the number is larger.

## Instructor note

Take a vote before the reveal. Ask students to label the numerator and denominator of each measure. Then ask for a managerial response that welcomes accurate information without lowering standards.

Keep the conclusion narrow: the stipulated records refute the claim that mistakes necessarily quadrupled. They do not prove a particular management practice caused improvement. All print-shop data and proposals are fictional and unrelated to the airport.

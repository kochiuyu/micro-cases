# Larger factory. Lower cost from what?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 390 -->

STR-33 · Taiwan · 15–20 minutes · Scale and utilisation · Learning and experience · Sources of cost advantage

![Two adult managers in a tidy Taiwanese specialty workshop compare a current production line with a tabletop plant layout, while an experienced colleague documents a safe handling routine.](../larger-factory-lower-cost-from-what-scene.jpg)

## More than one reason costs fall

A lower unit cost can reflect more units sharing a fixed bill, improved production methods, or cheaper inputs. These mechanisms do not imply the same growth strategy. The Taiwanese producer and all figures below are fictional; no source establishes a universal learning rate or guarantees that a larger plant is cheaper.

## Opening decision

You lead fictional Taichung producer Formosa Display, making one unchanged specialty display fitting. For the next twelve months, choose G: lease a larger equipped plant, or L: renew the current line and fund a structured learning programme. Both routes retain production internally; no outsourcing decision is involved.

An earlier month produced and sold 1,000 good fittings at average production cost NT$420. A later month produced and sold 2,000 at NT$300. A colleague argues that doubling output cut cost substantially, so adding a larger plant should continue the same reduction. The cost records have not yet been separated by cause.

The current line can safely make up to 2,500 good fittings per month; G can make 4,000. Quality, product specification and delivery service are identical. There is no hidden bottleneck. Paid breaks, wage rates and safe working conditions are protected, and no staffing reduction is part of either proposal.

L’s annual fixed operating commitment is NT$1,440,000 plus NT$120,000 for paid coaching, method documentation and improvement reviews. G’s total annual fixed commitment is NT$2,880,000, including relocation, transition support and an equivalent learning programme. The larger plant is not an alternative to learning; both routes preserve time for it.

These are complete twelve-month service-and-lease packages, not building purchases. All route-specific setup and exit costs are included; no residual asset or later payment remains after the comparison. Both are affordable. The objective is lower total relevant cost for the same required sales, without reducing quality or service.

The approved sales quantity and continuing input prices await the detailed review. No investment has been signed. Produce only for the approved sales plan: unsold output cannot be treated as a cost-saving benefit. Ignore identical corporate overhead and revenue; no price premium, additional market share or strategic benefit is quantified.

## Evidence

| Measure | Earlier | Later |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Good units sold | 1,000 | 2,000 |
| Average production cost | NT$420 | NT$300 |

**Student question:** Would you provisionally favour G or L, or withhold a recommendation? What would you need to separate in the NT$120 unit-cost fall before treating it as evidence for a larger plant?

## Round two

Both historical months incurred the same NT$120,000 fixed production bill. Earlier variable material cost was NT$180 per good unit and variable conversion cost NT$120. Later they were NT$150 and NT$90. The table below reconciles those records; the accounting boundary and product mix did not change.

| Component | Earlier | Later |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed bill ÷ monthly units | 120 | 60 |
| Materials | 180 | 150 |
| Conversion | 120 | 90 |
| Total | 420 | 300 |

Verified method records attribute the NT$30 conversion saving to routines developed through repeated production: less rework and fewer handling steps per good fitting. Input wage rates, quality checks and work protections did not change. This causal attribution is a classroom assumption supported by stipulated records, not inferred merely from the two monthly averages.

The NT$30 material saving was a temporary supplier promotion, unrelated to purchase quantity. It has ended. The confirmed next-year price is NT$180 per unit under either route, with no bulk rebate. The routine that reduced conversion cost remains usable at either site; merely leasing more space provides no additional conversion saving.

For this comparison, next-year sales are fixed at 2,000 good units every month, 24,000 in total. All can be supplied safely by either route. There are no additional sales, inventory builds or later recapture. Both funded programmes maintain the existing methods; budget conversion at NT$90 per unit throughout the year.

The programmes also test further improvements, but no extra reduction is assumed in this decision. Keep the same design and experienced team; both routes include needed knowledge transfer and induction. Actual performance must be reviewed. More cumulative experience could help, but neither a doubling rule nor unlimited cost reduction has been established.

**Student question:** Which route would you recommend for the stated year? Reconcile the historical saving, calculate each route’s annual and unit cost, and explain which evidence would justify revisiting a larger plant without assuming that capacity creates demand or learning.

## Debrief · Three different mechanisms

The unit-cost drop is NT$420 − NT$300 = NT$120, or about 28.6%. NT$60 comes from spreading the unchanged NT$120,000 monthly bill across 2,000 rather than 1,000 units. NT$30 comes from the stipulated method improvement; NT$30 comes from a temporary input-price reduction.

Only half the observed fall is fixed-cost spreading. That effect requires more current units while the relevant fixed bill remains unchanged. It is not proof of a superior larger-plant technology or of long-run economies of scale. G changes the fixed commitment, so the old denominator calculation cannot simply be carried forward.

## Debrief · Volume is not experience

Current monthly output determines how a fixed monthly bill is shared. Cumulative production experience concerns what the organisation has learned over repeated work. A firm can retain useful routines without doubling current output; adding unused capacity does not itself create experience. Here existing conversion knowledge is available under both routes.

The material promotion changes the price of an input, not the amount needed or the team’s skill. It is explicitly not a volume discount. In other settings purchasing scale can improve supplier terms, but that mechanism is absent here. Separate the three drivers before projecting a persistent cost advantage.

## Debrief · Cost the actual sales plan

Annual variable production cost is 24,000 × (NT$180 + NT$90) = NT$6,480,000 under either route. L adds NT$1,440,000 + NT$120,000 = NT$1,560,000 fixed costs: total NT$8,040,000, or NT$335 per good unit. The funded learning programme is included, not free.

G adds NT$2,880,000: total NT$9,360,000, or NT$390 per good unit. L saves NT$1,320,000 over the year, equivalent to NT$55 per unit. Choose L for the supplied equal-sales, equal-service objective. This is a relevant-cost comparison, not total company profit or a permanent instruction never to expand.

## Debrief · Explain the rise from NT$300

L’s NT$335 exceeds the recent NT$300 because the temporary material saving disappears, adding NT$30, and its annual learning programme adds NT$120,000 ÷ 24,000 = NT$5. Retaining the existing NT$90 conversion cost does not cancel those changes. A rising unit cost need not mean the team forgot its methods.

## Debrief · Reopen the growth case with evidence

G’s unused capacity is not sold output. Dividing its costs by 48,000 units would assume twice the supplied sales. Future expansion needs credible additional demand and prices, site-specific productivity evidence, transition costs and a comparison with other feasible capacity routes. None is supplied for a new growth scenario.

Track input prices, fixed commitments, good output, rework and conversion resources separately. Record which methods are learned and whether another team can reproduce them. Further savings should enter a revised case only after supported testing. Do not turn a historical percentage into a guaranteed annual improvement target.

## Instructor note

Allow three minutes for the first recommendation, four for the cost bridge, five for the annual comparison and three for a corrected growth argument. Ask which costs change because of current output, acquired know-how or input prices. Keep total fixed costs distinct from fixed cost per unit.

STR-03 concerns which capability to outsource; OPS-03 concerns the limiting production stage. Here both routes have sufficient internal capacity and preserve learning. The strategic issue is whether a claimed cost advantage requires more scale, rather than whether a faster station or an outside supplier can fulfil the work.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

All firms, costs, quantities, capacity, learning records, quotations and sales are original classroom fiction. NT$ denotes New Taiwan dollars. Product quality, safe work, wage rates and service remain unchanged. Historical costs share a constant accounting boundary. The conversion-saving attribution is stipulated by verified method records, not statistically identified from two observations. Next-year 24,000 sales and per-unit NT$180 materials plus NT$90 conversion are fixed comparison assumptions, not real forecasts. Fixed annual packages include all route-specific transition, learning and exit costs, with no retained assets or subsequent liabilities. Equal common overhead and revenue are omitted; no profit, NPV or general technology ranking is claimed. Further learning, supplier discounts and extra demand are not credited. Source concepts are educational; the historic experience-curve argument does not establish a universal percentage reduction.

- [OpenStax, Principles of Economics 2e, 7.5 Costs in the Long Run](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-2e/pages/7-5-costs-in-the-long-run). Explains alternative production technologies and scale-dependent average costs. A within-plant fixed-cost change is not by itself a long-run technology comparison.
- [CORE Econ, Economy, Society, and Public Policy, section 7.2](https://books.core-econ.org/espp/book/text/07.html). Separates technological scale advantages, spreading fixed costs and supplier bargaining. This case supplies no quantity-linked input discount.
- [Bruce Henderson, The Experience Curve, BCG, 1968](https://www.bcg.com/publications/1968/business-unit-strategy-growth-experience-curve). Original historical strategy argument links cumulative experience with cost and acknowledges managerial effort. Its broad rate and universality claims are not adopted as a law or forecast.
- [NASA, Cost Estimating Handbook v4.0, Appendix C, February 2015](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/ceh_appc.pdf). Cost-driver and normalization sections distinguish observed association from a supported causal explanation. Aerospace estimating practice does not supply this producer’s costs.
- [Karen Willcox and Olivier de Weck, MIT OpenCourseWare, Design for Value, lecture 19, 2010](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/ids-338j-multidisciplinary-system-design-optimization-spring-2010/6b2cfeb01c320b2d85f2e4c5dd67dd55_MITESD_77S10_lec19.pdf). Recurring-cost and learning-curve slides illustrate reductions through repeated production. No aircraft rate, curve coefficient or cost share is transferred to this case.

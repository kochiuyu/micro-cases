## Progression need not mean more direct reports

Technical careers can grow through wider contribution without becoming people-management careers. Public engineering frameworks distinguish advanced specialist work from supervising staff, while expecting collaboration in both. These are examples, not evidence that one structure suits every workplace. The Shenzhen studio and proposed roles below are fictional; no employee is being assessed or appointed.

## Opening decision

You lead fictional Harbour Form, a Shenzhen product-design studio with 12 designers and two existing supervisors. Several designers ask how advanced technical work can lead to greater responsibility and recognition. Nobody has been promised promotion. Current employment terms, rights and required consultation processes remain protected; today’s decision is a proposed career structure, not an appointment.

A opens one additional supervisor vacancy by transferring four designers’ reporting relationships from the existing supervisors. It includes workload allocation, regular one-to-one meetings, performance feedback and escalation responsibilities. It is not an honorary title. An appointee would spend substantially less time on personal design work and receive management development and cover.

B creates a senior technical specialist level without direct reports. Its proposed work combines difficult design problems, reusable design methods and technical mentoring across project teams. The current supervisors would retain staff allocation and performance responsibilities. Before announcing B, the studio must verify recurring work, meaningful standards, funded recognition and clear decision boundaries.

Leadership can fund one progression role, not both, within the coming year’s staffing plan. No extra headcount, unpaid work or removal of existing supervision is allowed. Either route requires a transparent later selection process and an interested, suitable candidate. The studio has not yet completed its workload review or confirmed B’s pay band.

## Evidence

| Route | Distinct responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| A · Supervisor | Four direct reports; people-management decisions |
| B · Specialist | Cross-project technical contribution; no direct reports |
| Both | Clear standards, fair assessment and funded work |

**Student question:** Which route would you develop first, and what evidence must you obtain before announcing it? Explain why excellent personal design work alone neither qualifies someone for supervision nor fully defines an advanced specialist role.

## Round two

The review confirms that the existing supervisors have sufficient funded capacity for all required supervision. No additional supervisory workload or service gap has emerged. In contrast, several projects repeatedly need help with difficult design decisions, reusable methods and technical mentoring. Transferring four reporting relationships under A would redistribute existing duties, not meet a newly identified management need.

For B, the proposed paid weekly work plan allocates 24 hours to complex design delivery, eight to technical mentoring and eight to reusable methods and peer reviews: 40 hours in total. Existing project commitments have been reallocated within funded capacity. Mentoring is not an extra obligation on top of a full delivery workload.

Finance confirms a RMB24,000–28,000 monthly salary band for either role and adequate funding for the one approved position. The specialist has the same access to the studio’s advanced-grade development budget and design-review forums as the supervisor grade. These are stipulated company choices, not legal equal-pay rules or market salary benchmarks.

The proposed specialist criteria require sustained handling of complex design work, reusable methods that colleagues can apply, and mentoring that builds others’ capability. Evidence comes from project work and peer feedback, not personal output alone or years served. The specialist leads technical reviews; supervisors retain workload, leave and performance decisions. Unresolved technical priorities go to the studio head.

**Student question:** Choose the progression structure now. Specify what would make the specialist pathway credible, what responsibilities it must not quietly absorb, and what evidence would justify reopening the supervisor option later. Do not select or rank an actual employee.

## Debrief · Match progression to the needed work

I would develop B first. The review identifies recurring technical contribution beyond individual delivery and confirms that supervision is already adequately covered. A remains a genuine management role, but its proposed reporting transfer does not solve the identified gap. Creating another supervisor mainly to confer status would confuse recognition with organisational need.

This does not mean management is inferior, technical experts cannot manage, or existing supervisors will never need help. If sustained supervisory work later exceeds funded capacity, reconsider A with a real workload case and role-fit assessment. Neither a promotion request nor an impressive design portfolio establishes that need by itself.

## Debrief · Give the specialist a different contribution

A senior specialist must do more than the same personal work under a grander title. Here the added contribution includes solving difficult shared problems, building reusable approaches and helping colleagues reason through technical choices. Strong individual delivery matters, but contribution beyond one’s own project is part of the supplied role.

Mentoring and technical leadership involve people, communication and disagreement. “No direct reports” does not mean working alone or avoiding collaboration. Equally, mentoring is not authority to rate performance, allocate leave or become an unofficial supervisor. Put those boundaries in the role description and explain them to colleagues.

## Debrief · Make time and recognition real

The weekly plan is 24 + 8 + 8 = 40 paid hours: 60% complex delivery, 20% mentoring and 20% shared methods and reviews. Reallocating existing commitments makes that work feasible. Counting every hour as personal project delivery and then adding mentoring would contradict the funded design.

The confirmed RMB24,000–28,000 band and access to development and review forums make B more than a symbolic consolation. The shared band does not mean every person receives the same salary or that different jobs are identical. Explain assessment and pay decisions consistently within the supplied framework; do not promise automatic progression.

## Debrief · Assess contribution without making a checklist game

Use examples of complex work handled well, methods adopted appropriately and colleagues becoming more capable. Consider project context and fair access to opportunities. A count of mentoring meetings or documents alone can reward visible activity without useful contribution. Peer feedback adds perspective but should not become a popularity vote.

Publish the role’s scope, evidence expectations, assessment process and review route before inviting applications. Allow people to discuss career interests without treating reluctance to supervise as lack of ambition. A technically strong employee still needs to demonstrate the specialist role’s wider contribution; neither route is owed solely because of tenure.

## Debrief · Keep the two paths workable

Review whether specialist advice is useful, project delivery remains covered and staff still know who makes management decisions. Check whether mentoring time is actually protected and whether access to development matches the announced grade. A new title, chart or salary band alone does not prove the pathway works.

Keep future movement between paths possible through interest, preparation, vacancies and fair assessment rather than implying it is automatic. The studio is choosing one funded role now, not guaranteeing unlimited specialist posts or permanent reporting arrangements. No retention increase, productivity gain or financial return has been measured in this exercise.

## Instructor note

Minutes 0–4: distinguish the two jobs and identify missing evidence. Minutes 4–8: use the workload and funding reveal to choose a structure. Minutes 8–13: write one specialist contribution criterion and one boundary with supervision. Minutes 13–18: test whether the workload, recognition and assessment make the path credible.

PPL-03 selects a person for an existing supervisor vacancy. Here no employee is chosen: the question is whether career progression should require another management post when the unmet need is technical. Do not turn either technical expertise or management ambition into a stereotype about who deserves advancement.

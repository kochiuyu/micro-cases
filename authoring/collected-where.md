# Compostable here. Collected where?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 130 -->

SUS-11 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · End-of-life systems · Compostability · Collection compatibility

![A caterer and two event colleagues compare moulded-fibre and aluminium trays beside collection containers in a community hall.](../collected-where-scene.jpg)

## In the news

In a Legislative Council reply on 8 May 2024, Hong Kong’s Government described efforts to reduce disposable tableware, including reusable-container programmes.

That historical context does not rank every replacement material. The event, products, prices and collection arrangements below are fictional.

## Opening decision

**Fictional classroom situation.** A Hong Kong caterer needs 500 trays for an indoor community event next week. Reducing unnecessary servings and borrowing or renting reusable stock have been checked; enough suitable stock and return capacity cannot be arranged for this booking. This is a constraint for this event, not an argument against reuse generally.

Both shortlisted trays have been checked for the intended food, safe handling and applicable requirements. A is an uncoated, non-plastic moulded-fibre tray with a valid industrial-compostability certificate covering the whole item. B is an uncoated aluminium tray accepted by the event’s existing commercial metal collector if emptied, rinsed and kept separate. Neither has a lid or attached insert.

The organiser has HK$4,000 for this complete tray-and-handling package. Both quotations fit. A costs less; B includes the extra handling needed for its confirmed collection route. The organiser wants useful recovery where practicable, but has made no zero-waste or carbon claim. No order has been placed. A’s certificate is verified, but the event’s destination for it has not yet been checked.

## Round two

Before ordering, the contracted food-waste collector confirms that its receiving facility does not accept A’s trays, even though the industrial-compostability certificate is valid. A is also excluded from the event’s paper and metal collections. No suitable composting destination or separate transport has been secured for this booking. This is a fact about this fictional event’s arrangements, not a claim that no such service exists anywhere in Hong Kong.

A can still be used lawfully and safely, but its quoted after-use route is ordinary residual-waste collection, not composting. B’s collector has confirmed the exact tray and the receiving metal recycler in writing. The HK$4,000 package covers an attendant, safe emptying and rinsing, separate storage, collection and downstream acceptance of trays meeting the agreed condition. Food scraps follow the existing accepted food-waste route; badly contaminated trays go to residual waste.

A is HK$3,000 all-in; B is HK$4,000 all-in. These totals are unchanged and include the relevant handling and lawful residual disposal. No recycling rebate, recovery percentage or comparative life-cycle assessment is supplied. The event has not happened: a booked route is not completed recovery.

**Student question:** Would you spend the additional HK$1,000 on B’s collection package or retain A with residual disposal? State the route honestly and name one record you would require after the event.

## A property is not a booked destination

The certificate concerns how the item can behave under specified industrial treatment conditions. It does not oblige this collector or facility to accept it. A valid certificate and an unavailable route can coexist; neither implies a dishonest supplier.

## Follow the item through the whole chain

Check the exact product, sorting instructions, collector acceptance, receiving facility and treatment conditions. A bin labelled compostable does not create that chain. Do not put excluded trays into food-waste or recycling collections merely to make a recovery total look better.

## Pay for a route, not a green winner

B costs HK$1,000 more for 500 trays, equivalent to HK$2 per tray in the complete package comparison. That is not the cost per tray actually recycled: the recovered number is unknown. B is a defensible choice if the organiser values a funded, accepted recovery route enough to pay the difference.

A remains an affordable service option with honest residual disposal if the organiser decides not to buy that route. It cannot be described as composted at this event. The supplied facts do not prove B has lower total carbon, water use or environmental impact; manufacturing, cleaning, transport and actual treatment would need comparable assessment.

## Verify the outcome after collection

Assign someone to check sorting and record issued trays, collected quantities, rejected material and the receiver’s acceptance or treatment record. Keep collection separate from completed processing. Do not convert a count into a weight without measurements or claim all 500 were recovered because 500 were purchased.

## Improve the next booking

Arrange reuse and returns earlier where feasible. If considering A again, obtain a suitable facility’s exact-product acceptance and a complete collection quote before promising composting. The case supplies no free alternative route and no evidence that paying an unspecified premium would secure one.

## Instructor note

Allow 3 minutes for the first choice, 3 for the reveal, 4 to map the treatment chain and justify spending, and 3 for an honest claim and follow-up record. Ask students to distinguish the item’s certified property from this event’s booked destination.

Both packages remain defensible: A saves HK$1,000 with residual disposal; B funds an accepted collection route. The HK$2 premium is per purchased tray, not per recovered tray. No recovery rate or comparative life-cycle impact is supplied.

Keep the fictional commercial contract separate from public collection guidance. Do not invent a free composting route, assume all Hong Kong facilities reject the item, or equate collection with completed treatment. End with one practical acceptance or outcome record to check.

# Most respondents liked it. Most customers?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 128 -->

IS-11 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Survey evidence · Nonresponse · Decision uncertainty

![Three parcel-service colleagues discuss questionnaire responses at a counter beside shelves of parcels and a telephone.](../respondents-or-customers-scene.jpg)

## Hong Kong survey context

Hong Kong’s Land Registry commissioned a customer-satisfaction survey during March–June 2025 using telephone interviews, field interviews and self-completed questionnaires. It described customer feedback as a way to identify service improvements.

Different ways of reaching customers matter when using survey evidence. This is not an allegation of bias in that survey. The parcel service and all numbers below are fictional.

## Opening decision

**Fictional classroom situation.** A Hong Kong parcel-collection service is considering an optional grouped collection notice for customers receiving several parcels. Existing individual notices and collection rights remain available. The optional feature has passed technical, security and accessibility checks; it makes no payment or safety decisions.

The manager wants to understand preference among the fixed list of 1,000 adult customers active in the last three months. Each received the same neutral description and one voluntary invitation through an authorised contact channel. Two hundred submitted one complete response each; 160 favour the option. No duplicates or unknown eligibility remain. These are stated preferences, not observed use or measured time savings.

Choose A: offer the option now, with clear opt-in and funded support; or B: spend an additional HK$6,000 and delay the launch decision two weeks for targeted follow-up. The launch itself is funded under either choice. Existing service continues during a delay. Both are affordable, but follow-up costs real money and postpones possible benefits.

B funds a trained researcher, neutral phone or paper responses where appropriate, and attempts to contact a randomly selected set of initial nonrespondents from each customer group. It records selection and contact outcomes; participation remains voluntary. It cannot guarantee replies or perfect representation. Neither option is a vote that obliges every customer to use the feature.

## Evidence

| Initial voluntary survey | Customers |
|---|---:|
| Eligible invited | 1,000 |
| Complete responses | 200 |
| Favour among respondents | 160 |
| Do not favour among respondents | 40 |

160 ÷ 200 = 80% of respondents. The 800 nonrespondents’ views are unknown. The initial summary has not shown participation by group.

**Student question:** Would you launch the optional feature now or spend HK$6,000 on follow-up first? Explain what the 80% establishes and what uncertainty your choice accepts.

## Round two

The analyst links the replies to the existing customer list. Frequent customers used the service at least monthly during the defined three-month period; occasional customers used it less often. The two groups are mutually exclusive and include all 1,000 customers.

| Customer group | Invited | Replied | Favour |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Frequent | 200 | 160 | 144 |
| Occasional | 800 | 40 | 16 |
| Total | 1,000 | 200 | 160 |

Frequent customers make up most replies, although occasional customers make up most of the list. No additional views have been collected. The missing customers might be more favourable, less favourable or similar to those who replied.

Follow-up can now deliberately reach initial nonrespondents in both groups rather than simply ask the same respondents again. The proposed service, voluntary participation, budgets and two-week delay remain unchanged.

**Student question:** Keep or change your choice? Use the group table to explain the risk in saying “most customers favour it”, then name what the follow-up should report if some selected customers still do not answer.

## Change the denominator, change the claim

Respondents favouring the option are 160 of 200: 80%. Participation is 200 of 1,000: 20%. These answer different questions. The first describes observed preferences; neither establishes the preference of all 1,000 customers.

Frequent participation is 160 ÷ 200 = 80%; occasional participation is 40 ÷ 800 = 5%. Frequent customers are 20% of the list but 80% of replies. Their observed preference is 144 ÷ 160 = 90%, versus 16 ÷ 40 = 40% among occasional respondents.

## A warning, not a verdict about missing people

The responding mix differs sharply from the customer mix, and respondent preferences differ between groups. That makes the unqualified headline risky. It does not reveal what nonrespondents think or prove the exact size or direction of bias.

With no assumptions about the 800 missing views, total support could range from 160 to 960 customers: 16% to 96% of the list. These are extreme logical bounds, not forecasts or a confidence interval.

## Reweighting is not a magic repair

Weighting the observed group preferences to the list mix would give 20% × 90% + 80% × 40% = 50%. That calculation assumes respondents represent nonrespondents within each group, which has not been established.

Do not replace an unsupported 80% customer claim with an equally certain 50% claim. Follow-up should investigate the missing views, not merely rearrange existing replies.

## Make either decision honestly

B is stronger if uncertainty about the broader customer base matters enough to justify HK$6,000 and two weeks. Use the same clear question, track who was selected and reached, and explain remaining nonresponse and possible mode differences.

A can be defended because the feature is optional, supported and leaves existing service available. But justify it as accepting uncertainty for a limited-risk offer, not as proof that 80% of all customers want it. Subsequent opt-in uptake describes users of the offer; it still does not automatically reveal everyone’s preference.

## Report what is known and what remains missing

Report the original and follow-up groups separately before any combined estimate: eligible list sizes, selection method, invitations, completed replies and observed preferences. Account for different sampling chances if combining them; do not just pool counts.

State which customers remain missing. A larger sample or a higher participation rate alone does not guarantee less bias. Do not pressure people to answer or infer opposition from silence.

## Instructor note

Allow three minutes for the initial claim and launch choice, four for group participation and preference calculations, and five for the decision and reporting limits. Keep the optional weighting paragraph as a deeper debrief if time permits.

This is descriptive representativeness, not IS-01’s causal inference, IS-07’s rollout-site choice or IS-10’s experimental interference. The feature is technically ready; the issue is the population represented by voluntary feedback. No survey can substitute for appropriate operational safeguards.

Neither a missing majority nor a nonresponse pattern proves customers oppose the change. Ask students to separate a defensible business decision under uncertainty from an overstated statistical claim.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

The parcel service, optional grouped notice, fixed three-month list, survey, counts, eligibility, contact permission, budgets and follow-up design are original fiction. All 1,000 adults were invited once; 200 unique complete replies, 160 favourable and 40 not favourable. Frequent group: 200 invited, 160 replies, 144 favourable; occasional: 800 invited, 40 replies, 16 favourable. No missing eligibility or duplicates. Participation and preference percentages have distinct denominators. Logical support bounds 16–96% assume only the supplied observed answers, not an estimated distribution. The 50% reweighted figure is conditional on within-group representativeness, not a verified estimate. Follow-up costs an additional HK$6,000, delays the launch decision two weeks and does not guarantee response; launch is separately funded either way. Optional opt-in preserves existing notices/rights, safety, accessibility and security. Surveys measure preference, not causal benefit. No allegation is made about the two public registries.

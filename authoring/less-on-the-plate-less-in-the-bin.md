# Less on the plate. Less in the bin?

SUS-46 · Hong Kong · 15 minutes · Food-waste prevention · Measurement boundary · Service design

![A canteen supervisor compares two ladles while a colleague weighs a bowl.](../less-on-the-plate-less-in-the-bin-scene.jpg)

## Reducing food waste at source takes more than collecting it

Hong Kong’s Food Wise Hong Kong Campaign promotes food-waste prevention, and the Environmental Protection Department’s food-service guidance suggests offering different portion sizes. Hong Kong separately publishes figures on disposal of food waste. The canteen, measurements and pilot rules below are fictional.

## Opening decision

Ivy manages a fictional Hong Kong workplace canteen. On two comparable lunch services, the kitchen tests Standard and Small Default for the same dish. Diners can request a standard portion instead of the small default and can ask for a free refill under either design. Ingredient quality, service price and access are held fixed for this exercise.

Weigh the edible food served that was left uneaten, including uneaten refills. The team records first-portion leftovers and refill leftovers separately with no double counting. Exclude kitchen trimmings, unserved surplus, liquids and any inedible parts. Divide by diners served, not requests for refills. Waste sent for processing is still waste for this measure.

Roll out Small Default only if it reduces edible plate waste per diner by at least 10% relative to Standard and at least 85% of its diners say their lunch was sufficient. All diners answer that question. Treat the pilot comparisons as the supplied planning estimates, not proof that the portion policy caused every difference.

## Evidence

| Fictional lunch pilot | Standard | Small Default |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diners served | 200 | 160 |
| Edible first-portion leftovers | 24 kg | 10 kg |
| Edible refill leftovers | 2 kg | 4 kg |
| Diners saying lunch was sufficient | 90% | 88% |

**Student question:** Calculate edible plate waste per diner under each design. Does Small Default qualify for rollout, and why would looking only at the first-portion bins give a different answer about its size of effect?

## Round two

Before rollout, the team finds that one bin containing 8 kg of edible Small Default refill leftovers was collected separately. It was correctly weighed but omitted from the refill total. Add it to the reported 4 kg. There are no changes to diner counts, first-portion waste, the sufficiency survey or Standard.

**Student question:** Does Small Default still qualify? Given Small Default’s diner count, how much edible refill waste in total could it produce and still just meet the waste requirement?

## Debrief · Weigh what diners did not eat

Standard has (24 + 2) ÷ 200 = 0.13 kg or 130 g per diner. Small Default initially has (10 + 4) ÷ 160 = 87.5 g, about 32.7% less. At 88% sufficient, it clears both gates. First portions alone give 120 and 62.5 g per diner, suggesting too large a reduction.

## Debrief · Include the missing refill bin

Small Default actually has 10 + 4 + 8 = 22 kg of edible plate waste, or 137.5 g per diner. That exceeds 130 g rather than reducing it; do not roll out under the given rule. The sufficiency measure still passes, but it cannot compensate for failing the waste gate.

## Debrief · Translate the target into the right units

The waste ceiling is 0.9 × 130 = 117 g per diner. At 160 diners that is 18.72 kg total, leaving 18.72 − 10 = 8.72 kg of refill waste at most. Recheck where the bins go, use matched repeated lunches and measure sufficiency alongside complete bin weights.

## Instructor note

Use 2 minutes to read, 4 to compare grams per diner, 4 to audit the refill bin and 5 to refine the next pilot. Different dishes and crowd mixes can change waste. No inference about nutritional needs, reducing access to food or any individual diner is intended.

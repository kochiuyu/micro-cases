# A useful industry meeting. Which topics are off limits?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 213 -->

GVR-18 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Competition compliance · Meeting governance · Information boundaries

![A meeting chair and two shop owners discuss a plain agenda beside stacked cardboard.](../keep-the-meeting-in-bounds-scene.jpg)

## Collaboration is not a blanket exemption

On 1 March 2024, Singapore’s competition authority issued guidance on business collaborations pursuing environmental sustainability. It recognised that collaboration can be useful while warning against using environmental goals to conceal anti-competitive conduct. That is Singapore guidance, not legal clearance in Hong Kong. Our fictional Hong Kong trade group uses the explicit conduct rules below, informed by local official guidance.

## Opening decision

Six competing Hong Kong stationery-shop owners are considering a voluntary clean-cardboard collection arrangement. An independent chair must set a sixty-minute planning meeting. No collection contract, joint purchase or commercial agreement will be signed at the meeting. Each shop’s existing compliant disposal remains funded and available. Any eventual arrangement needs separate appropriate review before implementation.

A allocates forty-five minutes to collection-site access, safe storage and handoff questions, then fifteen minutes to published handling guidance. B allocates thirty minutes to those logistics, fifteen to handling guidance and fifteen to an already-screened briefing on published territory-wide recycling trends. B offers wider context but less time to resolve site questions. The briefing contains no identifiable shop figures or individual commercial plans; there is no open “market outlook” round. Both agendas fit the same funded meeting.

The group’s fictional conduct policy prohibits exchanging or coordinating selling prices, future pricing intentions, customer allocation, bids, output plans or sensitive business costs. No participant supplies private customer lists or company-level sales data. The chair circulates the exact agenda and permitted materials, states the limits and keeps attendance and minutes. Questions outside the reviewed scope are held for separate compliance assessment, not improvised in the room. Public or historical labels alone do not guarantee that an exchange is harmless.

## Round two

Before the meeting, one owner asks the chair to add a round on next month’s selling prices and which customers each shop should serve. No prices, customer identities or allocation proposals have been circulated, and no one has agreed to coordinate them. The chair rejects the suggested topic immediately, records the request and refers it to the designated compliance adviser. It is not an item students may choose to retain.

The adviser confirms that neither commercial discussion belongs in the meeting and requires the planned controls to remain. The organiser now has two funded scheduling options. R runs the narrowly scoped A agenda today with the independent chair, a second trained minute-taker and no unscreened contributions. D postpones the meeting one week so an experienced competition-compliance moderator can attend the same A agenda. D delays decisions about access and storage; existing disposal continues safely, with no contractual deadline or customer promise breached. R preserves momentum but places more reliance on the chair’s intervention. Neither option supplies blanket legal approval for a future recycling arrangement.

Under both options, participants are reminded not to share sensitive information in the meeting, breaks or follow-up chat. If such an exchange nevertheless arises, the stated protocol is to object clearly, publicly distance themselves and leave immediately, record what occurred and report it to the Competition Commission, with appropriate legal support. Records are preserved; the group does not delete an incident or quietly resume the commercial discussion. Any later recycling meeting requires a fresh reviewed scope. The classroom exercise itself sends no report or message.

**Student question:** After rejecting the proposed commercial discussion, would you run R today or postpone for D? Explain your governance choice, the stopping protocol and how legitimate recycling work can proceed without coordinating competitors’ commercial decisions.

## Purpose does not erase boundaries

Shared handling knowledge and practical collection planning serve a legitimate operational purpose, but that purpose does not make every piece of information appropriate. A future selling-price round or allocation of customers is different from discussing where a collection vehicle can stop. Do not redesign the prohibited proposal into coded hints or an informal side conversation.

## Control the process before and during the meeting

Both initial agendas total sixty minutes: A gives more time to site questions; B adds screened context. Neither includes a free exchange of commercial plans. The proposed late addition tests whether those limits are real. Rejecting it is mandatory within the exercise; the managerial choice is how much facilitation support and delay to accept for the remaining work.

## Two defensible governance routes

R can be defended if the chair and minute-taker can enforce the narrow scope and stop immediately when necessary. D can be defended if stronger moderation is worth a week’s delay. Existing disposal means D is not an unsafe abandonment of waste handling. A moderator, minutes or a sustainability label is not legal immunity. Review the design of any actual arrangement separately, and retain independent commercial decision-making.

## Instructor note

Do not ask students to agree prices or allocate customers, even hypothetically. Assess agenda design, clear objection, record preservation and separation of legitimate logistics from commercial coordination. STR-06 weighs shared facilities against differentiation; this case governs a meeting’s information boundaries. Hong Kong guidance informs the protocol, while Singapore material supplies comparative context only. This is a classroom scenario, not legal advice or authorisation to contact participants or a regulator.

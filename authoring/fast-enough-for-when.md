# Fast dispatch. Here by which day?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 571 -->

MKT-48 · Hong Kong · 15 minutes · Delivery claims · Arrival expectations · Promise calibration

![A stockroom worker studies two blank delivery cards beside ordinary parcels.](../fast-enough-for-when-scene.jpg)

## Dispatch and delivery are different events

A Hong Kong Consumer Council trial of food-ordering services compared displayed delivery estimates with actual arrivals, including both single estimates and ranges. The fictional household-goods shop below measures what buyers think a message means, then checks that interpretation against recorded arrival days.

## Opening decision

A Hong Kong shop sells the same desk lamp from the same stock with the same carrier and price. In a fictional design exercise for Tuesday morning orders, card S says Dispatches by Wednesday. Card A says Estimated arrival by Thursday. Neither card guarantees an arrival date or changes customer rights. Both include the same link to ordinary detailed delivery terms in the fictional mockup.

A recent matched cohort of 100 comparable Tuesday orders was all dispatched by Wednesday. Count actual arrivals by the end of each stated day. The manager will not publish a date estimate unless at least 90% of comparable recent shipments reached customers by that date. The 90% threshold is this shop’s classroom rule, not a legal standard.

The pilot compares customer interpretation, not purchasing intention or conversion. It uses fixed synthetic records, no live checkout and no individual addresses.

## Evidence

| Arrival of the 100 recent shipments | Count |
| --- | --- |
| By Wednesday | 65 |
| On Thursday | 30 |
| After Thursday | 5 |

**Student question:** Which card would you test first? Compute how many orders arrive by Thursday, and explain why dispatching by Wednesday does not establish a Wednesday arrival.

## Round two

Two independent sets of 40 mock shoppers saw one card each. On S, 24 understood it to mean the lamp would arrive by Wednesday. On A, 35 understood that Thursday was an arrival estimate, not a guarantee. These are different response categories, not an across-card percent improvement in comprehension.

The shop also checked 40 comparable orders placed after Tuesday’s cutoff: 28 reached buyers by Thursday, 11 arrived Friday and one later. Staff can show different estimates based on the checked order cutoff. No warehouse, carrier or return policy changes are needed for this display decision.

**Student question:** What would you display before and after the cutoff? Compute each supported arrival rate and explain how you would verify shoppers’ interpretation before launching the revised wording.

## Debrief · A fast intermediate step can mislead

All 100 early orders were dispatched by Wednesday, but 65% actually arrived by then. By Thursday 65 + 30 = 95 arrived, or 95%, which clears the shop’s 90% rule. Test Card A first because it clears that threshold and names arrival directly. S states a dispatch fact, but 24/40 (60%) of its mock viewers inferred Wednesday arrival. Do not imply customers can rely on that day.

## Debrief · Test the date on the right population

After cutoff, Thursday coverage is 28/40 = 70%. Friday coverage is (28 + 11)/40 = 97.5%. Use Thursday before and Friday after the cutoff on these synthetic records, clearly marked as estimates. An aggregate historical result does not guarantee the next shipment. Pause the date if the supporting cohort or fulfillment calendar changes.

## Debrief · Ask comparable questions next time

Test both versions with the same question about the expected arrival and whether it is certain. The 35/40 figure for A is 87.5% for its own measured interpretation only. Keep dispatch as secondary status, not the lead promise. Check both display comprehension and actual missed estimates; neither alone tells you whether people made good decisions.

## Instructor note

Use the first exhibit to separate when the parcel leaves from when it arrives. Ask whether a broad estimate can be accurate but unhelpful, and require students to design a single common comprehension question before comparing variants.

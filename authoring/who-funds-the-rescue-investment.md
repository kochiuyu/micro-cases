# Who funds the rescue investment?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 493 -->

FIN-48 · Hong Kong · 15 minutes · Debt overhang · Cash-flow priority · Corporate restructuring · Participation constraints

![A finance manager and a lender review a repair proposal beside a cold-storage door and a rack of produce crates.](../who-funds-the-rescue-investment-scene.jpg)

## Hong Kong banks add options for SME financing

In October 2024, the Hong Kong Monetary Authority and the banking sector announced measures to help smaller businesses upgrade, including financing for low-carbon equipment and more flexible repayment options. The announcement did not promise bank co-funding. The negotiation below is fictional.

## Opening decision

A cold-storage operator in Kwai Chung is losing orders when its old refrigeration controls fail. A retrofit could reduce spoilage and win back customers. Finance manager Ada asks the owners to put in HK$22 million today. The firm has no spare cash and already owes one bank HK$100 million at year-end.

At year-end the bank gets the lower of cash available and the amount due. The owners receive the remainder. Assume both sides use expected cash, with no discounting, taxes, transaction costs or claims other than the bank loan. The owners’ contribution pays the installer today; it does not add directly to year-end cash.

## Evidence

| Order demand | Chance | Cash without retrofit | Cash with retrofit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weak | 25% | 30 | 42 |
| Steady | 50% | 70 | 104 |
| Strong | 25% | 130 | 156 |

**Student question:** Who gets the cash in each demand state? How much value does the retrofit create, and would the owners fund it on the old terms?

## Round two

The bank offers to pay a portion of the HK$22 million installation bill if the owners pay the rest. Its contribution does not create a new claim or change the old loan. If either side walks away, the retrofit does not go ahead.

Before signing, a customer cuts its order forecast. Stress-test the proposal by changing only the steady-demand cash with the retrofit from HK$104 million to HK$94 million. Leave the other outcomes and the no-retrofit forecast unchanged.

**Student question:** How much can the bank contribute while making both sides better off? Give a range and one offer. Does any offer still work under the revised forecast?

## Debrief · Split cash before averaging

Without the retrofit, the bank collects 30, 70 or 100 and the owners collect 0, 0 or 30. With the retrofit, the bank collects 42, 100 or 100 and the owners collect 0, 4 or 56. All amounts in the debrief are in HK$ million.

## Debrief · A good project the owners reject

Expected year-end cash rises from 75 to 101.5. The retrofit creates 101.5 − 75 − 22 = 4.5. The bank’s expected recovery rises from 67.5 to 85.5, while the owners’ expected receipts rise from 7.5 to 16. If the owners pay all 22, they gain only 8.5 in receipts and lose 13.5 net.

## Debrief · Divide the installation bill

If the bank pays x, its net gain is 18 − x. The owners pay 22 − x, so their gain is x − 13.5. Both gain when 13.5 < x < 18. Each endpoint leaves one party indifferent. At x = 15, the bank gains 3 and the owners gain 1.5.

## Debrief · When no split can work

At 94 in the steady state, the bank collects 94 and the owners collect zero in that state. The bank’s expected receipts fall to 82.5 and the owners’ to 14. Against no retrofit, the bank will pay at most 15, while the owners need the bank to pay at least 15.5. Expected incremental cash drops to 21.5 against a cost of 22.

## Takeaway · Can the deal change the decision?

Debt overhang blocks the original deal because the owners bear the cost but the old lender captures most of the gain. Co-funding can share the gain when it exists. Under the weaker forecast the project itself destroys value, and splitting the same costs cannot fix that.

## Instructor note

Allow 15 minutes: 2 to frame the decision, 5 for the cash split, 5 to negotiate a contribution, and 3 to recheck the offer after the forecast changes.

Pair lenders and owners. Each must compare the offer with walking away, not with a deal the other side would refuse. Ask what happens if shared legal or monitoring costs exceed the initial project surplus.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

All companies, people, contract terms and amounts in the exercise are fictional. The news context refers to historical public announcements, not the fictional bank’s terms. This exercise is not legal or financing advice.

- [HKMA, support for SME development, upgrading and transformation (2024)](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202410/18/P2024101800179p.htm). Announces financing for low-carbon equipment and flexible repayment options; it does not establish the fictional cost-sharing deal.
- [HKMA and HKAB, joint Taskforce on SME Lending (2024)](https://www.hkma.gov.hk/eng/news-and-media/press-releases/2024/08/20240823-3/). Official remarks describe prior relief and the taskforce’s approach to individual cases while maintaining ordinary risk standards.
- [Stewart Myers, Determinants of Corporate Borrowing (MIT working paper, 1976)](https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/5e242704-fb08-47d5-b47d-79bf02da4af6). Original model links risky debt to underinvestment when old creditors receive part of the payoff from a new investment.
- [Filippo Occhino and Andrea Pescatori, Debt Overhang in a Business Cycle Model (2010)](https://www.clevelandfed.org/publications/working-paper/2010/wp-1003r-debt-overhang-in-a-business-cycle-model). Shows how a creditor’s recovery in default can reduce the firm’s investment incentive in a model with non-contractible investment.
- [Thomas Philippon, Debt Overhang and Recapitalization in Closed and Open Economies (2010)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~tphilipp/papers/PhilipponIMF.pdf). Extends the financing problem to banks and households, illustrating why the distribution of gains can matter for support.

# Three nearby outlets. Three different cities?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 174 -->

STR-15 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Strategy · Geographic scope · Operating density

![Two alteration-chain colleagues discuss plain project folders beside sewing equipment and garments in a workshop.](../nearby-or-three-cities-scene.jpg)

## In the news

DFI Retail Group’s 23 July 2025 half-year presentation reported 119 additional South China convenience stores during the first half. A larger network is a real strategic choice, but that announcement does not establish the best geography or support model for a small service chain.

The service chain, cities, support schedules and budgets below are fictional. No DFI operating practice or result is being simulated.

## Opening decision

A fictional Hong Kong-founded clothing-alteration chain runs two company-owned outlets in one city. It is planning three more, also company-owned, with the same service menu and quality promises. A places all three in nearby neighbourhoods. B places one in each of three other cities. All proposed sites have passed the relevant preliminary premises and lawful-operation checks; final opening readiness must still be confirmed.

The owner has HK$1.8 million ring-fenced for fit-out, setup and the complete first six months of incremental operation, including support. Either route has access to suitable paid operating staff. Existing outlets have protected staffing and budgets. No new leases or customer promises have been made. This is a choice between two whole expansion packages, not a free combination of both.

A would build presence in a market the chain already understands; the new neighbourhoods are not identical to its existing catchments. B would reach three genuinely new local markets, where initial enquiries suggest interest but no sales are committed. The owner values both a dependable service network and wider market learning. There is no comparable revenue or profit forecast, nor evidence that wider geography automatically reduces business risk.

## Round two

The support map is now complete. During the first twelve operating weeks, each new outlet needs six hours of scheduled on-site coaching per week. This is an assessed development allowance, not ordinary shift staffing or a substitute for technical competence. The central trainer has 30 funded hours a week available for the expansion, after protected duties elsewhere.

For A, the three weekly visits need 18 coaching hours plus six total travel hours: 24 hours. The nearby network also has a paid, qualified relief arrangement for short absences without removing required cover from existing outlets. The full six-month package costs HK$1.5 million, including this support, ordinary staffing and safe working time. HK$300,000 remains uncommitted within the envelope; it is not profit.

Trying to operate B with those same three central visits would require 18 coaching hours plus 24 travel hours: 42 hours, beyond the trainer’s 30-hour allocation. But B is not limited to that unsuitable model. Three experienced local managers have accepted conditional offers, one in each city. A costed local-manager plan provides the six weekly coaching hours at each outlet within paid roles, qualified local absence cover and scheduled central oversight. The central oversight and travel together fit the existing 30-hour allowance. All this is included in B’s HK$1.8 million six-month package, not an extra unfunded hire.

The managers’ experience has been checked, but they have not yet completed the chain’s service-method training. B must pass a funded pre-opening assessment of technical standards, escalation and reporting; dates can move without breaching any customer promise. A must pass the same service-readiness standard. Both packages preserve rest, safe workloads and legal obligations. Neither has proven customer demand, service outcomes or a return on investment. B’s new-market opportunity remains, but it requires a more locally managed network.

**Student question:** Keep or change your geography choice? Compare the support systems, not just outlet count or travel distance, and specify a readiness condition that must be met before opening under your preferred route.

## Density changes what can be shared

For A, 18 + 6 = 24 central-trainer hours fits within 30, leaving six hours of that allocation unused. Shared coaching and a qualified relief arrangement are concrete benefits of proximity in this case. They do not prove extra customer demand or eliminate overlap with existing outlets. The HK$300,000 budget difference is retained capacity to spend, not an earned return.

## Reach needs a different management design

B’s central-only imitation would require 42 hours, exceeding the allowance by 12. That rejects a support design, not necessarily the geography. Its funded local managers supply on-site coaching, while central oversight fits the available allocation. Wider reach is credible only with the capabilities and reporting to sustain the promised service.

## Make the expansion conditional on readiness

A is defensible for consolidating the existing market with a more shared support structure and lower known six-month cost. B is defensible for learning in new markets through a deliberately decentralised operating network, provided the local teams pass readiness checks. A manager’s prior experience does not automatically establish competence in this chain’s methods.

Before opening, require observed service demonstrations, an absence-cover roster, a clear escalation route and usable reports. If readiness fails, delay or revise the chosen package rather than cover the gap with unpaid work. Monitor customer demand and service consistency after launch. No data here identifies which route earns more, how much sales overlap occurs or whether dispersed markets have less correlated risk.

## Instructor note

Have students name the strategic purpose before showing the travel map. Then separate the impossible central-only B model from the feasible, funded local-manager B proposal. Do not reward an automatic switch to nearby outlets just because 42 exceeds 30. The stronger answer connects geographic scope with the operating capabilities it requires.

STR-07 compares ownership and franchising; all outlets here remain company-owned. STR-10 examines dependence on a customer. This case focuses geographic density and managerial reach, not a diversification calculation or a universal preference for local expansion. Six-month feasibility is not a full investment valuation; subsequent funding and results need future review.

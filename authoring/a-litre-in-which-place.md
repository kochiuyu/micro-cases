# A litre saved. In which place?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 154 -->

SUS-13 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Water stewardship · Catchment context · Resource allocation

![Two hospitality facilities colleagues discuss a tap fitting beside a demonstration sink and plain project folders.](../a-litre-in-which-place-scene.jpg)

## Industry context

On 1 June 2025, a Hong Kong Government feature described the Water-smart Taskforce Programme and hotel participation in monitoring water-use patterns. It presented measurement and tailored follow-up as tools for finding savings.

The feature does not establish savings or water-stress rankings for the properties below. Both properties, their supply systems and every project figure are fictional. No real Hong Kong or Asian location is being labelled water-stressed.

## Opening decision

A Hong Kong-based hospitality group has two fictional properties in different, unnamed Asian supply areas: Harbour House and Valley House. It has HK$60,000 for one complete, indivisible water-efficiency package this year. Each package costs exactly HK$60,000, including qualified installation, first-year maintenance and monitoring. Both can be installed before the next dry season. Neither can be split or partly installed; funding for both is unavailable in this round.

Both packages use assessed water-efficient fittings while maintaining the same safe, lawful service, hygiene and guest comfort. Existing equipment is serviceable, mandatory repairs and safeguards are already funded, and no current emergency or rationing obligation requires one project. The decision is which additional improvement to fund first, not whether to withhold essential water.

At the stipulated occupancy and service levels, the first complete operating year is forecast to use 900 cubic metres less supplied freshwater at Harbour House, or 600 cubic metres less at Valley House. One cubic metre is 1,000 litres. These are estimated reductions at each property’s freshwater inlet meter against its own no-upgrade baseline. They are not measured results, basin-wide savings or net water consumption after discharges. No rebound, outsourcing or service reduction is assumed in these estimates. The manager has not set a single priority between total volume saved and responding to local water pressures.

**Student question:** Which project would you fund first, Harbour House or Valley House? Compare the forecast annual freshwater reductions at the same project cost, and identify the local information you would want before treating the larger total as the stronger stewardship choice.

## Round two

A fictional local supply review adds information about where and when the reductions occur. Harbour House draws from a network with several usable supply sources and adequate planned dry-season reserve under the review’s operating assumptions. Its package would reduce demand by 100 m³ during the coming four-month dry season and 800 m³ during the rest of the year.

Valley House draws from a different system dependent on one seasonal reservoir shared with households and other businesses. The operator’s review identifies a much narrower dry-season supply margin and asks commercial users to reduce avoidable demand voluntarily before that period. There is no current restriction or emergency. Its package would reduce demand by 400 m³ during the same four-month period and 200 m³ during the rest of the year. Local user discussions confirm that reducing demand during those months is a shared priority.

These fictional local findings are not scores from a global map. They support a comparison of context, not a precise probability of an outage. Both projects retain the same costs, service and annual estimates. Neither is shown to prevent a specific shortage, deliver extra water to a household or reduce source abstraction litre for litre. Discharge and ecosystem effects, utility bill savings and full-lifetime benefits are not quantified. Do not invent a scarcity multiplier, monetary benefit or universal environmental score.

**Student question:** Keep or change your project choice? Compare annual and dry-season reductions, state the objective your recommendation prioritises, and explain one benefit the evidence does not allow you to claim.

## First compare like quantities

Harbour House saves an estimated 900,000 litres per year; Valley House saves 600,000. At the same HK$60,000 project cost, Harbour offers 300,000 more litres of annual inlet-water reduction, or 50% more than Valley. If maximising this first-year volume is the stated objective, Harbour remains the stronger of the two proposals. No tariff or lifetime is given, so this is not a financial payback calculation.

## Then put the litres in context

The dry-season forecasts are 100,000 litres at Harbour and 400,000 at Valley. Valley’s reduction is four times as large during the identified pressure period, in a supply system with less reserve and a shared local reduction priority. Its annual total is still lower. A litre is the same physical volume in both places; the pressure it may help address is not necessarily the same.

## Choose a priority without inventing a score

Choose Harbour if the round is explicitly governed by the largest forecast first-year inlet-volume reduction, while acknowledging Valley’s local priority. Choose Valley if responding to the documented seasonal pressure is more important than the additional 300,000 annual litres available at Harbour. Explain that trade-off openly. Neither decision can be derived from an unspecified water price, a universal risk multiplier or a claim that the whole shortage will disappear.

## Follow the whole water pathway

Track inlet-meter reductions against an occupancy- and service-adjusted baseline, and keep the seasonal records separate. Also understand where water is sourced and discharged, consult the operator and local users, and check whether the supply context changes. Lower metered withdrawal is not automatically the same as lower net consumption, lower catchment abstraction or quantified ecosystem recovery. Robust supply at Harbour does not make conservation there worthless.

## Instructor note

Take an initial vote using annual volumes, then reveal the local supply and seasonal evidence. Ask students to name the objective before defending a final choice. Do not award marks merely for switching to Valley: a transparent annual-volume objective still favours Harbour. Distinguish a physical measurement, a local priority and a predicted outcome. The exercise is not a mandatory compliance choice, a water-rights dispute or a health trade-off. Global risk tools can guide screening but do not replace local source, season and stakeholder evidence. No actual site rating, outage probability or monetised social benefit is provided. Unlike the electricity-timing case, there is no common emissions factor or additive impact score; the task is place-based stewardship allocation under competing legitimate objectives.

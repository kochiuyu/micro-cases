# More demand today. More supply when?

## Capacity takes time to respond

Demand can rise faster than productive capacity adjusts. Economic teaching distinguishes equilibrium with some inputs fixed from equilibrium after entry and investment. The timing depends on the market, not a universal number of months. This fictional bicycle-hire exercise supplies both horizons explicitly; it is not a forecast for an actual island.

## Opening decision

You advise a classroom planning team studying a fictional Hong Kong island’s competitive bicycle-hire market. Many independent operators and adult customers are price takers. The product is one standard bicycle for one full day, with unchanged maintenance, safety equipment and service. No operator coordinates prices or withholds available bicycles.

The island has exactly 100 serviceable bicycles available daily. Each can support only one full-day hire. All 100 are offered at every price relevant here. No extra fleet, imports, entry or extra operating capacity can arrive during the coming one-week festival. Existing staffing can safely serve those 100 hires.

Let Q be total bicycle-days hired per day and P the complete HK dollar price per bicycle-day. Before the festival, inverse demand is P = 140 − Q. During the festival it is P = 200 − Q. These invented schedules describe willingness to pay, not prices chosen jointly by operators.

Use exact competitive market clearing: quantity demanded equals the 100 offered hires, and the common price adjusts. There is no price ceiling, tax change, queue allocation or change in quality. The relevant demand range is 100–150 hires daily. Costs and individual operators’ market shares are not supplied.

A briefing writer proposes: “Festival demand permanently lifts the island’s daily hire price.” Another says: “More demand automatically creates more bicycles this week.” Calculate the old and festival equilibria before accepting either claim. The immediate festival is known to last one week; nothing yet establishes demand after it ends.

## Round two

Now evaluate a separate hypothetical scenario: instead of ending after the festival, the higher demand schedule P = 200 − Q persists indefinitely. This persistence is stipulated for the exercise, not inferred from festival bookings. The initial fleet and immediate equilibrium remain unchanged.

New operators and existing firms can add fully serviced bicycles after a six-month preparation period. For this scenario, all necessary funding, facilities, staffing and approvals are available through that process. Until adjustment is complete, supply remains 100 daily hires. There is no gradual adjustment path to calculate.

After six months, use the long-run industry supply schedule P = 20 + 0.2Q, valid for 100–150 daily hires. It incorporates entry, fleet adjustment and higher required input costs as this market expands. It is an equilibrium supply schedule, not an individual operator’s cost curve or a fleet delivery order.

Demand remains P = 200 − Q after adjustment. Safety and service stay unchanged; the long-run supply quantity already includes sufficient compliant capacity. Replace the fixed-100 constraint with the supplied long-run schedule. Do not add the two quantities or assume the old fleet disappears.

**Student question:** What price and quantity clear the persistent-demand market after adjustment? Compare all three equilibria and market receipts. Does entry restore the old price, and can the festival receipts establish an individual investment return?

## Debrief · Clear the fixed fleet

Before the festival, Q = 100 gives P = 140 − 100 = HK$40. During the festival, the same fleet clears at P = 200 − 100 = HK$100. Quantity remains 100 daily hires. Daily market receipts rise from HK$4,000 to HK$10,000; the change is HK$6,000.

Higher demand raises price rather than this week’s quantity because the supplied short-run constraint is fixed. Neither headline is justified: additional bicycles cannot arrive this week, and one week of demand does not establish a permanent price. If demand returns to its old schedule with the same fleet, price returns to HK$40.

## Debrief · Change the supply horizon

For persistent demand after adjustment, set 200 − Q = 20 + 0.2Q. Thus 180 = 1.2Q and Q = 150. Substitution gives P = HK$50 on both schedules. Daily market receipts become 150 × HK$50 = HK$7,500. The expanded fleet serves 50 more daily hires than initially.

Relative to the immediate high-demand equilibrium, price falls HK$50 and quantity rises 50. Relative to the original market, price is still HK$10 higher and quantity 50 higher. Daily receipts are HK$2,500 below the immediate peak but HK$3,500 above the original total. These are market totals, not any firm’s sales.

## Debrief · Entry need not restore the old price

The long-run supply schedule passes through the original equilibrium: at Q = 100, P = HK$40. It slopes upward because the exercise stipulates rising required input costs with market expansion. At 150, the required price is HK$50. More responsive supply does not mean an unlimited quantity at the original price.

A horizontal long-run supply curve would be a different assumption, often associated with identical firms and unchanged input prices. It is not the curve supplied here. The six-month date is a fictional adjustment condition, not the definition of “long run” or evidence that real investment can always occur that quickly.

## Debrief · Separate scarcity from investment value

The immediate price premium is consistent with scarcity of fixed capacity. Such opportunities can encourage entry when they are expected to persist. But receipts are not economic profit or a quantified scarcity rent: operating costs, capital opportunity costs and firms’ alternatives have not been supplied. Do not label the HK$6,000 receipts increase pure profit.

Nor can HK$7,500 of later market receipts establish an individual bicycle purchase’s return. We lack its purchase price, financing, maintenance, useful life, resale value, share of hires and risk. A proposal extrapolating HK$100 per hire indefinitely contradicts the supplied persistent-demand equilibrium. No real investment approval follows from this classroom calculation.

## Debrief · A defensible planning statement

Use: “With the fleet fixed, the temporary demand increase raises the clearing price to HK$100. If higher demand persists and the stipulated capacity response occurs, the later equilibrium is 150 daily hires at HK$50.” Keep the temporary festival and persistent-demand scenario separate; neither is a prediction about actual local operators.

The model clears by willingness to pay. It provides no household incomes, accessibility assessment, congestion costs or environmental effects, so it does not establish a fair or socially optimal policy. Maintaining safety is a fixed condition, not an adjustable expense to improve the numerical outcome.

## Instructor note

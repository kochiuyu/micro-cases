# The invoice is sold. Is the risk gone?

FIN-13 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Factoring · Recourse · Liquidity and credit risk

![A stationery wholesaler and accounts colleague compare an invoice with two financing folders in a mezzanine office.](../cash-before-risk-scene.jpg)

## Industry context

On 29 April 2026, the Hong Kong Monetary Authority and banking sector announced additional SME support measures, including trade-finance arrangements and greater use of cargo and trade data in credit assessment.

Access to finance does not itself establish who bears an unpaid invoice. This fictional transaction is not an offer from a named bank or a participant outcome from those measures.

## Opening decision

A fictional Hong Kong stationery wholesaler has delivered an order and issued a HK$100,000 invoice due on day 60. The invoice is currently undisputed. All costs of the delivered goods are already incurred and unchanged by this decision. The wholesaler needs HK$90,000 today for separately approved working capital; its proceeds and costs are identical under either funding route and excluded from the comparison.

A factor offers HK$98,000 cash today in exchange for the full invoice, with no reserve held back. The HK$2,000 difference is the entire non-refundable factoring charge. The headline says invoice purchase and collection service; detailed repayment clauses have not yet been reviewed. No agreement has been signed.

Alternatively, the wholesaler can retain the invoice and draw an already approved HK$90,000 bridge today. The entire bank repayment on day 60 is HK$93,000, including a fixed HK$3,000 charge. The bridge is repayable even if the customer does not pay. Both routes meet today’s need; other funded resources provide sufficient repayment capacity if this customer fails to pay. Those resources are separate from the stated cash flows, carry no different cost between routes and are not a reason to ignore the exposure. Assume no taxes, further fees or relevant time-value differences beyond the supplied charges.

## Round two

The fictional contract makes the factoring fully recourse for customer credit failure. If the customer pays nothing because it cannot pay, the wholesaler must pay the factor HK$100,000 on day 75; the factor then transfers the full unpaid receivable back to the wholesaler. The original HK$2,000 fee is not refunded. For this stress scenario assume no customer recovery at any later date. The factor cannot also keep a customer payment for the same amount; there is no double collection.

A separate dispute clause applies if the wholesaler agrees a valid HK$20,000 credit for missing goods. In that alternative scenario the customer pays HK$80,000 to the factor on day 60 and the wholesaler pays the factor the HK$20,000 adjustment on the same day. The fee remains HK$2,000, with no further charge. This is not a credit-default event and is not combined with the no-payment scenario. Without factoring, the customer would instead pay the wholesaler HK$80,000; the bridge repayment would still be HK$93,000.

If the invoice is paid in full on day 60, the factor receives HK$100,000 and nothing further passes between factor and wholesaler. No new cash or risk protection is offered beyond these clauses. Assess all three separate scenarios; their probabilities are not supplied. The funded repayment capacity stated earlier remains available under either route.

**Student question:** After reading the recourse and dispute clauses, would you keep your funding choice? Calculate net cash across each route for full payment, zero recovery and the HK$20,000 valid adjustment, and identify the obligation that cash today has not removed.

## Trace cash, not the word sale

With full customer payment, factoring leaves HK$98,000 from the invoice and funding transaction. The bridge route produces HK$90,000 today plus HK$100,000 from the customer less HK$93,000 repayment: HK$97,000. Factoring is HK$1,000 better on the supplied charges. This is not sales profit: delivered-goods costs and the identical working-capital activity are outside the comparison.

## Recourse returns the credit exposure

In the zero-recovery stress scenario, factoring produces HK$98,000 today minus HK$100,000 on day 75: net −HK$2,000. The bridge produces HK$90,000 today, no customer receipt and HK$93,000 repayment: net −HK$3,000. Both routes still leave the seller exposed to the failed HK$100,000 receivable; factoring has not insured it.

Compared with their respective full-payment outcomes, each route is HK$100,000 worse. Do not call the entire HK$100,000 repayment a second loss in addition to the missing customer receipt: the earlier factor payment must also be included. The −HK$2,000 figure is net cash from these transaction flows, not the total economic loss on producing goods that were never paid for.

## A valid adjustment is a different event

For the separate HK$20,000 credit, factoring leaves HK$98,000 − HK$20,000 = HK$78,000. The bridge leaves HK$90,000 + HK$80,000 − HK$93,000 = HK$77,000. Both are HK$20,000 below their full-payment outcomes. The factor’s right to an adjustment is supplied by the dispute clause, not inferred from the customer becoming insolvent.

The factor receives HK$80,000 from the customer plus HK$20,000 from the seller, not two full invoice payments. Keep distinct reasons for non-payment and read their separate rules; a credit-protection label would not automatically settle responsibility for defective or missing goods.

## Choose finance without inventing insurance

Factoring remains defensible here: it supplies enough cash, saves HK$1,000 in every stated scenario and funded resources can meet its contractual repayment. The reveal changes what the arrangement achieves, not necessarily which route is preferred. It buys earlier cash and collection service, not removal of credit risk.

A seller unable to meet the day-75 HK$100,000 obligation would need a different arrangement, less exposure or confirmed protection before signing. No default probability is provided, so do not invent an expected credit-loss premium or price a non-recourse alternative. Different contract terms, reserves, fees or recovery rights could reverse the recommendation. Accounting derecognition and legal enforceability are outside this cash-flow exercise.

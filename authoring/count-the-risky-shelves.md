## Digital records still need physical checks

On 16 January 2026, Hong Kong’s Transport and Logistics Bureau announced the rollout of the Port Community System and other modern-logistics measures. This supplies a dated context for better connected records, not evidence that a particular warehouse has inaccurate stock. The spare-parts store and count results below are fictional.

## Opening decision

A fictional spare-parts store is planning its next twelve-week cycle-count schedule. Cycle counting means checking selected item records repeatedly during operations, rather than checking the whole store only at a year-end event. It has 60 item types: 20 high-value motors, 20 inexpensive connectors and 20 inexpensive brackets. Each type occupies one identified bin. Each scheduled count takes five paid minutes, including normal preparation and documentation. The routine budget is ten hours, or 120 counts.

A counts every item twice, six weeks apart. B counts one chosen 20-item priority group four times, three weeks apart, and each of the other 40 items once. Both therefore cover every item. Initially, the manager proposes motors as B’s priority because their unit values are highest; no comparable transaction or verified discrepancy history has yet been presented. The choice is uniform repeated coverage or greater frequency for a justified priority group, not permission to ignore other stock.

Both schedules preserve all financial-audit requirements, mandatory checks, safety, customer commitments and paid staffing. An independently funded exception team handles prompt recounts, investigations and any urgent checks outside the 120 routine counts. Counts are performed at a controlled transaction cutoff with movements held briefly or fully reconciled. Expected system quantities are hidden from counters. No record is changed merely to match a first count: a different trained colleague recounts discrepancies, and an authorised reviewer resolves the evidence and records any supported adjustment. Nothing here implies misconduct.

## Round two

A comparable prior twelve-week record is now supplied: every item was checked once under the same verified-count protocol. Two of the 20 motor records needed a supported quantity correction, compared with eight connector records and one bracket record. These are item counts, not total missing units or monetary losses. Connectors had ten times as many stock-movement transactions per item as motors, and a false availability record can stop an otherwise ready repair. Brackets have convenient substitutes; motors remain financially significant. None is a safety-critical or legally mandatory priority in this exercise. The history is a reason to investigate, not a forecast error probability or proof that frequency alone fixes the process.

Two separate recent exceptions illustrate verification. One bin showed 30 units on the system and a first count of 26. An independent blind recount included a tray the first counter missed and confirmed all 30: no quantity adjustment was supported. Another showed 40, with two independent counts of 36; cutoff, locations and ownership were reconciled, and the reviewer approved a four-unit quantity correction while investigating its cause. No fraud conclusion follows.

Before the new schedule starts, the manager may retain A or choose any one of the three 20-item groups for B’s four-count frequency. Staff time and all safeguards are unchanged. B with connectors still counts each motor and bracket once, but gives up their second routine check. There is no measured financial benefit or controlled comparison of the schedules. Additional urgent checks remain available through the separate exception process.

## Count the workload, not only the items

A uses 60 × 2 = 120 counts. B uses 20 × 4 + 40 × 1 = 120. At five minutes each, either uses 600 minutes, or ten routine hours. Extra exception work is separately resourced, not silently squeezed out. B changes the timing and distribution of checks; it does not create additional counting capacity.

## Value is one risk dimension

The prior verified corrections were 2/20 motor records, 8/20 connector records and 1/20 bracket records. These describe that period, not future probabilities or the value lost. Connectors’ movement frequency and repair consequence support giving them attention even though their unit value is low. Motors’ financial significance still supports regular coverage. A remains defensible when broad repeated checks are valued; focused B is a testable allocation, not a proven optimum.

## A recount is evidence, not an adjustment button

The 30-versus-26 example was a counting omission; the correct record stayed at 30. The 40-versus-36 example supported a four-unit quantity correction after reconciliation. Verify units, locations and cutoff before posting, preserve the record of what happened and investigate process causes. Repeatedly correcting balances without fixing transaction handling may leave the same operational problem in place.

## Instructor note

ACC-04 separates physical existence from valuation at a reporting date. ACC-17 considers audit selection and population conclusions. Here all items remain covered and the decision is recurring operational count frequency. Do not turn the historical counts into expected loss or claim a particular schedule meets financial-audit requirements. Monitor verified discrepancies per comparable count opportunity, transaction causes, time spent, availability failures and coverage gaps; a raw discrepancy count alone is affected by how often a group is counted.

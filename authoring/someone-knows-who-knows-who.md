## Finding expertise is a team capability

Teams can hold useful knowledge without reliably finding it. Transactive-memory research examines how colleagues recognise, trust and coordinate one another’s different expertise. A contact list can support that process but is not the process itself.

The consultancy, people, time budget and diagnostic below are fictional. Research supplies a lens, not a promised performance gain.

## Opening decision

You lead eight established staff at fictional Hong Kong research consultancy Harbour Insight. They work on regional client studies with different technical specialisms. Existing approved guidance remains available. Choose one four-week improvement package: A adds reusable answer notes; B develops and rehearses an expertise-location map.

There are 16 pooled paid person-hours for the entire team, not 16 hours each. Each package uses 12 hours for setup and four for upkeep over four weeks. All preparation, participation and updating count. Both packages fit the allowance, with displaced work covered. They cannot be combined within it.

A uses 12 hours to write and check additional notes for recurring technical questions, then four hours to maintain them. B uses six hours for eight individual 45-minute expertise entries, four hours to verify and assemble the map, and two pooled hours for four paired 15-minute routing rehearsals, one per person. Four upkeep hours remain.

B records only approved work specialisms, their boundaries, work contact routes and review dates. Staff agree their entries; existing competence checks verify the claims. No private traits or performance rankings are collected. Neither package qualifies anyone for new technical work or changes who may approve client outputs.

Specialist consultation time is separately funded and unchanged under either package. Both can use the same existing request queue, priority rules and escalation route when someone is unavailable. Neither creates spare specialist capacity. Existing confidentiality, access permissions and release checks remain mandatory; questions use only appropriately authorised information.

## Round two

A diagnostic reviews 12 recent internal requests requiring case-specific technical judgement. Four reached an appropriate specialist first; eight first went to a familiar colleague outside the relevant specialism and were redirected. The sample is small and selected for review, not representative of all requests or proof of either package’s causal effect.

For all 12, approved expertise was already present in the team. Existing notes provided background but could not settle the particular question without specialist judgement. Consultation capacity was available once requests arrived correctly. No breach or incorrect client release occurred. These findings do not establish that every future question needs consultation.

Verified specialisms include Jo: questionnaire wording; Mei: fieldwork scheduling; Arun: data-cleaning rules. Each has approved limits and the same existing consultation queue. A pilot question asks whether a proposed wording may confuse survey respondents. The requester knows Arun best, but Arun’s listed specialism does not cover wording.

**Student question:** Keep or change your choice after the diagnostic. Route the wording question, explain what the map does not authorise or solve, and specify how you would check its usefulness within the four-week allowance.

## Debrief · Find the bottleneck

B better targets the observed routing problem: needed knowledge already exists but requests do not reliably reach it. Four of 12 were initially well routed; eight of 12 were redirected. Those are request counts, not hours wasted, client failure rates or a forecast of B’s benefit.

## Debrief · Keep notes useful

A remains useful for recurring, codifiable answers and may reduce repeat questions. But the reviewed requests needed specialist judgement, so additional general notes do not directly repair their expertise-location problem. Prefer B for this bounded pilot; reconsider if a broader review finds missing reusable answers rather than poor routing.

## Debrief · Knowing where is not knowing how

Route the wording question to Jo through the approved queue, with only authorised context. That identifies an appropriate source, not an instant answer, new qualification or permission to bypass review. Jo can clarify scope or escalate beyond approved competence. Arun’s familiarity is not evidence of wording expertise.

## Debrief · Rehearse the connection

Use the funded rehearsals to practise matching a question to a specialism, checking scope and using the request route. Ask recipients to confirm or correct the match. Shared recognition, credible expertise and coordinated use matter together. Uploading a directory without people understanding or using it does not establish a working transactive memory system.

## Debrief · Maintain and check

Assign a map steward within B’s four upkeep hours: one pooled person-hour each week for entry corrections and reviewing sampled routing outcomes. Count time across every participant. Existing request records supply first destinations and redirections; do not create unfunded interviews or collect confidential client detail for the evaluation.

## Debrief · Test, do not promise

Compare appropriate first routing and redirections for similar new requests, while checking unchanged approval controls. Record request mix and specialist availability. Small before-and-after counts are diagnostic, not causal proof or guaranteed time savings. If the upkeep allowance is exhausted, narrow the pilot rather than assume unpaid work. Continuation needs a fresh funded decision.

## Debrief · Distinct capabilities

The map does not make everyone interchangeable, remove specialist bottlenecks or create external connections. When a specialist is unavailable, use existing escalation and priority rules, not an unqualified substitute. The exercise improves locating and coordinating existing expertise; backup training and additional capacity are different investments.

## Instructor note

Allow three minutes for a provisional choice, four for the diagnostic and seven for routing and evaluation. Ask groups to distinguish a useful document from a functioning shared practice. Require the pooled-hours reconciliation and one boundary the map cannot override. Do not award marks for promising automatic efficiency gains.

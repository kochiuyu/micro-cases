# One network. Every decision at headquarters?

STR-20 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Decision rights · Local information · Network coordination

![Two print-and-binding colleagues discuss a plain planner beside unmarked booklets.](../one-network-local-decisions-scene.jpg)

## One network can serve different patterns

In a 28 February 2024 reply on public library services, Hong Kong’s Government described weighing usage patterns, staffing and expenditure when setting service hours. Published library schedules also show different service patterns within one network. This is context, not evidence that library branches set their own hours. The commercial network below is fictional.

## Opening decision

A fictional two-branch print-and-binding company is introducing an additional staffed drop-off window for non-urgent booklet jobs. Existing opening hours, booked jobs, deadlines, access arrangements and ordinary customer services stay unchanged. The new window lasts four hours: either noon–4 p.m. or 3–7 p.m. Both fit each building’s permitted opening and have funded, agreed staffing with breaks and no compulsory overtime. Printing is done at a shared production centre; neither branch may change quality, privacy, safety or contractual protections.

A lets headquarters approve the window for each branch after considering local submissions. Headquarters may choose different windows; central authority does not require identical hours. B delegates the window choice to each branch manager within the same two approved options and budget, with advance notice to headquarters and customers. Neither approach has a measured cost or revenue advantage. Both can consult customers, and both retain a central coordinator for shared production and transport commitments.

The specific question is who has final authority over this optional local window, not ownership, staffing levels or the whole business. Before launch, the coordinator must check proposed service promises against transport and production capacity. No customer has yet accepted terms for the new window. Managers cannot create an unfunded guarantee or waive an existing promise in either arrangement.

## Round two

Each branch asks 24 consenting existing customers to choose one preferred window. At North, 18 choose noon–4 p.m. and six choose 3–7 p.m. At South, four choose noon–4 p.m. and twenty choose 3–7 p.m. These are selected customer preferences, not random demand forecasts, booking commitments or estimates of incremental profit. Both branch managers recommend the locally preferred window; headquarters can receive the same results.

The transport check confirms that the single existing collection at each branch is 4:30 p.m., with a 4 p.m. acceptance cutoff for reaching the production centre that day. Material accepted after 4 p.m. can be securely stored and reaches production on the next operating day. There is no funded second collection. The South manager had proposed advertising every job received during its new window as reaching production that day. That wording cannot be used under either governance arrangement; no customer has seen or relied on it.

A late window is still feasible if customers receive accurate timing before accepting the service, may decline without penalty, and all existing commitments remain protected. The coordinator must approve the shared timing promise, whether headquarters or a branch chooses the window. Either governance arrangement can use North’s early and South’s late windows, or choose differently with reasons. The decision is whether to keep central approval of the local window as well, or retain local authority with a clear escalation rule for changes affecting shared resources.

**Student question:** Would you retain central approval of each local window or delegate it with a shared-service boundary? Specify who chooses hours, who approves transport promises and what happens when a branch proposal would require another collection.

## Do not confuse authority with uniformity

Headquarters can approve different hours, and a delegated branch can choose the same hours as another. The two customer groups suggest different preferences, but do not prove that local managers must hold authority. A gives a single accountable approval point; B gives bounded adaptation without routing every permissible change through that point. The supplied facts do not quantify an optimal governance cost.

## Separate local adaptation from a network promise

North’s early preference is 18/24 = 75%; South’s late preference is 20/24, about 83%. Those selected groups are not population forecasts. South’s late window does not move the courier: a job after 4 p.m. reaches production on the next operating day. A local convenience can coexist with that delay if explained truthfully; it cannot be sold as an unavailable same-day service.

## Reserve the interdependent decision

A workable delegation rule permits either funded window, requires advance notice and accurate published timings, and sends any proposal affecting collections, production capacity or existing commitments to the coordinator before approval. The coordinator may reject the proposal or seek a properly funded, feasible change; a local manager cannot promise a second collection first. Central approval of all windows remains an alternative if the firm values that oversight, but it should still use local evidence.

## Instructor note

ENT-09 concerns founder–successor authority during succession; PPL-18 concerns a manager’s available attention. This case allocates one continuing centre–branch decision while retaining coordination of a shared dependency. Neither central control nor delegation is universally superior. Theoretical and manufacturing-survey sources supply concepts, not effect estimates for a printing network. Ask students to write two separate decision owners rather than simply recommend more autonomy or more control.

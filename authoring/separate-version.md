# The big client wants its own version

Case 36 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Entrepreneurship · Business models · Product scope

![Two software founders consider a large client proposal beside laptops in a shared office adjoining a craft studio.](../separate-version-scene.jpg)

## In the news

SHOPLINE, founded in Hong Kong, updated its plans effective 31 March 2026. Advanced customisation and catalogue-management features became part of its Enterprise-exclusive offering.

This is a real example of defining different product offers for different customers. It does not disclose the development economics or mean SHOPLINE maintains a separate version for each customer. Our fictional founders face a more demanding request.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. Two Hong Kong founders sell a shared booking-and-materials application to small craft studios. They want to grow by improving one product that many studios can use.

A workshop chain offers HK$180,000 for an expanded version, ready in three weeks. At first, the requested features sound similar to the founders’ planned improvements. The initial build is estimated at 30 developer-days. One developer-day means one person working for one day.

After routine obligations, the founders have ten developer-days per week in total. Their next shared-product release also needs 30 developer-days and was internally planned for the same three weeks. Its date has not been promised to customers. They cannot hire or add overtime capacity this quarter.

## Evidence

| Development choice | Initial work |
|---|---:|
| Workshop-chain build | 30 developer-days |
| Planned shared-product release | 30 developer-days |

Three weeks provide 3 × 10 = 30 developer-days. Both cannot fit. HK$180,000 is contract revenue, not profit or subscription revenue.

**Student question:** Prioritise the HK$180,000 customer build or protect the shared-product release? What would make this a good growth opportunity?

## Round two

The detailed brief changes the founders’ understanding of the work. The chain requires its own separately maintained version, with a unique approval sequence. Other studios do not need that workflow. The chain will not accept the shared product’s configuration options.

The HK$180,000 covers the initial build and the first 12 months of agreed changes and support. Those continuing obligations are estimated to require two developer-days every week after launch, from the same ten-day weekly pool. The initial build still takes 30 developer-days. Scope limits and acceptance terms must be documented before signing; additional work beyond that scope would need a new agreement.

The founders cannot treat this offer as shared-product development or promise the same release pace to everyone. The customer is not offering a smaller pilot or a standard-version alternative. There is no commitment yet.

**Student question:** Accept a client-specific service commitment or decline this contract to protect the shared product? Which business are you choosing to build?

## What the contract really buys

The build occupies all 30 available developer-days in the first three weeks. After launch, the chain’s continuing needs take 2 ÷ 10 = 20% of that weekly development pool, leaving eight days for other work under the estimate.

That is a capacity calculation, not a margin estimate. The price must cover the build, continuing work and other relevant costs. The offer is not an automatic annual renewal or evidence that more chains will buy the same service.

Separate versions also create separate testing and release responsibilities. Those responsibilities belong in the workload estimate, not in an unpriced promise to do everything later.

## Two legitimate businesses

Accept deliberately: choose a more client-specific service model. Confirm the 12-month scope, costs, payment timing and acceptance terms, reserve the capacity and revise the shared roadmap honestly. Serving a demanding client can be a sound business if the economics and founders’ goals support it.

Decline deliberately: keep the common product and accept losing this revenue. Explain which reusable customer needs the shared release serves and why that opportunity is worth protecting. Product scalability is an ambition, not proof that subscriptions will grow.

There is no free compromise in the stated offer: the chain rejects standard configuration and requires its own version. A different offer could change the choice, but students should not assume the customer has already agreed to one.

## A decision you can defend

For founders who remain committed to one shared product, decline this particular separate-version contract. If they prefer a specialist service business, acceptance can be defensible after costing the whole obligation. Neither choice is established by the headline fee alone.

Early hands-on customer work can reveal reusable needs. Here, however, the revealed requirement is customer-specific and continuing. Distinguish learning with a customer from becoming responsible for that customer’s changing workflow.

Key idea: a large customer can change not only the roadmap, but what business the founders are in.

## Instructor note

Use 2 minutes for the initial vote, 3 for the separate-version reveal and 5–7 for contrasting the two business models. Ask acceptance advocates to price and reserve continuing capacity. Ask rejection advocates what evidence supports the shared-product opportunity they are protecting. Do not treat either services or software subscriptions as inherently superior. No legal interpretation of an actual contract is required.

# The impressive co-founder. The complementary co-founder?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 127 -->

ENT-11 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Founding teams · Complementary skills · Working alignment

![A software founder listens to two potential partners beside a laptop prototype, workflow sketches and blank exhibition-label samples.](../complementary-founder-scene.jpg)

## In the news

On 22 January 2025, HKSTP reported that Hong Kong Techathon+ 2025 had brought together more than 1,800 participants forming over 380 teams. The event connected people around proposed ventures.

Meeting an impressive teammate is not the same as choosing a long-term co-founder. The people, product and working sessions below are fictional, not accounts of any Techathon participant.

## Opening decision

**Fictional classroom situation.** You are Kai, a Hong Kong developer building a tool that turns exhibition organisers’ approved object lists into print-ready labels. It does not research or authenticate exhibits. You can build the core software, but have little experience introducing a new workflow to venue staff.

Two adults are interested in founding the venture with you. Alex is an accomplished developer with successful product launches and a strong record of working well with you on earlier projects. Bo has introduced production workflows at several small venues and understands staff training, handoffs and customer conversations. You know Bo’s work through verified references, but have not previously worked together.

The next three months require both reliable software and learning how paying venues would adopt it. You can fund one co-founder’s agreed initial stipend and a small fixed contractor allowance. Either combination can pursue a narrower, achievable scope; there is no promised customer deadline or investor target requiring a particular choice. The allowance buys defined tasks, not a second co-founder’s continuing ownership of decisions.

Both candidates initially say they can commit at least 16 hours a week. You contribute 24. Stipends, operating costs and a responsible stop option are affordable under either route. Neither person has been offered ownership or a management title. Today’s choice is whom to invite into detailed founding discussions, subject to mutual agreement, not to issue shares or sign a partnership.

## Evidence

| Potential founding combination | A · Kai + Alex | B · Kai + Bo |
|---|---|---|
| Useful strength | Technical depth, launch experience and known rapport | Venue workflows, implementation and customer learning alongside Kai’s development |
| Main gap | Neither has led venue adoption; Kai must own that learning with limited task support | Kai remains main developer; complex technical changes may require narrower scope |
| Missing evidence | How shared technical preferences shape priorities | How unfamiliar partners disagree and divide work |

No supplied sales forecast, skill score or formula converts hours into success.

**Student question:** Which person would you provisionally invite into founding discussions, Alex or Bo? Identify the work your chosen combination covers, the gap you would still own and one observation that could change your choice.

## Round two

The same paid two-day working exercise is completed separately with each candidate, using fictional venue records and an agreed brief. Alex quickly diagnoses an import problem and makes the prototype more reliable. Alex prefers to complete another integration before approaching venues; you initially agree.

Bo works through the print-and-approval sequence with you and notices that a corrected object list can leave an older label in the print queue. Bo proposes testing a clear approval handoff before adding more integrations. When you disagree about priority, Bo asks what evidence would settle it; you jointly specify a small workflow check. No actual customer result has yet been observed.

The availability discussion also becomes concrete: Alex can provide 24 hours a week for three months; Bo can provide 16, including two agreed shared working blocks. Both commitments are credible and voluntary, with ordinary boundaries respected. Alex is not unavailable, and Bo is not offering unlimited support.

This is one bounded exercise, not a verdict on either person’s character or a guarantee of compatibility. Alex’s useful technical contribution and familiar rapport remain real. Bo offers evidence of a different contribution and a workable discussion process, but fewer hours and less shared history. Budgets and your own 24 hours are unchanged.

**Student question:** Keep or change your preferred partner? Explain which observation matters for this venture, not just who has more hours. Before making an ownership offer, name one responsibility and one disagreement rule you would need to agree together.

## Choose a combination, not a résumé winner

Alex adds depth to work Kai already does and demonstrated a real technical improvement. Bo adds capability closer to the current adoption gap. Neither is the better co-founder for every venture: a difficult technical milestone could make extra technical depth particularly valuable.

Ask who will own each important task after the discussion ends. An impressive background is evidence of experience, not proof that a task will be covered in this team.

## Difference must become usable work

Bo’s observation links the software to the venue’s approval process. The respectful disagreement and agreed evidence check make that contribution more usable. They do not prove that every future conflict will be easy or that venues will buy.

A deliberate choice of Alex remains defensible if Kai prioritises technical progress and accepts responsibility for venue learning. It is weaker if it simply assumes that known rapport removes the uncovered work.

## Hours do not replace coverage

Kai plus Alex provide 48 planned hours a week; Kai plus Bo provide 40. The eight-hour difference is real capacity, not an eight-unit advantage in every task. Work is not interchangeable, and meetings and coordination use some of those hours.

Bo’s two shared blocks are already within the 16 hours. Neither a contractor allowance nor a lower-priority task can silently become a free additional founder.

## Agree before ownership

Discuss the first responsibilities, realistic availability, how priorities will be chosen and how an unresolved disagreement will be handled. Both people must be willing to accept the arrangement; Kai is not appointing an employee unilaterally.

Ownership, pay, intellectual-property arrangements and exit provisions need separate, appropriate agreement and advice. This case asks which relationship to explore, not for a universal equity split or legal document.

## Instructor note

Allow 2 minutes to read the opening, 3 for a first vote and task-coverage map, 2 for the reveal, 3 for a revised choice and 2 for synthesis. Ask students to name who will own technical delivery and venue adoption under each combination.

Keep the decision narrow: Kai is choosing whom to invite into mutual founding discussions, not appointing an employee, issuing ownership or agreeing an equity split. Both candidates remain defensible. Ask Alex supporters how they will cover adoption; ask Bo supporters how fewer hours and less technical depth affect the initial scope.

Alex’s 24 hours and Bo’s 16 are availability, not productivity scores. With Kai’s 24, the totals are 48 and 40 planned hours; Bo’s two shared blocks are already included. The exercise gives bounded evidence about contributions and discussion, not proof of future compatibility or customer demand. End by naming one uncovered task and one question to resolve before any founding agreement.

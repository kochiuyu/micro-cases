# Sell the product. Or license the invention?

Case 91 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Entrepreneurship · Commercialisation · Licensing

![Two adult founders examine a connector and a rearrangeable wooden desktop organiser at a workbench with plain packaging and sketches.](../route-to-market-scene.jpg)

## In the news

On 1 April 2015, PolyU announced licensing its construction-uniform technology to the Construction Industry Council. Its account described sublicensing to contractors, fabrication to PolyU specifications and continued PolyU testing support.

This historical example separates invention, rollout and technical support. It does not establish startup royalty returns or the performance of our unrelated, fictional desktop product.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. Two Hong Kong founders have a working connector prototype that lets users rearrange desktop storage dividers without loose screws. Their company owns the relevant invention rights and documented know-how. For this exercise, appropriate professional work has established that both proposed commercialisation routes are available.

A: launch their own organiser brand, using a contract manufacturer. B: license the connector to an established office-accessory maker for its own branded products, while retaining ownership of the invention.

The founders enjoy technical design but are also interested in building a brand. A small launch or the transfer route is affordable, but they can give only one route proper attention this quarter. Market demand is unproven, no agreement is signed and no return forecast settles the choice.

## Evidence

| Responsibility or return | A · Own product brand | B · License to maker |
|---|---|---|
| Product range, inventory, customers and returns | Founders’ company | Maker, for its products |
| Production execution | Contract manufacturer under founders’ commercial plan | Maker’s own arrangements |
| Founders’ continuing work | Product and customer business, plus invention | Technical transfer and licence relationship, plus invention |
| Potential return | Product-business upside and downside | Negotiated licence payments; terms and sales not established |

The decision is where to direct the quarter’s work, not whether to sign a legal document today. Licensing is not selling the invention outright or simply outsourcing production for the founders’ own brand.

**Student question:** Which route would you prioritise this quarter? Name the operating responsibility you want to own and one fact about the partner that would change your answer.

## Round two

The maker supplies a funded six-week engineering and validation plan, a named lead and access to suitable existing production and sales systems. In a confidential supervised check, its engineer makes a working sample from the founders’ technical pack without the founders doing the assembly. Durability and production-quality testing are still required.

The proposed transfer scope is now specific: ten working days of founder effort in total, across both founders, spread over six weeks for documentation, troubleshooting and review. A separate proposed fee covers that stated transfer effort; it is not dependent on sales royalties. The maker funds its own engineering, testing and production preparation. Extra development would require a separate agreement.

This bounded scope fits the founders’ quarter. The fee and other commercial terms must still be agreed before work begins; royalty rates, reporting, obligations and exit provisions need professional negotiation. Nothing is signed, no payment or profit is guaranteed, and the founders remain responsible for what they promise.

**Student question:** Does the demonstrated transfer and funded plan change your preferred route? Which responsibilities would move to the maker, which remain with the founders, and what evidence would stop your chosen route?

## Choose the business, not just who makes the object

Under A, another firm makes the product, but the founders still run the customer-facing business. They must arrange quality control, inventory, acquisition, orders and returns. Outsourcing fabrication does not remove those commercial responsibilities.

Under B, the maker builds its own products around the licensed technology. The founders retain their invention but focus on transfer and the licence relationship rather than operating that retail brand. These are different ventures, not simply two manufacturing quotes.

## Recognise what the positive evidence adds

The named team, funded plan and successful supervised assembly make B a more credible execution path. The partner brings resources and systems the founders would otherwise need to arrange. The defined transfer scope also makes the founders’ own workload easier to assess.

This gives a reason to prioritise completing B’s negotiations and preparing its bounded transfer, subject to acceptable terms before work. A remains a valid choice if the founders value building a brand and direct customer learning enough to undertake the operating work.

## Do not confuse transferability with a proven market

A working sample shows that this engineer could use the technical pack in the supervised check. It does not establish reliable mass production, durability, customer demand or future sales.

The separate fee addresses the stated transfer effort, not all future obligations or guaranteed profit. Sales-based royalties would depend on the eventual agreement and sales. No quantities or payment forecast allow a numerical winner here, and a royalty is not the maker’s total revenue or the founders’ profit.

## Keep the founders’ remaining responsibilities visible

For B, agree the technical deliverables, support boundary and review arrangements before starting. Watch whether the partner funds the promised validation, provides usable reporting and can progress beyond a supervised sample. Reconsider if the work expands without workable resources or terms.

For A, check that the founders can actually organise production quality, inventory and customer service alongside development. Reconsider if launch evidence undermines demand or the operating burden is beyond the team’s capacity. Neither route removes commercial risk or the need to honour commitments.

## Instructor note

Take a provisional route vote before the partner evidence. Do not make the founders exclusively interested in design: genuine interest in a brand keeps A credible. Ask students which responsibilities they want to own rather than whether licensing is always easier.

The reveal enables bounded transfer, not immediate signing. Ten working days is combined founder effort, not ten each; six weeks is elapsed time, not six weeks of full-time founder work. Fees are proposed and limited to the stated effort. Extra development and acceptable commercial terms are not silently assumed.

Rights and route availability are stipulated so the discussion can focus on commercialisation. This is not patent eligibility, a secrecy decision, a university ownership dispute or a legal opinion guaranteeing freedom from every future claim. The quarter’s attention is the constraint; no artificial revenue calculation is needed.

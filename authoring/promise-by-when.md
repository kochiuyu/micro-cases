# Promise Friday. Based on which stock?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 148 -->

OPS-13 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Available to promise · Time-phased commitments · Delivery scheduling

![A production coordinator and salesperson discuss a delivery request beside desk components and neatly stored assembly kits.](../promise-by-when-scene.jpg)

## Industry context

On 9 April 2024, DHL announced its Hong Kong West Service Center, describing automated sorting and shipment-handling checks alongside expanded capacity.

Capacity and handling are part of a delivery process, not proof that any particular customer promise is feasible. The workshop, stock records and times below are fictional and unrelated to DHL’s service commitments.

## Opening decision

A fictional Hong Kong desk workshop assembles a standard desk after a customer orders. Each desk needs one complete kit. The weekly record shows ten inspected kits on hand, eight more kits arriving this week and eight kits committed to earlier accepted orders. There is no loss, substitution or extra material requirement. A new customer asks for four desks delivered by 2pm Friday; it has not yet received a promise or paid.

The salesperson calculates 10 + 8 − 8 = 10 kits left for new work and wants to confirm Friday. The coordinator says that weekly totals omit the dates when kits become usable and when existing commitments require them. All underlying records are current and accurate; this is not a stale-screen problem. The date-level record is available for checking before any commitment is made.

Assembly staff and vehicles can support either proposed delivery pattern once uncommitted, inspected kits are available. Each new batch of up to four desks takes four elapsed working hours to assemble, check and pack, then two hours to load, travel and deliver. No processing overlaps for the same batch. Existing orders have separately reserved staff and transport capacity. No extra labour, unsafe shortcuts or broken commitments are allowed.

## Round two

The current date-level record shows that six of the ten on-hand kits are reserved for an accepted Friday order. Two more are already allocated to another accepted order whose customer agreed earlier preparation on Friday for Monday delivery. That allocation is not available to the new customer; no change has been agreed. This leaves two on-hand kits uncommitted and ready for Friday assembly at 8am.

The eight incoming kits physically arrive at 3pm Friday but become usable only after required inspection ends at noon Saturday. For this calculation, arrival and successful release of all eight at those times are stipulated; there is no other receipt or failure scenario. The inspection cannot be waived. Existing orders use exactly the eight already reserved kits; no further demands arise before these options finish.

The new customer can choose either four desks together at 6pm Saturday at the original quoted price, or two by 2pm Friday and two at 6pm Saturday for an additional HK$300 covering the entire extra delivery cost. The workshop can support both schedules with its funded normal capacity. The customer has said both patterns could be useful, but has not accepted either or authorised the extra charge. Full Friday delivery is still its preference. No borrowing from existing allocations or external emergency supply is available.

**Student question:** Can you promise all four desks by Friday 2pm? Trace usable, uncommitted kits and processing times, then propose an honest full or split delivery offer without changing earlier commitments.

## A weekly balance is not a Friday promise

The weekly total is correct: ten on hand plus eight incoming less eight existing commitments leaves ten kits for new demand over the horizon. It does not put all ten at Friday’s assembly start. Before the incoming release, uncommitted usable kits are 10 − 6 − 2 = 2. Four desks by Friday therefore cannot be promised under the stated constraints.

The two kits for Monday delivery are not automatically free on Friday. They have an approved earlier preparation allocation. Removing it without agreement would simply transfer the shortage to an earlier customer. A promise system must respect commitments as well as physical quantity.

## Work backward from the requested delivery

Friday delivery at 2pm requires assembly to begin at 8am: four hours to assemble, check and pack, then two hours to load and deliver. Only two uncommitted kits are usable at 8am. A 3pm Friday arrival is already too late for that delivery, even before considering inspection; noon Saturday release is the actual material-availability point.

For the full order on Saturday, four kits can enter assembly at noon, finish processing at 4pm and reach the customer at 6pm. Keeping the two uncommitted on-hand kits unused until then is feasible. Under the split pattern, two use Friday’s 8am–2pm flow and two use Saturday’s noon–6pm flow. Each pattern has separately confirmed capacity; material arithmetic alone would not establish that in a real workshop.

## Offer a choice that the customer can accept

Explain that all four cannot arrive Friday without breaking a prior allocation or skipping required steps. Offer four together at 6pm Saturday for the original price, or two Friday at 2pm plus two Saturday at 6pm for HK$300 more. Do not silently impose the split or add the charge.

A customer who needs some workstations Friday may value the split; one who wants a single delivery may prefer Saturday together. The supplied facts do not price that preference. Confirm the chosen quantities, dates and charge before reserving the new allocation, and record it so later enquiries cannot use the same kits.

## Physical stock, usable stock and uncommitted stock

Physical arrival is not inspection release, and inspected stock is not necessarily free of commitments. For either accepted new four-desk order, total remaining uncommitted kits after both receipts and all orders are accounted for are 10 + 8 − 8 − 4 = 6. A positive ending balance does not repair a negative or inadequate balance at an earlier date.

This example isolates time-phased material promising while supplying feasible assembly and transport capacity. Real made-to-order promises must also check production capacity, component completeness, handling, transport and uncertainty. Future receipt dates are planning assumptions, not guarantees; monitor changes and communicate promptly.

## Instructor note

Use 3 minutes for the weekly-total vote, 4 to draw the Friday/Saturday timeline and 4 for a two-sentence customer offer. Ask students to mark physical arrival, usable release, existing allocations and the start of processing separately. Do not reward a Friday promise created by borrowing someone else’s kits.

There is a definite feasibility answer for full Friday delivery, but two legitimate later offers. The customer decides whether the split is worth HK$300. All quantities, timing, capacity and successful receipt/inspection outcomes are explicit fiction. This is not a software recommendation or claim about a named logistics provider. The distinction from stale information is intentional: correct data can still be aggregated incorrectly.

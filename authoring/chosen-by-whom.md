# Designed for an age group. Chosen by whom?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 75 -->

MKT-07 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Marketing · Positioning · Buyer and user

![An older woman checks a daybag’s zipper and strap in a small shop, with an adult child nearby and the owner listening.](../chosen-by-whom-scene.jpg)

## In the news

On 27 May 2025, Hong Kong’s government announced 30 silver-economy measures across five areas, including consumption and industry development.

This gives a fictional bag brand a reason to consider older consumers. It does not establish demand for its product, government endorsement or a requirement to advertise with an age label.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. A small Hong Kong bag brand is launching a light daybag with easy-grip zipper pulls and wide straps. Its factual benefit claims have been checked. It is not a medical product.

An older adult might choose the bag personally, or an adult child might buy it as a gift. The owner can run one small launch-message pilot first and has two equally feasible mock-ups.

Both use the same bag, price, readable typography, photographs and placement. Both naturally feature older people. Only the headline differs: A identifies an age group; B describes practical benefits. Neither is deliberately patronising or guaranteed to work.

## Evidence

| Message A · Age-led | Message B · Benefit-led |
|---|---|
| “A daybag for 60+” | “Light to carry. Easy to open.” |
| May make relevance easy for some buyers to recognise | May make use benefits clear without defining the customer by age |

**Student question:** Which message, A or B, would you pilot first? Name whose response you are trying to win and a specific response that would make you change your mind.

## Round two

The owner realises the first informal reactions came mainly from adult children shopping for parents. A second conversation includes intended users. Three fictional comments illustrate different perspectives:

- Gift buyer: “The age heading helps me spot a possible gift, but I still need to know whether Mum wants it.”
- Intended user: “The easy zip matters to me. I want to choose the colour myself; I wouldn’t search for an age label.”
- Another intended user: “I like knowing the designer considered people my age. Show me what is different about the bag.”

These are illustrative conversations, not a representative survey or evidence that either headline sells more. No sales result is supplied.

**Student question:** Keep or change your first choice of A or B? Specify one result from intended users that would justify that choice, rather than relying only on gift buyers’ reactions.

## Separate the person buying from the person using

A gift buyer may welcome a quick relevance cue, while the intended user may care more about features, appearance or having a choice. Sometimes buyer and user are the same person; sometimes they are not.

The new evidence does not show that gift buyers are wrong. It shows that their reactions alone cannot establish whether the message resonates with intended users. A useful pilot must hear from both where both matter.

## An age category is not one motivation

The two user comments point in different directions. One responds to the practical feature without seeking an age label; another welcomes age relevance but still wants concrete differences explained. Neither speaks for every older customer.

Do not infer that older people always dislike age labels, always want them, or share one level of independence, activity or need. Accurate neutral age information can be useful, and benefit-led language can be useful. Their fit depends on the actual audience and offer.

## Make a choice that evidence can change

Choose A if your hypothesis is that intended users recognise and welcome its relevance, not merely that adult children notice it. Choose B if your hypothesis is that clearly stated benefits better communicate why users would consider the bag. Neither hypothesis is established by these comments.

Before the pilot, specify an observable result: for example, whether intended users correctly explain the offer, find the wording relevant and want to try the bag. If the selected message repeatedly confuses users or makes the offer feel irrelevant to them, that is a reason to reconsider it. Do not invent a numerical success rate that the case has not measured.

## Keep the comparison focused

Include intended users as well as gift purchasers, and distinguish their responses. Keep the product, claims, price, presentation and placement comparable. If showing both mock-ups, vary which appears first so the order does not consistently favour one.

This small pilot is not a complete causal field experiment. Subsequent purchases, returns and user feedback can provide stronger evidence than a few convenient conversations. A later variation could combine a benefit headline with accurate age-related information, but it still needs testing; it is not a magical third option that removes the initial choice.

## Instructor note

Require students to commit provisionally to A or B before the reveal, name their intended audience and state reversal evidence. Afterwards, ask whether they have learned that their message is wrong or that their evidence omitted an important voice.

A strong answer preserves the user’s agency, treats the comments as varied qualitative clues and does not announce a universal winning headline. Both mock-ups are deliberately legitimate starting points.

The brand, bag, messages and comments are invented. No medical benefit, consumer stereotype or sales forecast is implied.

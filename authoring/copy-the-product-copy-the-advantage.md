# A copied product. A copied advantage?

## The visible offer and the system behind it

Strategy research distinguishes a product customers can see from the resources and relationships that help deliver it. Some capabilities develop through repeated joint work rather than a single purchase. That can make copying harder, but does not make the capability permanent or automatically profitable.

This fictional case asks what to invest in after imitation. It is not an allegation about a real competitor, an intellectual-property dispute or advice to restrict partners’ freedom to work elsewhere.

## Opening decision

**Fictional classroom situation.** You run a Hong Kong rental service providing decorative plant displays to offices and event venues. A rival now offers a lawful, similar-looking version of your existing modular planter. Basic product quality and ordinary rental service are comparable. There is no infringement or confidentiality dispute in this scenario.

You have **HK$60,000** for discretionary development this quarter, but management capacity permits only one programme. A develops new interchangeable colour sleeves, makes samples and tests customer reactions. B strengthens a service routine developed with your independent delivery and display-care partners: agree venue access, check the requested arrangement, confirm who handles exceptions and complete the handover together. B pays for joint practice, a shared handover log and backup coverage. Either original proposal costs HK$40,000; unused funds stay uncommitted.

The aim is a more compelling future offer, not fixing unsafe or unfulfilled existing work. Current contracts, normal partner fees and required safety, privacy and access arrangements remain separately funded under every choice. B would test a new coordinated short-notice changeover service before offering it; no customer has been promised that service. Partners remain independent and free to work for others.

Recent customer conversations mention both a fresh appearance and fewer calls to coordinate changes. They supply neither a willingness-to-pay estimate nor a sales forecast. No evidence yet establishes how quickly the proposed feature could be copied or how reliably the routine would survive a change of personnel. You may also defer both programmes while seeking better evidence.

## Round two

**Before any development money is committed, three updates arrive.** A supplier assessment establishes, for this hypothetical situation, that the rival has access to the same sleeve-making equipment and could reproduce the new visible feature within four weeks of seeing it. That is a capability assessment, not proof it will do so, that imitation is free, or that customers will leave.

A supervised rehearsal of ten scripted, non-customer changeover requests records nine complete, timely handovers by the established partner team and six by a newly assembled, appropriately qualified team using the same planter and written checklist. Success means the agreed arrangement and handover are completed within the booked window, with no avoidable coordination callback. All safety and required access checks are preserved. The newly assembled team is not the rival. This small, non-randomised exercise does not isolate the effect of any one routine or predict future success rates.

The established partners can name shared habits and whom they contact when plans change, but cannot separate the effects of experience, trust, the checklist and individual judgement. The rival does not currently have this jointly practised routine with these partners; it could develop another system.

One key partner now chooses not to join the new programme next quarter. Existing contracts remain covered. A replacement is available, but onboarding and rehearsal add a quoted **HK$12,000** to B, making its complete development cost HK$52,000. The revised quote funds the same test scope, including replacement-team checks; it does not guarantee matching the established team’s performance. A remains HK$40,000. Both remain within the HK$60,000 envelope.

**Student question:** After the imitation and partner update, would you choose A at HK$40,000, revised B at HK$52,000, or defer both? Distinguish evidence of a harder-to-copy capability from proof of a lasting advantage, and set one test before making a new customer promise.

## Separate the output from the capability

A planter shape or sleeve is a visible output. The equipment that makes it is an input. A repeatable handover depends on people recognising issues, contacting the right partner and acting together. Owning the planter or buying the same machine does not establish that a rival can reproduce that whole service immediately.

Neither does being less visible establish value. The routine must solve something customers care about, at a cost the firm and its partners can sustain. A feature can still be worth developing for customer appeal or competitive parity even if it is easy to copy. Copyability alone is not a reason to abandon useful design.

## Two different imitation difficulties

**Causal ambiguity** means the link between resources and performance is unclear. Here even the team cannot identify which combination explains the rehearsal difference. The counts alone do not establish a cause, and hiding a document is not the same thing as causal ambiguity.

**Social complexity** concerns relationships and coordinated habits formed through joint work. Someone might understand that familiarity helps handovers without being able to purchase an equivalent working relationship tomorrow. These mechanisms can overlap, but are not identical. The case offers reasons to investigate imitation difficulty, not proof of an uncopyable advantage.

## What the rehearsal does and does not show

The established team completed three more of the ten scripted requests without a coordination callback: nine versus six. That is a descriptive difference in this exercise, not a causal treatment effect, a probability forecast or a comparison of profits. Team composition, familiarity and other conditions may matter. The replacement must be tested rather than assumed to inherit past performance.

A harder-to-copy routine can also be harder to transfer inside your own network. Write down useful handover information and train backups without assuming a checklist contains every skill. Deliberately keeping staff confused would damage learning; ambiguity is a limit to understand, not a management goal.

## Reprice the capability you want to maintain

Revised B costs HK$40,000 + HK$12,000 = **HK$52,000**, leaving HK$8,000. A leaves HK$20,000. B therefore commits HK$12,000 more this quarter. Both are affordable; affordability does not establish which generates more value.

The extra onboarding bill illustrates a maintenance burden, not a permanent barrier protecting the owner. A relationship can weaken when a partner leaves, and partners have their own costs and alternatives. Assess ongoing coverage, fair compensation, consent and workable handovers. Do not assume the firm owns partners’ knowledge or can prevent departure.

## A recommendation with a condition

A defensible preference is revised B if the intended customer promise is lower coordination effort and management accepts paying more to test that capability. Before selling the new service, use fresh representative rehearsal requests with the replacement team, explicit completion criteria and recorded callbacks, delays and full delivery costs. A pass should be followed by a limited, honestly described customer pilot and evidence of customer value; no tiny rehearsal proves durability.

A is defensible if management prioritises visible appeal and rapid customer testing while accepting a short copying window. Defer both if neither has enough evidence to justify the development spend. There is no supplied revenue or margin forecast to produce a unique financial winner.

Whichever you choose, monitor alternatives. Customers may satisfy the same need differently, and rivals may build their own effective routines. Sustained advantage requires more than one good result or a resource labelled “rare”.

## Instructor note

Allow 15–20 minutes: three for the first choice, four for the updates, five for distinguishing visible output and service capability, and three to eight for conditional recommendations. Keep the update closed until students state what they believe creates value. Require an evidence gap, not a slogan such as “relationships are our moat”.

The budget arithmetic is deliberately simple; the judgement concerns imitability, value and upkeep. Do not convert nine out of ten into a guaranteed 90% future success rate. Do not claim the rival fails six tests: it was not in the rehearsal. Replacement-team validation is part of revised B, not a free extra. Existing customers’ promises and mandatory standards remain protected even if both proposals are deferred.

STR-06 weighs sharing a physical set with a competitor; STR-19 evaluates transferring capabilities to a new business; ENT-06 concerns discovering a visible mechanism and investigating legal protection. Here an existing business compares investment responses to imitation, distinguishing outputs from joint routines and recognising their maintenance cost. No legal filing, exclusivity or anti-competitive coordination is requested.

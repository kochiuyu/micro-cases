# Share the studio. Share the advantage?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 66 -->

STR-06 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Strategy · Coopetition · Differentiation

![Two photographers discuss a shared workspace beside a camera tripod, a softbox and a plain backdrop.](../shared-advantage-scene.jpg)

## In the news

On 9 November 2023, Hong Kong’s Communications Authority approved a joint spectrum swap and transfer request involving China Mobile Hong Kong, Genius Brand, HKT and Hutchison Telephone. Its assessment considered technical efficiency, service quality and competition, and the approval carried conditions.

A separate April 2024 announcement by Singtel, Optus and TPG described sharing regional Australian network resources while retaining separate core networks and metropolitan networks. These are examples of defined cooperation, not evidence that sharing every resource is beneficial. The photography studios below are fictional.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. You run an independent Hong Kong product-photography studio. A competing studio proposes sharing premises and basic shooting equipment for the coming year. Your annual facility expense would fall from HK$240,000 to HK$160,000.

Both businesses would keep their own photographers, clients, quotations and prices. You would still compete for assignments. Your studio also has a distinctive modular set that creates a recognisable look for clients’ products. It is central to your current offer, alongside your photographers’ skills.

The quoted annual figures cover the same facility-cost categories and include any move and setup costs for that year. Staffing and other operating costs are outside this comparison. You can afford either option. The saving is attractive, but the proposal’s detailed access schedule has not yet been reviewed.

## Evidence

| For the coming year | Own premises | Shared arrangement |
|---|---|---|
| Your facility expense | HK$240,000 | HK$160,000 |
| Photographers and client relationships | Separate | Separate |
| Quotations and prices | Independent | Independent |
| Resource access | Your studio controls access | Detailed schedule not yet reviewed |

**Student question:** Would you favour sharing the facility or keeping your own premises? Which resource-access detail would you check before making a commitment?

## Round two

The access schedule includes your distinctive modular set. The rival would be able to book it for its own client shoots, not just use the neutral equipment. The HK$160,000 quote is for this complete arrangement. Excluding the set at the same price has not been offered.

Your photographers and client relationships remain separate. The rival’s access would remove your exclusive use of the set, but it would not automatically reproduce your photographers’ skills or service. There is no evidence yet showing how many clients would switch, what prices either studio would charge, or whether a cheaper facility would help you win additional work.

**Student question:** Now that sharing includes the distinctive set, would you accept the quoted arrangement, retain your own premises, or seek revised terms? Explain which customer promise you would protect or deliberately change, and what you would do if narrower terms are unavailable.

## Separate cooperation from surrendering the whole business

Coopetition means cooperating in one activity while competing in others. Sharing neutral equipment need not mean merging businesses, sharing client lists or agreeing customer prices. The relevant question is which resources the arrangement makes common.

Here, the access schedule changes the strategic meaning of the proposal: it includes a set central to the studio’s current proposition, not only a neutral facility. Separate company names do not preserve exclusive access to a shared resource.

## The saving is real; its strategic value is not automatic

The stated facility saving is HK$80,000 for the coming year: HK$240,000 minus HK$160,000. It is not an established increase in profit. Revenue, other costs and future competitive responses are not supplied.

Accepting could be sensible if the studio chooses a lower-cost operating model and expects its skills, relationships or service to remain compelling. Rejecting could be sensible if exclusive access to the set is important enough to the chosen customer promise. Neither conclusion follows from the saving alone.

## Ask what customers value, not just what you own

Review why clients selected the studio and what they would be unable to obtain elsewhere after sharing. Distinguish the physical set from the photographers’ judgement, preparation, reliability and service. These may complement one another; access to one does not necessarily reproduce the whole offer.

Ask for evidence about willingness to pay for exclusive access and what alternatives clients already use. The case does not establish lost customers, weaker pricing, extra bookings or a monetary value for exclusivity. Do not invent those effects to force a preferred answer.

## Define a boundary, and a fallback

A revised proposal might share neutral space and equipment while reserving the distinctive set. But its availability, cost and practical scheduling would need to be established. Do not claim the HK$80,000 saving while quietly removing a condition attached to that quote.

If a narrower arrangement is unavailable, the decision returns to the actual alternatives: accept shared access and deliberately adapt the proposition, or pay for independent premises. Keep client quotations and prices independent. Any real competitor collaboration needs appropriate commercial and legal review; this classroom discussion does not approve an agreement.

## Instructor note

Invite students to defend both cost savings and independent control before the reveal. Then ask them to mark the boundary between a common input and a resource supporting differentiation. A strong answer names the intended customer promise and the evidence needed, rather than saying simply that rivals must never cooperate.

Do not reward a negotiation answer unless it includes a fallback. A narrower arrangement has not been offered. Nor should students treat every possible customer loss as certain or assume photographers’ skills become identical when they share a set.

The regulators assess competition and consumer effects across a market; the studio also considers its own positioning. Those are different questions. A private advantage does not itself justify a restriction on competition. The telecom arrangements are not interchangeable precedents or allegations about the studios or real operators.

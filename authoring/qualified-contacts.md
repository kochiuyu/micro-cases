# More contacts. More customers?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 63 -->

MKT-06 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Marketing · Lead qualification · B2B sales

![Packaging suppliers discuss inquiry cards with a visitor beside sample presentation boxes at an exhibition booth.](../qualified-contacts-scene.jpg)

## In the news

In September 2026, an HKTDC-issued report on the Hong Kong Watch & Clock Fair and Salon de Time described more than 17,000 trade buyers attending. It also noted that online business matching remained available after the physical fair, allowing discussions to continue.

Attendance creates opportunities; suppliers still have to discover which conversations fit their products and buying timetable. The following packaging supplier and its contact lists are fictional, not an assessment of exhibitors at the reported event.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong presentation-box supplier returns from a trade exhibition. Its broad promotional activity generated 120 business contacts, while its packaging demonstrations generated 40. All 160 contacts are distinct businesses, with no overlap between lists, and have agreed to the relevant business follow-up.

The sales team can prepare only 36 tailored proposals next week. Each takes the same amount of staff time. The marketing manager suggests giving the larger list most of those slots: “It brought us three times as many contacts. Let’s not waste that success.”

The objective for these proposal slots is near-term purchasing discussions, not general awareness. A useful prospect needs packaging the supplier can make, an order size within its capacity, and a buying timetable within the next three months. The same criteria will be used for both lists. Contact counts alone do not tell the team who meets them.

**Student question:** Would you give most proposal slots to the 120-contact list? What would you find out before assigning the team’s limited time?

## Round two

The team completes the same initial screening for every business on both lists, using the same three criteria and time window. 12 of the 120 promotional contacts and 24 of the 40 demonstration contacts meet all three criteria.

These 36 businesses have confirmed interest in receiving a tailored proposal. None has placed an order. The other contacts either need a different specification or order size, or have a later buying timetable. No comparative campaign costs, margins or eventual sales outcomes have been supplied.

**Student question:** How should you allocate the 36 proposal slots now? What can you say about the two lists, and what would still be unsupported about sales or marketing effectiveness?

## Qualify the prospect, not the size of the list

The relevant screening shares are 12 ÷ 120 = 10% for the promotional list and 24 ÷ 40 = 60% for the demonstration list. These are shares meeting this supplier’s stated criteria, not sales conversion rates.

The smaller list contains twice as many currently suitable prospects: 24 versus 12. But the larger list also contains 12 worthwhile prospects. Allocating time to a whole list is less useful than identifying the businesses within it that need the next step.

## Use the available slots across both groups

With equal proposal effort and exactly 36 suitable, interested prospects, prepare 12 proposals for the promotional group and 24 for the demonstration group. This uses all 36 slots without pretending the source of a contact determines its individual value.

If capacity were tighter or proposal effort differed, prioritisation would need further evidence such as deadlines, requirements, expected contribution and staff effort. Those differences are not supplied here. Do not invent a profit-maximising ranking.

## A lead is not yet a customer

A contact is an opening conversation. A qualified prospect meets a defined test for the next sales step. A customer has actually bought. Keep those stages visible rather than reporting all 160 contacts, or all 36 suitable prospects, as new customers.

A tailored proposal may still be declined, delayed or lost to another supplier. Track subsequent responses, orders and contribution before evaluating commercial returns. A 60% screening share does not mean a 60% chance of winning sales.

## Do not discard tomorrow’s buyer

A business with a later timetable may still be a good future match. Where follow-up remains welcome and appropriate, record its timing for a later conversation. A request outside the supplier’s capabilities may need a clear, courteous response rather than an irrelevant proposal.

The observed difference does not prove demonstrations caused better prospects: visitors selected their activities. Nor does it prove broad promotion was wasteful; awareness and future relationships can be separate objectives. Review activity costs and later outcomes before reallocating the next exhibition’s budget.

## Instructor note

Invite an initial allocation based on the opening evidence, then ask what “success” means for a tailored-proposal team. Reveal the screening results only after students specify what they would check.

The best revision is not “ignore the big list” but “follow the 36 suitable prospects across both lists”. Require students to distinguish qualification shares from order conversion, and to separate immediate follow-up from next year’s marketing-budget choice.

The criteria concern business requirements, capacity and timing. No personal profiling, software deployment or external data upload is required.

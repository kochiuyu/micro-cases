# Reward the suggestion. Change the reason for suggesting?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 281 -->

PPL-24 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Intrinsic interest · Reward interpretation · Informational feedback

![Fictional workshop colleagues discuss plain sketches and a small cardboard prototype at a craft workbench.](../reward-suggestion-change-reason-scene.jpg)

## A reward can carry more than money

Motivation research distinguishes enjoying an activity from doing it for a separate outcome. Rewards and feedback can also communicate different things: appreciation of competence, useful information or pressure to produce a counted result. Research does not support treating every cash reward as harmful or every unpaid task as motivating.

This fictional workshop concerns an optional improvement programme on paid time. It does not question normal wages, employment protections or earned rewards.

## Opening decision

Eight staff in a fictional Hong Kong display-making workshop can voluntarily suggest improvements to its sample-making process. Routine work, safe methods and normal pay are fully covered. Essential safety fixes and required job tasks are handled normally, not entered for prizes. Some staff enjoy experimenting, but nobody is assumed to share the same interests. There is no disciplinary consequence for declining this programme.

Both next-month packages preserve four already-funded paid exploration person-hours: half an hour per person. Staff may work alone or collaborate, choose a relevant problem and receive specific feedback from a competent lead. Lead-review time is separately funded and equal across packages. The workshop has a further HK$1,600 for one package, not both.

Prizes offers HK$100 for each accepted idea. Each employee may register at most two submissions before the pilot, so no more than 16 can earn a prize; joint submissions count once and contributors agree any split in advance. Acceptance means a genuinely useful, feasible improvement supported by a simple demonstration, not novelty for its own sake. Small improvements qualify. Every accepted registered idea is paid; no retrospective rule change or unfunded promise is allowed.

Time spends the HK$1,600 on eight additional paid person-hours, one per person, at an all-in HK$200 each. Total exploration time becomes 12 person-hours. Staff retain discretion and informational feedback, but receive no new per-idea prize. Existing commitments stay covered without overtime or withheld wages. Longer exploration may produce fewer immediately accepted ideas and need not produce a breakthrough. A report from an earlier short prize pilot is due before this new allocation is finalised.

**Student question:** Would you use the optional HK$1,600 for idea prizes or additional protected exploration time? Explain the kind of improvement you want and how staff might interpret each offer, without treating ordinary pay as optional.

## Round two

The earlier prize pilot involved the same eight staff and four paid exploration person-hours, with the same acceptance rule. It produced 12 accepted small improvements, all genuinely useful and paid HK$100 each, costing HK$1,200. The remaining HK$400 was unused and is not added to next month’s fixed budget. Examples include clearer layout of an internal template and a simpler sample-storage label arrangement. No safety defect or damaged output is reported.

One collaborative fixture idea remained unfinished and was not submitted or rewarded. Its two contributors said that demonstrating it needed more time; one also said they had focused first on an idea that could qualify quickly. Another employee said the prize felt like recognition. These are a few voluntary comments, not a representative motivation survey. No one promised the unfinished idea would work or be more valuable than the small improvements.

There is no comparable no-prize period, random assignment, initial interest measure or verified future saving. Work demands and available ideas may have differed from earlier months. The record therefore does not establish that prizes reduced intrinsic interest, caused short-term thinking or improved total business performance. It does show what was accepted and what remained unfinished under that package.

Next month still permits either complete package. Preserve all earned prizes and normal pay. A switch to Time must be explained prospectively, with no moral judgement about people who value cash. The different number of paid hours is part of the real resource choice and would also confound a simple comparison of package results. Do not require unpaid continuation to prove interest.

**Student question:** After reviewing the earlier prize pilot, would you keep prizes or fund exploration time next? Separate what the record shows from what it cannot establish about motivation, and name evidence that could change your choice.

## Count useful outputs without inventing a verdict

The 12 accepted ideas are not fake or worthless: they met the stated rule. Prize spending was 12 × HK$100 = HK$1,200, within the HK$1,600 ceiling. An unfinished idea is not yet an implemented benefit. Counts, usefulness, effort, sustained interest and eventual savings are different outcomes; this record measures only some of them.

## Ask what the offer means to the recipient

A staff member may interpret a prize as appreciation, a helpful extra income or pressure to seek an easily counted acceptance. Specific feedback can explain what worked and leave the next approach open; praise can also feel controlling if it implies that compliance is the only acceptable response. These are possible interpretations to investigate respectfully, not personality labels inferred from one comment.

## Make either priority explicit

Keep Prizes if the immediate priority is recognising feasible incremental improvements and staff value that arrangement, while checking whether longer ideas lack support. Choose Time if the priority is exploring problems that do not fit the short demonstration window. Time costs eight additional paid hours and gives up the new prize opportunity; it does not automatically create interest, quality or commercial value. The existing four hours and feedback remain in both options.

## Test motivation separately from volume

Invite confidential voluntary feedback about choice, task interest and whether feedback helps competence. Check participation and non-participation without penalising either. Review the usefulness, development stage and time used for ideas, including collaborative work. Follow any implemented improvements before claiming savings. A bigger idea count is not proof of greater intrinsic interest; a lower count is not proof of deeper thinking.

## Keep the evidence limits visible

Comparing next month’s Time package with the earlier Prizes package changes both the optional reward and paid time, alongside period effects. It would not isolate a reward effect. A later fair, funded comparison could hold resources, tasks and feedback constant, but no such experiment is supplied here. Research varies by task interest, contingency, context and outcome; evidence about task performance is not automatically evidence about continuing intrinsic interest.

## Instructor note

Require a genuine priority and cost for either choice. Reject “cash always destroys creativity” and “more submissions proves motivation improved”. Ask learners to distinguish an optional contingent prize from guaranteed wages, and appreciation from controlling pressure. The reveal is a diagnostic observation, not an experiment. Preserve earned entitlements, safe work and paid time. Unlike the parcel-pay case, there is no hidden damage cost; unlike the learning-goal case, the key question is how an optional reward is interpreted, not a prescribed training target.

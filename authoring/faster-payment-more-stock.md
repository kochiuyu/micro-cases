# A sales forecast. How much working capital?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 446 -->

FIN-38 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Operating working capital · Cash conversion cycle · Growth funding

![Two adult distribution-business colleagues compare blank invoice sheets and stock cards beside neatly arranged cartons in a warehouse office, without visible figures or funding decisions.](../faster-payment-more-stock-scene.jpg)

## Growth needs operating investment

Growth can tie up money in stock and unpaid customer invoices even when sales have a positive margin. Supplier credit offsets some of that investment. A shorter collection period may help, but it must be considered alongside inventory requirements and the different monetary bases behind each operating-cycle measure.

## Opening decision

Fictional Harbour Office Supply, a Hong Kong distributor, plans continuing sales growth rather than one exceptional order. Existing annual credit sales are HK$3,650,000. Inventory purchases and cost of goods sold each equal 60% of sales. Current operations and their working-capital investment are already funded.

The new channel would add annual credit sales of HK$1,825,000 at the same 60% cost ratio. It does not displace existing sales. Initially the planner assumes both existing business and the new channel hold thirty inventory days, collect invoices after forty days and pay suppliers after twenty days.

Use 365 days per year and uniform daily sales, deliveries, collections and purchases once each operation is established. All purchases are on credit. At steady state, purchases equal cost of goods sold; there is no continuing inventory accumulation. Inventory is valued at purchase cost, receivables at invoiced sales value.

Average inventory = annual cost of goods sold × inventory days/365. Average receivables = annual credit sales × receivable days/365. Average payables = annual credit purchases × payable days/365. Operating working-capital requirement here means inventory + receivables − payables, excluding cash, debt and other balances.

The board has earmarked HK$210,000 of additional internal funding for this steady-state operating investment, after separately protecting all other obligations and reserves. Compare the required increment with that allowance. This is a funding-envelope decision, not permission to launch before checking the dated transition cash budget.

All amounts and days are invented planning assumptions, not contracted demand or observed averages. Other operating costs, tax, equipment and financing effects are outside this narrow calculation and separately budgeted. There are no bad debts, discounts, purchase-price changes or inventory losses in the model. Supplier payment deadlines must be honoured.

## Round two

Before commitment, the channel proposal changes: its customers would pay after ten days, but its agreed service design requires seventy inventory days, including safety stock. Supplier credit remains twenty days. The sales projection, 60% cost ratio and all existing-business assumptions remain unchanged.

These changes apply only to the new channel. The extra stock cannot be removed while claiming the same service promise; supplier payments cannot be postponed. Both payment and inventory assumptions still need verification before launch. Assess the revised funding envelope and compare cash-conversion days, without treating either as proof of profitability.

**Student question:** Does faster collection outweigh the new inventory requirement in money terms? Recalculate the additional and total operating working capital. Compare initial and revised cash-conversion days. What must still be checked before the available allowance becomes a credible launch funding plan?

## Debrief · Establish the existing base

Existing sales are HK$10,000 per day and cost of goods sold is HK$6,000 per day. Inventory is 6,000 × 30 = HK$180,000; receivables are 10,000 × 40 = HK$400,000; payables are 6,000 × 20 = HK$120,000. The funded operating requirement is HK$460,000.

The new channel adds HK$5,000 sales and HK$3,000 cost per day. Initially its inventory is HK$90,000, receivables HK$200,000 and payables HK$60,000. Its incremental requirement is 90,000 + 200,000 − 60,000 = HK$230,000. Combined business needs HK$690,000 under these assumptions.

## Debrief · Compare the allowance

The initial increment exceeds the HK$210,000 earmarked allowance by HK$20,000. Do not compare the allowance with all HK$690,000: existing HK$460,000 is already funded. Equally, do not call projected annual sales cash available today or count the existing funding a second time.

This result identifies a steady-state shortfall, not a loan automatically available from a bank. The board would need a verified additional funding source or a revised feasible proposal. It cannot create headroom by using protected reserves or assuming suppliers accept late payment.

## Debrief · Rebuild the new channel

With seventy inventory days, new-channel stock becomes 3,000 × 70 = HK$210,000. Ten receivable days give 5,000 × 10 = HK$50,000. Payables remain 3,000 × 20 = HK$60,000. Revised incremental operating working capital is 210,000 + 50,000 − 60,000 = HK$200,000.

Compared with the initial channel, inventory rises HK$120,000 while receivables fall HK$150,000; payables do not change. The net requirement falls HK$30,000. Combined with the unchanged HK$460,000 existing base, total operating working capital is HK$660,000. The growth allowance now has HK$10,000 unused capacity.

## Debrief · Days are not one cash multiplier

The initial cash-conversion cycle is 30 + 40 − 20 = 50 days. The revised new-channel cycle is 70 + 10 − 20 = 60 days. It is longer even though its monetary requirement is lower. Stock days and receivable days are multiplied by different daily amounts.

Here the requirement equals HK$3,000 × (inventory days − payable days) + HK$5,000 × receivable days. Multiplying all cycle days by sales or by cost would ignore that distinction. A lower cycle-day total alone cannot rank these two funding amounts when its components change in opposite directions.

## Debrief · Approve an envelope, not a launch

The revised HK$200,000 estimate fits the stipulated steady-state allowance. That supports the funding-envelope comparison only. It does not show whether pre-opening safety stock must be paid for before sufficient invoices are collected, or whether a particular transition date creates a larger temporary cash deficit.

Build a dated cash budget from opening cash, actual stock purchases, agreed supplier due dates and customer receipts. Include the separately budgeted obligations and protected reserves, test slower collections and demand changes, and verify committed funding. Do not book the HK$30,000 estimate reduction as an immediate cash receipt before anything has changed.

## Instructor note

Ask students to label every multiplier as sales per day or cost per day before calculating balances. Then reconcile the revised increment two ways: directly as HK$200,000, and as HK$230,000 plus HK$120,000 inventory minus HK$150,000 receivables. A longer cash cycle does not contradict the lower monetary result.

FIN-03 follows one order’s specific payment dates and a minimum customer deposit. This case models continuing activity at steady state and separates component-day measures from incremental funding. It deliberately does not solve the missing transition calendar or recommend a lender, actual borrowing amount or real business launch.

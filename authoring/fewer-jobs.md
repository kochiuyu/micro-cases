# Fewer sales. A worse price?

Case 47 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Economics · Pricing decisions · Contribution

![A parcel-wrapping shop owner and assistant discuss two blank price cards beside paper rolls and wrapped parcels.](../fewer-jobs-scene.jpg)

## In the news

On 23 March 2026, Hongkong Post announced postage adjustments effective that April, citing operating-cost pressures alongside electronic substitution and delivery competition.

The announcement states a rationale, not evidence of the eventual effect on demand or profit. Our small wrapping shop below is fictional. Its simple private-business comparison is not a model of Hongkong Post’s universal-service responsibilities.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. You run a Hong Kong shop offering a standard parcel-wrapping service at one uniform price. Postage is separate and unchanged. You are choosing between HK$10 and HK$12 per job for the coming period.

The working forecasts below refer to the same representative week. They are hypothetical planning estimates, not observed results from a controlled price trial or guaranteed sales.

The owner says: “At HK$12 we lose twenty jobs and HK$40 of sales. Keep HK$10.” Costs are not included on this first summary.

## Evidence

| Price per job | Forecast jobs | Forecast revenue |
|---|---:|---:|
| HK$10 | 100 | HK$1,000 |
| HK$12 | 80 | HK$960 |

Both are hypothetical forecasts for the same representative week.

**Student question:** Would you keep HK$10, choose HK$12, or ask for more information first? Is the lower forecast revenue enough to reject the higher price?

## Round two

Each job uses HK$6 of genuinely avoidable consumable materials. If the job is not performed, that materials cost is not incurred. This is the only variable cost in the simplified comparison.

Over this volume range, rent and scheduled staff pay remain unchanged. Service quality, opening hours and capacity also stay the same. Assume no setup expense, quantity-discount threshold, spillover into other sales or additional price-change cost.

The demand forecasts remain uncertain. These cost assumptions let you compare the two scenarios; they do not validate the forecasts.

**Student question:** Which price produces more contribution under these assumptions? How many whole jobs at HK$12 would be needed to beat the HK$10 forecast, and what would you verify before changing the price?

## Compare what remains after variable costs

HK$10: revenue HK$1,000 − materials (100 × HK$6) = HK$400 contribution.

HK$12: revenue HK$960 − materials (80 × HK$6) = HK$480 contribution.

Contribution is what remains to cover fixed costs and profit. The higher-price forecast produces HK$80 more contribution despite HK$40 less revenue. Because fixed costs are identical, its profit would also be HK$80 higher, but neither contribution figure is total profit.

## Find the comparison threshold

At HK$12, each job contributes HK$12 − HK$6 = HK$6. Matching the HK$10 forecast requires HK$400 ÷ HK$6 = 66⅔ jobs.

With whole jobs, 67 jobs contribute HK$402 and beat HK$400; 66 contribute HK$396 and do not. At 60 jobs, contribution is HK$360, so the higher price would lose this comparison.

This is an indifference threshold against the HK$10 forecast, not the shop’s break-even volume. The total fixed-cost amount has not been supplied.

## Choose with uncertainty visible

Under the stated forecasts and costs, HK$12 wins this two-option comparison. It is not proven to be the best of every possible price.

Before changing the price, assess how the volume forecasts were formed, customer alternatives, competitor responses and whether costs really vary as assumed. If testing a clearly communicated future price, monitor jobs, materials and contribution as well as revenue; respect existing commitments.

Key idea: lower revenue does not necessarily mean lower profit when fewer sales also avoid costs.

## Instructor note

Allow 2 minutes for the revenue vote, 3 for the cost reveal and 5–7 for the threshold. Ask students to distinguish revenue, contribution and final profit. Do not assume staff pay falls with jobs when the case says it is fixed.

Optional economics extension: midpoint demand elasticity magnitude is (20 ÷ 90) ÷ (2 ÷ 11) ≈ 1.22 for these two forecast points. This describes the hypothetical comparison, not an estimated real demand curve. Do not call it unit elastic simply because price and quantity each change by 20% of their different starting values.

# A thousand coupons. A better business?

Case 19 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Marketing · Incremental sales

![Two café managers discuss discount vouchers](../coupon-scene.jpg)

## In the news

On 13 December 2023, the Hong Kong Tourism Board announced 200,000 HK$100 dining vouchers for residents, available in two batches for evening meals. A popular offer can bring people to a restaurant. But a busy counter does not, by itself, tell a manager how much business the offer added.

## Opening decision

You manage a fictional independent Hong Kong café. Last week, 1,000 customers redeemed your HK$10 coupon on a HK$60 meal. Your team wants to repeat the campaign next week. You must approve it today.

Unlike the Tourism Board scheme, your café funds the entire discount. Each meal has HK$30 of variable cost. Running the campaign also costs HK$2,000. There is spare capacity, no extra staffing or displaced sales, no other purchases and no change in fixed overheads. Each redemption is one meal. For the first vote, you do not yet know which customers would have bought without the coupon.

## Evidence

Last week’s campaign · fictional café

Meals bought with a coupon: 1,000

Normal price per meal: HK$60

Discount funded by café: HK$10

Variable cost per meal: HK$30

Campaign running cost: HK$2,000

The discounted meals contribute 1,000 × (60 − 10 − 30) = HK$20,000 before the campaign running cost, or HK$18,000 after it. That is not yet the campaign’s gain compared with doing nothing.

**Student question:** Repeat the same campaign next week or hold it? What does the redemption total prove, and what is still missing?

## Round two

An analyst brings the result of a small randomized pilot comparing similar eligible customers who did and did not receive an offer. For this classroom exercise, accept its estimate: 700 of the 1,000 meals would have been bought at full price without the coupon; only 300 were additional purchases. The 700 include many regular customers.

Assume the same mix and costs if you repeat next week. Future full-price repeat visits are possible but unmeasured. All other assumptions stay fixed. This estimate is invented for the case; it is not a finding about the Tourism Board programme.

**Student question:** Would you repeat the unchanged campaign now? If you expect later benefits, say what must happen to justify them.

## Debrief

Count purchases caused by the offer, not just purchases using it. A redeemed coupon proves someone used a discount. It does not show that the discount changed their decision. Calling a customer “new” or “regular” is not enough either: a regular might make an extra visit, while a new customer might have come anyway.

Compare two worlds over the same week. With the campaign, contribution after its running cost is HK$18,000. Without it, the 700 full-price meals contribute 700 × (60 − 30) = HK$21,000. The campaign therefore reduces contribution by HK$3,000 under the supplied estimate. This is a comparison for the affected meals, not the café’s total profit.

Another way to see it: 300 additional meals earn HK$6,000; discounting 700 meals that would happen anyway gives up HK$7,000; running the campaign costs HK$2,000. Net change: 6,000 − 7,000 − 2,000 = −HK$3,000.

The repeat decision depends on the objective and the evidence. If the goal is next week’s contribution and these assumptions hold, do not repeat the unchanged campaign. If the goal is long-term acquisition, a loss now may be acceptable, but “they might return” is a hypothesis, not measured value. Ask how many later purchases the campaign causes, their contribution, and how long you will wait.

A better next experiment could target a different group or time slot, retain a comparable no-offer group and measure contribution rather than redemptions alone. Random assignment helps separate an offer’s effect from pre-existing customer interest. Check group balance, spillovers and whether a small pilot generalizes before treating its estimate as certain outside this exercise.

## Optional calculation

Hold total redemptions at 1,000 and let x be additional purchases. Each additional meal earns HK$20; each of the other 1,000 − x meals loses HK$10 relative to full price. After the HK$2,000 running cost, the campaign changes contribution by 20x − 10(1,000 − x) − 2,000 = 30x − 12,000. It breaks even at 400 additional meals (40%); it improves contribution only above 400.

At the revealed 300 additional meals, the shortfall is HK$3,000. A later full-price meal with the same cost contributes HK$30. Exactly 100 later meals caused by the campaign, with no further discounts or acquisition costs, would cover that shortfall before allowing for timing or risk. More than 100 would be needed for a positive gain. Repeat visits that would have happened anyway do not count.

## Back to the news

The public vouchers were intended to stimulate evening activity, not to run this café’s experiment. The Tourism Board’s December arrangements distinguished claiming a voucher into a digital wallet from redeeming it at a restaurant. Collection is not the same as redemption, and neither alone establishes additional spending. The January government reply described programme objectives, not a causal estimate for this café.

A separate 2011 US survey of restaurant deal users included both frequent customers and people new to a restaurant. That supports asking who uses an offer, but customer histories and stated intentions do not establish what would happen without it. The old US survey cannot supply a Hong Kong effect estimate. The café’s costs and 700/300 split are invented, and cannot evaluate a publicly funded programme with wider objectives.

## Instructor note

Minutes 0–3: ask for a repeat-or-hold vote using the opening exhibit. Ask supporters of repeating which number makes them confident; ask those holding what information would change their minds. Minutes 3–7: reveal the pilot estimate and compare HK$18,000 with HK$21,000. Minutes 7–12: discuss a redesign and a measurement plan. Use the optional threshold if time permits.

Do not equate “regular” with “not incremental”: the counterfactual purchase is what matters. Ask repeat supporters to specify measurable future contribution rather than promise loyalty. Ask hold supporters whether targeting or different timing could make a new campaign worthwhile. Distinguish a decision to repeat the unchanged offer from a decision to test a redesign. No conclusion about the real public programme follows from the fictional arithmetic.

## Sources and case assumptions

All information needed for classroom decisions is in the case. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

The café, all prices, costs, redemptions, spare capacity, pilot and 700/300 counterfactual split are invented. The pilot estimate is accepted as exact only for the classroom calculation; real estimates have uncertainty. The discount is entirely merchant-funded, unlike the public programme used as the news hook. No ineffective marketing or cannibalization is alleged about any named organization. Contribution excludes unchanged overheads. Artwork depicts fictional people and a fictional café.

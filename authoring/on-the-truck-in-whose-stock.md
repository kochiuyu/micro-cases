# On the truck. In whose year-end stock?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 265 -->

ACC-23 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Inventory cutoff · Transfer of control · Evidence reconciliation

![A fictional controller and warehouse colleague compare plain documents beside sealed cartons at a loading bay.](../on-the-truck-in-whose-stock-scene.jpg)

## A count is not the whole reporting boundary

Accounting guidance distinguishes control of goods from their physical location. Audit guidance also calls attention to movements around the reporting cutoff and inventory held elsewhere. A signed count sheet therefore answers an important question, but not every question.

The wholesaler and all transactions below are invented. This is a simplified year-end reconciliation, not a report of misconduct or a complete compliance assessment.

## Opening decision

You are the controller of a fictional Hong Kong stationery wholesaler closing its year on 31 December. A verified midnight warehouse count contains HK$60,000 of company-controlled goods. None of lots A–D is in that count, and no lot is counted twice. All amounts are whole-lot values.

The business recognises a goods sale when the customer obtains control, and records that lot’s carrying cost as cost of sales in the same period. It includes goods it controls in inventory even when held elsewhere. A purchase and payable are recorded when the wholesaler obtains control under the supplied terms. Do not assume that an invoice, dispatch or arrival alone settles control.

Apart from A–D, reviewed current-year revenue is HK$200,000 and cost of sales is HK$120,000. These baseline totals exclude all four lots. Their costs already include everything needed; no tax, currency, financing, damage, other returns or extra performance obligation affects this exercise.

An assistant’s draft uses HK$60,000 inventory and treats all three outbound lots as sales: revenue HK$239,000 and cost of sales HK$143,000. It omits A’s purchase and payable because A has not arrived. Only movement records are currently available; contract and acceptance evidence can be checked tomorrow, before approval. This is an internal close timetable, not a reason to invent certainty.

## Evidence

| Lot | Movement | Whole-lot HK$ |
|---|---|---|
| A inbound | Dispatched 30 Dec; arrives 2 Jan | Cost 12,000 |
| B outbound | Dispatched 30 Dec; arrives 2 Jan | Cost 10,000; sale 18,000 |
| C outbound | Customer carrier collects 31 Dec | Cost 7,000; sale 12,000 |
| D trial | At customer 30 Dec; acceptance unresolved | Cost 6,000; proposed sale 9,000 |

**Student question:** Would you approve the dispatch-based draft, approve only the physical count as the inventory balance, or keep the close provisional? Identify the evidence needed for each shipment.

## Round two

Before the report is approved, you obtain the following verified evidence about conditions at midnight on 31 December. It resolves the initial uncertainty; the January documents confirm the specified timing rather than making every shipment a January transaction.

A: verified contract and carrier receipt establish that control passed to the wholesaler when the supplier handed these identified goods to the carrier on 30 December. The purchase is unpaid; the payable also arises then.

B: verified contract and delivery confirmation establish that control passed to the customer only on arrival on 2 January. The wholesaler retained control throughout the journey.

C: verified contract, customer instructions and collection receipt establish that control passed to the customer on carrier collection on 31 December. No remaining promise or acceptance condition exists.

D: this was a genuine trial, with no commitment to pay until substantive customer acceptance. Compliance could not be objectively established beforehand. A dated customer message confirms rejection on 31 December, before any acceptance. The goods were returned on 2 January. Control never left the wholesaler; the goods remain undamaged and recoverable at cost.

**Student question:** Using the verified control terms, what inventory, revenue and cost of sales should the year-end report show? Explain each shipment and the difference from the draft.

## First decision: reconcile before signing

Keep the close provisional while obtaining the available evidence. The count is reliable for its stated location but does not establish the complete inventory balance. Request the actual contracts and amendments, carrier handover and delivery records, customer acceptance or rejection evidence, and third-party quantity/condition confirmation where relevant. Match lot identities and dates; do not just collect more invoices.

## Classify each lot

| Lot | 31 Dec inventory, HK$ | Current revenue / cost of sales, HK$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 12,000 | Not a customer sale |
| B | 10,000 | 0 / 0 |
| C | 0 | 12,000 / 7,000 |
| D | 6,000 | 0 / 0 |

A is our purchase in transit, not our revenue. B is our stock travelling to a customer. C is a completed sale and no longer our inventory. D is our stock returning from a trial that never became a sale. Its treatment is not a general rule that every return reverses all prior revenue; the stipulated substantive acceptance condition matters.

## Build the year-end bridge

Inventory: HK$60,000 + HK$12,000 + HK$10,000 + HK$6,000 = **HK$88,000**. A’s unpaid purchase also adds an HK$12,000 payable, with no immediate expense or cash payment.

Revenue: HK$200,000 + HK$12,000 = **HK$212,000**. Cost of sales: HK$120,000 + HK$7,000 = **HK$127,000**. Gross profit: HK$212,000 − HK$127,000 = **HK$85,000**. Other expenses are not supplied, so this is not net profit.

## Explain the corrections without double counting

Against the draft, remove B and D revenue: HK$18,000 + HK$9,000 = HK$27,000. Reverse their combined HK$16,000 cost of sales into inventory. Add A’s HK$12,000 purchase in transit and payable. C stays recorded. Inventory increases HK$28,000; gross profit decreases HK$11,000 from HK$96,000 to HK$85,000.

Do not expense A simply because a payable is recorded. Do not add C back because it is still travelling. The warehouse count remains HK$60,000: the report reconciles that physical observation with controlled goods elsewhere, rather than rewriting the count.

## Control is not a shipping slogan

Real conclusions require the complete arrangement and facts. Possession, title, payment rights, risks and acceptance may point in different directions. An acceptance signature can sometimes be only a formality; here D’s trial is expressly substantive. The case supplies resolved control points, not a universal interpretation of delivery terms.

## Instructor note

Allow 3 minutes for the initial evidence decision, 5 for the four-lot classification and 5 for the reporting bridge. Ask students to draw separate columns for location and control. Then trace revenue, cost of sales, inventory and A’s payable without double counting. Accept a provisional schedule explicitly marked unresolved before the reveal, not unsupported final balances. No fraud or audit opinion can be inferred from this draft error. This exercise does not assess every accounting or audit requirement.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

All transactions, dates, contracts, amounts and verified evidence are original fiction. Costs and revenue baselines exclude the listed lots; no duplicate count. Specific control points are supplied assumptions, not conclusions inferred merely from shipping labels. Optional sources support concepts and evidence practice; US and New Zealand material is not a statement of Hong Kong legal requirements.

- [XRB · NZ IFRS 15, paragraphs 31–38 and B83–B86](https://standards.xrb.govt.nz/standards-navigator/nz-ifrs-15/). Official equivalent-standard text: control, point-in-time indicators, substantive acceptance versus formality. Supports the conceptual distinction, not the invented contractual conclusions.
- [IFRS Foundation · IAS 2 Inventories overview](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-2-inventories/). Official overview describes cost measurement and expensing inventory when related revenue is recognised. Fictional costs are supplied; no valuation uncertainty is modelled.
- [XRB · ISA (NZ)501, paragraphs4–8 and A4](https://standards.xrb.govt.nz/standards-navigator/isa-nz-501/). Official audit standard addresses count records, inventory elsewhere and movement around cutoff. Used for evidence planning, not a complete local audit programme.
- [ACCA · Audit evidence: when is enough enough? · April 2024](https://www.accaglobal.com/gb/en/technical-activities/uk-tech/in-practice-ezine-archive/in-practice-archive-2024/April/Audit-evidence-when-is-enough-enough.html). Professional explanation distinguishes sufficient evidence from relevant, reliable evidence and illustrates different count-testing directions. No assertion that a single document proves everything.
- [RevenueHub · Determining the Transfer of Control · March 2016](https://www.revenuehub.org/article/determining-the-transfer-of-control). Educational ASC606 discussion cautions that shipping terms require contextual assessment and distinguishes customer acceptance. US guidance used comparatively, not as Hong Kong authority.

# Strong inside. Disconnected outside.

## A strong team still needs outside connections

A team can coordinate its own work well yet miss information held by neighbouring teams. Boundary-spanning research examines how teams obtain information, coordinate dependencies and represent their work beyond their membership. More contact is not automatically better: it also consumes attention. The company and records below are fictional.

## Opening decision

You lead six product staff at fictional Singapore booking-software company Cedar Route. Sales and customer support are separate teams serving the same business clients. Allocate a four-week improvement allowance between extra internal coordination and structured liaison with those two teams. Normal development, testing and required approval routines continue under either option.

The allowance is 12 pooled paid person-hours across all participating teams, not 12 hours per person. Preparation, meetings, recording and follow-up all count. Existing work has funded cover; there is no overtime or unpaid participation. Both packages fit, but their internal and boundary activities cannot both be maximised within this allowance.

A uses eight hours improving internal task alignment and four hours on cross-team liaison. B uses four hours internally and eight on liaison. Internal hours include preparation and follow-up. The existing internal planning routine is separately funded and unchanged; B reduces extra improvement time, not essential coordination or release checks.

A funds two half-hour meetings attended by one product, one sales and one support representative: three person-hours, plus one pooled hour for preparation and records. B funds four such meetings: six person-hours, plus two pooled hours for preparation, records and follow-up. These are complete four-week allocations, not weekly budgets.

Both packages use an authorised shared change log, dated evidence and an existing urgent escalation route. Representatives may clarify needs, dependencies and constraints; only the product manager may approve scope changes. Neither sales nor a liaison can promise a delivery date or waive testing. No new tool or access permission is needed.

The latest dashboard shows all six scheduled internal milestones completed on time. The team prefers A because its working relationships are strong and another internal review feels productive. You can examine last cycle’s cross-team change records before committing. Timely milestones alone do not establish that the specification remained appropriate.

## Round two

Last cycle, on working day 4, sales recorded a pilot client’s changed operating need: two sequential booking approvers rather than one. On day 5, support confirmed the same workflow in an authorised demonstration using fictional sample records. This was a documented change request, not an approved commitment or a legal, safety or security requirement.

The note remained in the sales/support log. Nobody owned bringing changes into product planning or returning a decision. Product froze its one-approver demonstration on day 10 and met all six internal milestones. At the day-12 pilot review, the client could not demonstrate its intended workflow. No live release or contractual deadline was missed.

The product team had permission to see the note but had not reviewed it. Sales and support had not received product’s scope constraints. Nobody deliberately withheld information. The failure concerns one documented episode; it does not estimate the frequency of missed changes or prove that B would have prevented this one.

For the next cycle, all three teams accept the proposed liaison schedule and named representatives. The same product manager remains available through the existing approval route. Implementation effort for a two-approver feature is unknown. Your decision funds a communication process, not the feature, a promised release or an immediate resolution of the request.

**Student question:** Keep or change your allocation. Design a two-way route from the changed requirement to an authorised response, explain what must remain protected internally, and choose evidence for judging the four-week pilot.

## Debrief · Diagnose the boundary

B better targets the revealed gap while preserving ordinary internal coordination. The team delivered against its existing specification, but relevant information outside the team did not reach its decision process. Six of six timely milestones measure execution against that plan, not continuing suitability for the pilot client.

A is not inherently poor management: extra internal alignment can matter when internal dependencies are failing. Here, the supplied episode identifies a missing cross-team responsibility rather than a demonstrated shortage of internal meetings. B is a bounded pilot preference, not a universal rule to double external contact.

## Debrief · Reconcile the trade-off

A’s meetings consume 2 × 0.5 × 3 = 3 person-hours; preparation and records add one, making four liaison hours. B’s meetings consume 4 × 0.5 × 3 = 6, plus two for related work, making eight. Each total includes the neighbouring teams’ attendance.

B shifts four of the 12 hours from extra internal work to liaison; it does not create four free hours. Keep the separately funded baseline routines. If the scheduled work overruns, reprioritise within the allowance or seek approval for more capacity rather than silently adding unpaid follow-up.

## Debrief · Build a return path

Name the product representative as intake owner and a backup within existing roles. Before each meeting, sales and support flag changed needs with dates, affected workflow and evidence. Product checks the log against its current plan, clarifies uncertainties and records which requests need the manager’s decision.

Return product constraints and an explicit status to sales and support: accepted for assessment, approved, deferred or declined, with reasons and a next review date where relevant. Acknowledging a request is not approving it. Escalate urgent issues through the existing route between meetings; do not wait merely because the calendar is full.

## Debrief · Represent, do not overpromise

The liaison must carry information in both directions. Explain the client workflow to product and explain product’s capacity, testing needs and decision boundaries to the other teams. Ask the product manager to resolve priority conflicts. Neither a louder stakeholder nor a meeting vote automatically sets the roadmap.

Use only authorised work information and necessary summaries, not private employee traits or identifiable customer records. The shared log and backup reduce dependence on one well-connected person. Representatives support the team; they do not gain authority to bypass access controls, testing or scope approval.

## Debrief · Test usefulness, not busyness

For every logged change in the four-week pilot, record when it was received, reviewed and given an authorised response, plus unresolved items at the end. Check whether sales and support received the response and whether product understood the workflow. Count actual pooled time and monitor ordinary internal milestones too.

More meetings or more discovered requests are not failures by themselves, and fewer logged requests do not prove fewer problems. Review missed or late transfers and reasons. A small before-and-after pilot cannot isolate causality from changes in workload or requirement complexity. No revenue gain, avoided cost or guaranteed feature delivery is supplied.

## Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading.

Original fictional company, records, client workflow, packages and figures. Twelve paid pooled person-hours across all participants for four weeks, with funded cover. A: eight internal plus four liaison; B: four internal plus eight liaison. Liaison totals include three attendees per half-hour meeting and all related preparation/recording/follow-up. Baseline work and approval capacity are separately funded and unchanged. Six internal milestones were met against an unchanged specification; one client request did not reach product before its freeze. This is a diagnostic episode, not a failure-rate estimate or causal comparison. Two-approver implementation effort, financial benefit and pilot effectiveness are unknown. No contract, safety or legal requirement is traded away. No real people, customer data or employee assessments are involved. Sources accessed 17 November 2026; research and guidance from other settings provide bounded concepts, not validation of these fictional allocations.

- [Ancona and Caldwell · Bridging the Boundary · Administrative Science Quarterly · 1992](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Group_Dynamics/Ancona_Caldwell_1992_Bridging_the_boundary.pdf). Original product-team research distinguishes external representation, task coordination and information scouting. The case uses these concepts without importing a performance effect or treating observational associations as a guaranteed intervention result.
- [Hu, Hinds, Valentine and Bernstein · A Distance Matters Paradox · 2022 author version](https://arxiv.org/html/2202.02484). Original ten-month ethnography distinguishes effective within-team collaboration from difficulties between teams. Its remote laboratory setting is not this fictional Singapore company, and its observations do not establish that this liaison schedule causes improvement.
- [Lee, Horth and Ernst · Boundary Spanning in Action · Center for Creative Leadership · 2014](https://www.ccl.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/boundary-spanning-tactics-research-paper-center-for-creative-leadership.pdf). Primary practitioner white paper discusses horizontal boundaries, clarifying responsibilities and exchanging perspectives across groups. Its practice illustrations are not a controlled evaluation or a mandate to remove useful boundaries.
- [UK Government Service Manual · Plan user research for your service · updated 15 October 2026](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/plan-user-research-for-your-service). Primary service-design guidance connects findings to planning and prioritisation and recommends sharing with wider stakeholders. It supports a decision-linked information route, not a claim that sales reports replace user research or that UK guidance governs this company.
- [Schwaber and Sutherland · The Scrum Guide · November 2020](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html). The authors’ framework distinguishes internal planning from stakeholder review of results and changed circumstances. This case borrows the distinction only; it neither claims full Scrum implementation nor substitutes its fictional liaison schedule for Scrum’s specified events.

## Tax paid and tax expense are not always equal

Current tax and deferred tax answer different questions. Current tax reflects taxable profit for the period; deferred tax recognises specified future tax consequences of differences between accounting amounts and tax bases. The invented tax system below isolates accelerated deductions and their reversal. It is not a description of Hong Kong tax rates or a tax-planning recommendation.

## Opening decision

You review fictional Harbour Parts’ year-one accounts. All amounts are thousands of invented currency units, written CU000. At the start of year one, the manufacturer buys and immediately uses a machine costing 90. Accounting and tax values both start at 90. Accounting depreciation is 30 annually for three years, with no residual value.

The enacted tax rate is 20% throughout the machine’s life. Permitted tax depreciation is 60 in year one, 15 in year two and 15 in year three. Accounting depreciation is added back in calculating taxable profit; the permitted tax deduction is then subtracted. These deductions are lawful, settled assumptions, not disputed claims.

Annual profit before this machine’s depreciation and income tax is 100 for all three years. All output is sold in its year of production, so depreciation is fully expensed, not held in inventory. There are no other accounting–tax differences, losses, credits, rate changes, revaluations, impairments or recognition exceptions. All tax effects belong in profit or loss.

A draft year-one report equates total tax expense with current tax paid. The accountant proposes recording current tax plus the change in the deferred-tax liability. Current tax for each year is paid in that same year, with no opening or closing current-tax balance. Deferred tax starts at zero; the company recovers the machine through use.

For this model, tax base means the machine’s remaining deductible cost. Closing deferred-tax liability equals 20% × (carrying amount − tax base). Deferred-tax expense is closing liability minus opening liability; a negative movement is a credit. Total tax expense equals current tax plus that movement.

## Round two

Year two now ends exactly as planned: profit before depreciation and tax is 100, accounting depreciation is 30 and the permitted tax deduction is 15. The 20% enacted rate remains unchanged. No new machine is bought and no asset is sold. The year-one deferred-tax balance has been properly recorded.

The finance manager notices that current tax paid is higher in year two than in year one, despite identical accounting profit before tax. They propose adding the closing deferred-tax liability to current tax to obtain year-two tax expense. The accountant says to use the movement in that liability, including its direction, instead.

For this exercise, year three also occurs exactly as scheduled. The machine is fully used up at year-end, with zero accounting residual value and no remaining tax deduction. All three years are profitable and recoverability through use is established. Do not introduce disposal proceeds, new deductions or a second payment of the deferred-tax balance.

**Student question:** Reconcile year two’s current tax, opening and closing deferred-tax liability, and total tax expense. Then check year three and the three-year totals. Why is the reversal a tax-expense credit rather than a separate cash refund?

## Debrief · Start with two profit measures

All calculations remain in CU000. Year-one accounting profit before tax is 100 − 30 = 70. Taxable profit is 70 + 30 − 60 = 40. Current tax is 40 × 20% = 8, paid during the year. Paying that amount settles current tax; it does not establish total accounting tax expense.

The closing carrying amount is 90 − 30 = 60. The tax base is the remaining deductible cost: 90 − 60 = 30. The taxable temporary difference is 60 − 30 = 30. Applying 20% gives a closing deferred-tax liability of 6. It reflects earlier tax deductions, not an overdue tax bill.

## Debrief · Record the movement

The deferred-tax liability rises from zero to 6 in year one, creating deferred-tax expense of 6. Total tax expense is current tax 8 + deferred tax 6 = 14. Profit after tax is 70 − 14 = 56. Approve the accountant’s report, not the draft that treats cash paid as total tax expense.

The deferred entry debits tax expense 6 and credits deferred-tax liability 6. It does not pay another 6 in cash. The current-tax charge and its cash settlement are separate entries. Current tax equals cash paid here only because the timing assumption explicitly makes them equal.

## Debrief · Year two reverses part of the difference

Year-two accounting profit before tax is again 70. Taxable profit is 70 + 30 − 15 = 85, giving current tax paid of 17. Closing carrying amount is 90 − 60 = 30; tax base is 90 − 75 = 15. The remaining taxable temporary difference is 15 and the closing liability is 3.

The liability falls from 6 to 3. The movement is −3, a deferred-tax credit, so total year-two tax expense is 17 − 3 = 14. Adding the closing balance instead would give 20, confusing a balance with an expense. Profit after tax remains 56.

## Debrief · Complete the three-year check

Year three again has accounting profit 70, taxable profit 85 and current tax paid 17. Both carrying amount and tax base close at zero, so the deferred-tax liability falls from 3 to zero. A further deferred-tax credit of 3 leaves tax expense 14 and profit after tax 56.

Across three years, accounting depreciation and tax deductions each total 90. Accounting profit and taxable profit each total 210. Current taxes paid total 8 + 17 + 17 = 42. Total accounting tax expense is 14 + 14 + 14 = 42; deferred-tax movements sum to 6 − 3 − 3 = zero.

## Debrief · Timing is not exemption

The first year’s larger tax deduction brings tax relief forward. Later deductions are smaller than accounting depreciation, and the difference reverses. The liability’s reduction recognises this reversal in tax expense; it is not a cash refund. Later cash payments already include the tax consequences, so paying the deferred balance again would double count them.

This result does not mean every entity’s tax expense equals accounting profit multiplied by one rate. Permanent differences, different rates, losses, exceptions and items outside profit or loss can change the reconciliation. They are deliberately absent here. Lawful accelerated depreciation does not itself establish evasion or a permanent reduction in total tax.

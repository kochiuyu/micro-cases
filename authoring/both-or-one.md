# A package deal. Must everyone buy both?

## In the news

On 3 July 2025, Dorsett Mongkok promoted a room package allowing guests to choose three perks, including transport or dining options.

This historical company announcement motivates thinking about combined services and choice. It is not the same menu as our fictional studio, evidence of higher revenue or a claim about current hotel availability.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong craft studio offers brush lettering and paper folding in separate time slots. Each session is useful on its own, and customers can attend either or both. Quality is unchanged across menus, capacity is sufficient and existing staff and room costs are the same.

Materials cost HK$20 for each person attending each session: HK$40 for both. There is no bundle cost saving. For this exercise, there are no extra booking or menu costs, taxes, refunds or timing clashes, and no transfer, resale or repeat purchase of a session.

Start with exactly one Customer A and one Customer B. Their values below are invented maximum willingness to pay, not survey findings. Values already account for time and effort; budgets are sufficient. Each chooses the available purchase with the highest nonnegative value minus total price. Buying nothing gives zero.

**Separate sales:** HK$90 per session; both cost HK$180. **Package only:** HK$125 for both, with neither sold alone. The pair adds no extra value beyond the two session values.

Compare contribution: sales revenue less incremental materials cost, not the studio’s complete profit. Customers attend the sessions they choose. No ties arise at these prices.

**Student question:** Which menu would you provisionally offer to these two customers? Identify their choices and compare buyer benefit and studio contribution. What customer profile could change your preference?

## Round two

Before the menu is published, the booking coordinator adds Customer C, who values lettering at HK$100 and folding at zero. The exercise now contains exactly one A, one B and one C, not a forecast of customer shares.

The booking system can offer a third menu at no extra administration cost: keep either session available for HK$90 while also offering the pair for HK$125. This is mixed bundling. The prices, quality, materials cost, capacity and timing are otherwise unchanged; no hidden fee or automatic addition applies.

Compare all three candidate menus using these same three buyers. Each still chooses the highest value minus price, not simply any purchase with positive benefit.

**Student question:** For all three customers, would you offer separate sessions only, the package only or the mixed menu? Work out their actual choices and name one real-world cost or demand assumption you would test before launch.

## For the first two buyers, the package earns its place

With separate sales, A chooses lettering and B chooses folding. Each gets HK$100 − HK$90 = HK$10 of buyer benefit. Revenue is HK$180 and materials cost HK$40, leaving HK$140 contribution.

With the package only, both buy both sessions. Each gets HK$140 − HK$125 = HK$15. Revenue is HK$250, but four session-places use HK$80 of materials, leaving HK$170 contribution.

For this two-buyer model, contribution rises HK$30 and combined buyer benefit rises HK$10. Costs also rise; the package does not manufacture extra value or make materials cheaper.

## Recompare the menus with the same three customers

Separate only: A and C choose lettering; B chooses folding. Revenue HK$270 − materials HK$60 = contribution HK$210. Each buyer’s benefit is HK$10, totalling HK$30.

Package only: A and B choose the pair; C buys nothing because HK$100 − HK$125 is negative. Revenue HK$250 − materials HK$80 = contribution HK$170. Total buyer benefit is HK$30.

Mixed menu: A and B choose the pair; C chooses lettering. Revenue HK$340 − materials HK$100 = contribution HK$240. Buyer benefits are HK$15, HK$15 and HK$10, totalling HK$40.

Mixed gives the highest contribution among these three candidates under the stated assumptions. Do not compare the initial two-buyer total with the later three-buyer total and attribute the difference to the menu.

## Predict the choice, not just whether a price is affordable

In the mixed menu, A and B prefer the pair’s HK$15 benefit to their favourite single session’s HK$10. C prefers lettering’s HK$10 to the package’s −HK$25 or buying nothing.

The standalone option lets C participate without taking away A and B’s preferred packages. A buyer who can afford both does not automatically choose the largest package: the available alternatives matter.

## Keep the model’s success within its limits

These are three candidate menus, not proof of the best possible prices. Different values, materials costs, limited session capacity or extra administration could change the result. Exact willingness to pay is supplied here for teaching, not normally visible to a studio.

Keep standalone options genuinely available and explain the total price, included sessions, schedules and conditions. This example does not establish that all bundles benefit all consumers or resolve competition-law questions.

## Instructor note

Take the first vote with only A and B visible, then add C and the feasible mixed menu. Require the highest-benefit purchase for each buyer before adding revenue. The service values are additive: no extra complementary-use value has been assumed.

After the reveal, every comparison uses the same three buyers. Mixed contribution exceeds separate by HK$30 and package-only by HK$70 at these prices; these are not estimates for the Hong Kong market. Contribution excludes unchanged staff and room costs, so no complete profit level is supplied.

For an optional check, ask what happens if the mixed-menu pair costs HK$135 while singles remain HK$90: A and B would prefer their favourite single’s HK$10 benefit to the pair’s HK$5. Keep that as a changed scenario, not a revision to the main prices. Rival behaviour, industry welfare and legal assessment are outside this model.

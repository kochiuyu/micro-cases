# More to choose. Easier to choose?

Case 111 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Marketing · Choice architecture · Shopper tasks

![Two stationery-shop colleagues consider a tablet beside display pens and shelves of writing supplies.](../easier-to-choose-scene.jpg)

## In the news

On 31 August 2021, Hong Kong’s Consumer Council launched a revamped website with a Product Picker for comparing selected products’ test ratings and prices.

This historical comparison aid does not establish sales effects or endorse the fictional shop below.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. InkLane, a Hong Kong pen retailer, sells 18 refillable models differing in grip, nib width, weight and price. All 18 will stay in stock and available. The owner is choosing the catalogue’s default entrance for next month, not which products to remove.

Two prepared entrances cost the same to implement. Both give accurate descriptions, prices and refill compatibility, with identical product pages and checkout. Neither adds profiling, paid rankings or a recommendation engine.

The choice concerns where customers begin. Some arrive with a particular model in mind; others are still working out what suits their writing task. Their actual proportions are unknown.

## Evidence

| Entrance | First view | Access to the full range |
|---|---|---|
| A · Complete catalogue | All 18 models, consistent comparison fields and visible search. | Immediate; no extra entrance step. |
| B · Starting suggestions | Six clearly described suggestions covering contrasting preferences, plus visible search. | Prominent “View all 18 pens” control, one explicit click away. |

Once opened, B’s full catalogue is identical to A, including its ordering and information. A is a finite, organised page, not deliberately tiny or confusing. B does not claim the six are universally best or falsely hide availability.

Both fit the deadline. B may reduce initial comparison work but adds a layer for some specific-model searches. Neither is known to improve purchases or sales.

**Student question:** Which entrance would you choose, A or B? Name the customer task you are prioritising and one observation that would reverse your choice.

## Round two

An original fictional prototype session recruits 80 adult volunteers: 40 initially undecided about attributes for a supplied writing task, and 40 seeking a named model. Within each group, 20 are randomly assigned to each entrance. Each person uses only one.

For the undecided task, completion means choosing a candidate within 90 seconds and accurately explaining two supplied attributes relevant to the writing task. A researcher uses the same prespecified checklist. A: 8 of 20 complete; B: 15 of 20.

For the named-model task, completion means finding its product page within 90 seconds. A: 18 of 20 complete; B: 13 of 20. Targets are balanced identically across entrances, including the same mix inside and outside B’s six suggestions.

Devices, connection, instructions, information and time allowance are the same; no coaching is given and search works normally. This convenience sample compares two complete prototypes, not just the number of suggestions. There are no purchases or conversion estimates.

**Student question:** Would you keep or change your default entrance? Explain which task gains and which loses in this session, and what evidence about actual visitors would justify your decision.

## The task results point in different directions

B has seven more observed undecided-task completions: 15 rather than eight, with 20 people per entrance. A has five more named-model completions: 18 rather than 13.

Those differences describe this session. They do not establish population effects or prove that six suggestions are optimal. B changes both the starting presentation and navigation, so the test does not isolate a single mechanism.

## Keep the same stock, change the starting experience

B can give an undecided visitor a manageable place to start comparing writing attributes. A lets a visitor inspect or search the whole range immediately. Those are different starting needs, not permanent types of people.

All 18 models remain available at the same prices. This is not an argument to drop a niche pen, reduce quality or restrict a customer’s choice. Nor does “more choice” necessarily mean displaying everything first.

## Do not turn the research mix into the store’s forecast

B totals 28 completions and A 26 across the deliberately equal task groups. Adding the rows does not reveal the best entrance for the shop: actual task proportions are unknown and the two completion measures mean different things.

A failed 90-second task is not a lost sale. A successful explanation is not proof of enjoyment, purchase or repeat business. No revenue or conversion ranking follows from these counts.

## Choose a priority and make its cost visible

Choose B if helping undecided visitors is the explicit priority and actual-use evidence supports that need, while watching specific-model finding friction. Choose A if direct access is more important, while checking whether undecided visitors can compare confidently.

Keep task feedback voluntary. Measure finding, understanding, abandonment and completed purchases separately, rather than silently treating them as the same outcome. Do not introduce undisclosed tracking or compulsory surveys.

A visitor-selected entrance or improved search might be worth testing later. Neither is an already validated, cost-free third option. Make the requested next-month choice first.

## Instructor note

Before the reveal, require a task priority, not a universal slogan about more or fewer choices. Afterward ask students to identify the opposing within-task comparisons and defend their default.

Optional observed rates are 40% versus 75% for the undecided task and 90% versus 65% for named-model finding. They remain sample task results. Random assignment within this convenience sample does not make it representative or isolate suggestion-count effects.

The supplied study is invented, not a result from the cited papers. Preference uncertainty is a decision state, not an age, ability or intelligence label. This is catalogue-entry design, distinct from assortment deletion or demographic targeting.

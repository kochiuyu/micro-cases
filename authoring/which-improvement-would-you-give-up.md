# They want everything. What will they trade?

<!-- legacy-case-id: 195 -->

MKT-17 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Attribute trade-offs · Preference elicitation · Offer design

![Two meal-service colleagues consider plain proposal folders beside prepared meals and an insulated delivery bag.](../which-improvement-would-you-give-up-scene.jpg)

## Several priorities at the same time

On 20 March 2026, Hong Kong’s HKFYG Youth I.D.E.A.S. released research on prepared dishes and consumer expectations. Its summary discusses taste, price and transparency as concerns. This is a local reminder that food choices have several dimensions, not a meal-subscription demand forecast. All bundles, costs and customer responses below are original fiction.

## Opening decision

A fictional Hong Kong meal service is choosing one bundle for a small, funded next-stage test, not a full launch. Each weekly bundle includes five individual meals with the same portion sizes, food-safety controls, ingredient disclosures and dietary suitability. All deliveries follow safe paid working schedules; a shorter promised window comes from additional routing capacity, not pressure to drive faster. No safety or disclosure requirement is traded away.

Twenty invited prospective customers have each rated lower price, more menu choice and a narrower delivery window as important when asked separately. That response does not say which improvement they would sacrifice. The team can afford to test one of the three feasible bundles below. All prices include delivery, and every meal’s delivery window is stated in advance. Two or six menu options means the number of dishes from which the customer chooses each day, not the number of meals supplied.

For this test, incremental costs include meals, packing, delivery and other costs that vary with each order; common funded test costs are separate and equal. Each feasible bundle contributes HK$40 per fulfilled weekly order before those common costs. Offering six menu options and a 30-minute window at HK$200 would cost HK$240 per order, a HK$40 loss; no subsidy for that combination exists. No demand or retention forecast is supplied. Choose a provisional feasible bundle, then explain what the importance ratings still leave unknown.

## Evidence

|---|---:|---:|---|---:|
| A | HK$200 | 2 | 2 hours | HK$160 |
| B | HK$240 | 6 | 2 hours | HK$200 |
| C | HK$240 | 2 | 30 minutes | HK$200 |

**Student question:** Which feasible bundle would you take forward provisionally, and why? What trade-off must a customer accept, and why do the separate importance ratings not settle that choice?

## Round two

The same 20 invited prospects now see all three complete cards together, with an equally visible “none of these” alternative. Card positions are rotated, terms are explained consistently, and each person chooses privately. Eight select A, six select B, four select C and two select none. No money is paid and no order is placed; this is one illustrative choice task, not a fitted conjoint model or a representative survey.

In short follow-up explanations, A choosers say they accept fewer menus and a wider window to keep the lower weekly price. B choosers accept the wider window for more menus. C choosers accept fewer menus for the narrower window. The two choosing none prefer their existing arrangements. These explanations describe the selected individuals’ stated choices; they do not establish stable market segments, exact willingness to pay or causal feature effects.

The test budget still funds one bundle only, with no free simultaneous offer of A, B and C. Each bundle has the same HK$40 contribution under the supplied costs, so these counts cannot justify a claim that the higher-priced bundle earns more per order. The manager must choose which customer need to test next and how to check actual purchase and repeat use before wider rollout.

**Student question:** Would you keep your provisional bundle or change it after the complete-profile choices? State the customer trade-off you would test next and one claim these results cannot support.

## A wish list is not an offer

Separate importance ratings allow every improvement to sound desirable without its price or operating consequence. Complete bundles make the sacrifice explicit. A offers lower price; B adds menu variety at a higher price; C adds a narrower window at that same higher price. None combines every desired feature at the lowest price.

## Read a choice within its alternatives

Eight chose A, six B, four C and two none, totalling 20. A is the most selected of these cards in this invited group, not a majority of all potential buyers or a 40% market-share forecast. The opt-out matters because a preferred bundle is not necessarily better than a person’s existing arrangement. Changing profiles, prices or recruitment could change the result.

## No marginal value has been estimated

These are stated choices among complete offers. Do not divide the HK$40 price difference by menu additions and call it willingness to pay, or infer every C chooser values a narrower window at exactly HK$40. One task supplies no fitted utilities, confidence intervals or independent estimate of each attribute’s effect. It motivates better questions, not a claimed statistical conjoint study.

## Choose a learning priority

A is reasonable for testing an accessible-price proposition; B for variety-seeking customers; C for those seeking delivery predictability. State which need the funded test addresses. Collect actual opt-in purchase and repeat-use evidence on clear terms, verify fulfilled-order costs and delivery performance, and investigate why people decline. Do not count these 18 hypothetical selections as sales.

## Instructor note

Have students commit to a feasible bundle before showing the 8/6/4/2 split. Require them to name what their target customer gives up. The point is preference elicitation, not selecting the highest count by rule. All three contributions are HK$40; no margin advantage resolves the decision. This is an accessible conjoint-style demonstration, not a sufficient design for estimating conjoint parameters or population demand.

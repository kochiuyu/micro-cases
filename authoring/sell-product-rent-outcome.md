## A service model changes more than the invoice

Manufacturers offer different combinations of equipment, maintenance and use-based services. An availability promise is not the same as selling an asset, and neither guarantees a customer’s commercial success. The fictional producer below must decide which business it is prepared to run, not merely how frequently to send a bill.

## Opening decision

You lead fictional HarbourPrint, an established Hong Kong producer of small display-label printers for exhibition contractors. The same proven printer technology can support two proposed offers to one defined segment of occasional exhibition users. These printers produce decorative labels, not safety signs, medical labels or critical instructions.

A continues sale-and-service: the customer buys and owns a printer, then buys clearly scoped maintenance visits and repairs at disclosed charges. Existing warranty and other rights remain protected. The producer earns the sale margin and service margins; the customer organises when to request service and bears the disclosed ownership and idle-equipment risks.

B provides printing availability for a twenty-four-month contract. HarbourPrint retains ownership, installs the printer and charges only for verified productive printing hours at an agreed rate. There is no deposit, fixed minimum bill or guaranteed usage. Customers avoid the equipment purchase payment but must pay for actual billable use.

Both offers are technically and legally feasible in this fictional setting. HarbourPrint can fund equipment, staffing and all agreed support without jeopardising existing commitments. No new organisation, outside finance or unsafe maintenance shortcut is required. The decision concerns one prospective segment; existing customers’ contracts are unchanged.

Some target customers say lower upfront equipment spending would help them. These conversations are not orders, demand probabilities or proof of willingness to pay the proposed usage rate. The team has not yet compared ownership, maintenance responsibility, utilisation exposure and the precise meaning of B’s availability promise.

## Evidence

| Dimension | A: sale/service | B: availability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Owner | Customer | HarbourPrint |
| Main receipt | Sale and service charges | Productive-hour charges |
| Upfront equipment purchase | Customer pays | Customer does not |

**Student question:** Would you pursue B for this segment or retain A? Explain the proposed customer benefit, then identify which responsibilities and revenue risks must be mapped before calling B a better business model.

## Round two

B’s draft scope requires a functioning printer or equivalent safe replacement during agreed weekday service windows. HarbourPrint bears preventive maintenance, ordinary repair, replacement transport and the specified service-recovery costs. The customer provides agreed access, suitable operating conditions and authorised use. Those obligations are explicit; misuse and changed scope require separately agreed treatment.

A successful repair is not itself the customer outcome. For B, service performance is whether the agreed printing capability is available in the service window. Productive printing hours are separately metered with customer-visible records. Being available but unused earns nothing; no minimum revenue has been quietly added.

Two distinct stresses are possible. Exhibition bookings can fall while the printer remains available, reducing billable hours. Alternatively, repair needs can increase during agreed ordinary use, raising provider costs and threatening availability. No probability, cost distribution or correlation between these stresses is supplied. Both require analysis rather than a guaranteed recurring-revenue forecast.

Under A, the customer owns an idle printer and pays applicable disclosed maintenance charges; HarbourPrint retains its warranty and service duties. Under B, HarbourPrint carries the owned asset, service obligations and uncertainty about usage receipts. Customer event sales and profits are never guaranteed under either offer.

The approved funding covers a bounded evaluation and the stated service obligations, not unlimited future expansion. Resale value, customer uptake, usage, maintenance costs and prices are not numerically established. No spreadsheet can yet rank total provider profit, customer total cost or investment return. The decision is which model to develop and under what evidence gate.

**Student question:** Keep or change your model-development choice. Map who owns the asset, who pays when usage falls, and who bears ordinary repair costs. Specify evidence that would justify B and a stopping condition; explain why better customer cash timing does not prove provider profitability.

## Debrief · Reframe the value proposition

A supplies ownership plus separately purchased service. B supplies an agreed usable printing capability without the customer’s equipment purchase. This can suit occasional users who value avoiding ownership and arranging repairs. It does not establish cheaper total use, greater customer sales or actual acceptance of the quoted hourly rate.

An availability outcome and a payment trigger need not be identical. Here capability during agreed windows is the service promise; productive hours trigger receipts. The provider can fulfil availability perfectly and still earn little when the customer has few exhibitions. That utilisation exposure is part of B’s design, not an accounting accident.

## Debrief · Trace value capture and retained risk

Under A, ownership transfers and sale receipts occur at purchase, while future service earns separately priced margins. Under B, asset funding precedes uncertain usage receipts, and the producer retains maintenance and residual-value exposure. More regular invoicing does not make amounts predictable when the bill depends on actual use.

B can strengthen incentives to maintain reliability and reduce avoidable failures, since repairs consume provider resources and interruption can prevent billable use. Incentive alignment is not complete: customers still control aspects of use and access, and may have little demand. Clear duties, measurement and safe working conditions remain necessary.

## Debrief · Set a development gate

I would develop B as a bounded segment test, not replace A across the business. Validate paid willingness to use the offer, productive-hour patterns including quiet months, repair and replacement requirements, and residual asset value. Retain A as the available alternative if the evidence cannot support an adequately funded service promise.

Before any customer commitment, specify service windows, measurable availability, permitted use, response responsibilities, customer access duties and fair treatment of exceptions. Fund the required replacement and recovery process. Check that the usage rate and realistic hours can cover equipment, maintenance, support and capital needs; do not invent free repair capacity.

## Debrief · A model, not a financing trick

A financing change alone would alter payment timing for an otherwise unchanged purchase. B also changes ownership, what the customer buys, operating responsibilities and exposure to utilisation. Identical printer technology therefore does not imply identical economics or a universally superior subscription model.

Stop or redesign B if feasible usage receipts cannot support the promised service, if required maintenance cannot be delivered safely within scope, or if customers do not value the offer at a viable rate. A minimum fee could change the risk allocation, but it is a new proposition requiring customer validation, not free protection.

## Instructor note

Have students draw three flows: the asset, payments and maintenance responsibility. Then apply low use and high repair needs separately. A strong response distinguishes availability from customer profit and from billable hours. No numbers support a breakeven, expected profit, lifetime value or customer-savings calculation.

All firms, offers and obligations are fictional. This is an established producer’s business-model choice, not STR-29’s separate exploratory unit or FIN-06’s loan-payment comparison. Real manufacturer examples illustrate different service arrangements; they do not validate this fictional scope, transfer all risk or prescribe aircraft maintenance practices.

- [Böhm, Backhaus, Eggert and Cummins · Understanding outcome-based contracts · 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2055563616669740). Original empirical study distinguishes availability-based and economic-result-based outcomes and perceived risks. Survey findings do not establish causation, guaranteed returns or the value of this specific offer.
- [Irene Ng and Gerard Briscoe · Value, Variety and Viability · 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1211.5407). Original three-case research examines equipment-plus-service provision and the challenge of varied customer use. Qualitative propositions inform the evidence questions, not a universal business-model superiority claim.

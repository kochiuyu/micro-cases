# More days to return it, or less trouble?

Case 41 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Marketing · Return policy · Customer friction

![Two homewares colleagues discuss a cushion cover and return parcel at a round table beside fabric swatches.](../return-friction-scene.jpg)

## In the news

In a 23 September 2025 announcement, Taobao described returns and exchanges as part of its localised shopping experience, including cross-border returns for Hong Kong and Taiwan. The announcement was the company’s own promotional statement, not evidence that a particular return feature caused higher sales.

An earlier HKUST Li & Fung Supply Chain Institute digest, covering 28 February–6 March 2025, reported a Hong Kong return-shipping fee reduction for eligible Taobao purchases through Cainiao points. These historical examples show attention to the practical cost of returning, not a current offer or a universal free-return promise. Our shop below is fictional.

## Opening decision

Fictional classroom situation. You run a Hong Kong online homewares shop selling cushion covers. First-time buyers cannot inspect fabric colour and texture before delivery.

Your voluntary change-of-mind policy allows customers to initiate a return within 30 days of delivery for unused eligible items and receive the full item price back after inspection. They print a form, arrange postage and pay the return-delivery charge. All three options below preserve the same item eligibility, refund amount and inspection standards.

You can fund one improvement for the next campaign pilot. Each is feasible within the pilot budget, but they have different ongoing costs. You cannot implement all three now. No reliable estimate of extra purchases or total return costs is available yet.

This exercise concerns a voluntary change-of-mind offer, not remedies for defective goods. Existing customer rights and promises remain unchanged. Any new pilot terms are clearly disclosed before purchase and honoured for the orders covered.

## Evidence

| Option | What changes |
|---|---|
| A · More time | 60 days instead of 30; same postal process and customer-paid return postage |
| B · Less effort and cost | Keep 30 days; add staffed local drop-off, no printing or customer return-delivery charge |
| C · Better information | Keep current returns; improve photography, texture close-ups and descriptions |

B still requires a trip. Test location and opening-hour convenience; the shop pays handling and transport.

**Student question:** Which improvement would you test first: more return days, an easier return process, or better product information? What customer concern does your choice address?

## Round two

A review of the last 20 completed change-of-mind returns finds that 18 were initiated within seven days of delivery. Customers in this sample could assess the cushion covers’ colour and texture promptly.

Separately, staff interview 20 hesitant prospective buyers who have not ordered. Asked for their main concern, 14 name the postage cost, printing or trip needed to return an item; three name the deadline; three name other concerns. Each person gives one main answer.

These are small, descriptive convenience samples. Completed returns exclude customers deterred from buying or returning. Interview answers are not completed purchases, and neither group establishes what all customers will do.

**Student question:** Does the new evidence change your choice? What would you measure before extending the policy beyond the pilot?

## Match the promise to the concern

A doubles the stated window, but the available evidence points more strongly towards return cost and effort than lack of days. B is therefore a reasonable first hypothesis to test, not a proven winner.

B may remove printing and postage arrangements, yet a distant or inconvenient drop-off point could still be unattractive. Ask whether the proposed service actually reduces the journey and effort for the customers you hope to reach.

C could reduce uncertainty before purchase, though photos cannot fully reproduce touch or every screen’s colour. A could still suit customers who need more time; the small samples do not show that they never exist.

## Do not confuse reassurance with profit

Easier returns may encourage purchases and may also create more returns, transport, inspection and resale costs. A policy that sounds more generous is not automatically more profitable.

Measure completed purchases, reasons for hesitation, return uptake, customer experience, returned-item condition and total contribution after return-related costs. Do not judge the pilot on gross sales or the lowest return rate alone.

Keep prices, product mix and promotional exposure as comparable as practical when evaluating the change, and allow the promised return window to run before interpreting final costs. Preserve promises made to pilot customers even if future offers change.

## Read the samples honestly

18 of 20 completed returns tells you about people who successfully used the current process. It does not reveal the needs of every buyer or of people who abandoned the process.

The prospective-buyer interviews help expose a different perspective, but 14 stated concerns do not mean 14 guaranteed purchases under B. Check a broader range of customers and observe actual behaviour.

Key idea: a better return promise may remove friction, not simply add days.

## Instructor note

Use 2 minutes for the first vote, 3 for the two-sample reveal and 5–7 for the pilot design. Ask A advocates whose time needs the sample misses; ask B advocates whether drop-off is genuinely convenient and affordable; ask C advocates what information cannot reproduce. Keep existing rights separate from voluntary policy design. Do not make legal claims, accuse customers of abuse or treat stated intentions as sales.

# Many volunteers. A dependable service?

SUS-17 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Volunteer coordination · Service continuity · Supported participation

![Three community meal-service colleagues discuss a plain folder and notebook beside prepared supplies.](../cover-the-service-not-the-signup-count-scene.jpg)

## A local example of volunteer participation

The Census and Statistics Department records that ten members of its Social Club Volunteer Team joined Food Angel on 20 April 2024 to prepare meal boxes for low-income families. This documents participation in a particular activity, not a comparison of recruitment models or a reliability estimate. The organiser, people, tasks and costs below are original fiction.

## Opening decision

You coordinate Harbour Help, a fictional community meal-support programme. Its paid team has already committed to four Saturday services next month. Each Saturday needs two one-hour helper places in preparation, two in distribution and two in follow-up: six places per week, 24 over the month. These are supervised, nonclinical tasks such as arranging tables, counting sealed bags and logging anonymous stock totals. Paid qualified staff retain food handling, safeguarding, personal records and all safety responsibilities.

Choose an initial volunteer-support plan before orientation. Plan A welcomes 24 different people for one hour each; Plan B welcomes six people who each hope to attend one hour every Saturday. Both offer 24 person-hours, but individual time preferences have not yet been mapped. A costs HK$600 to organise; B costs HK$1,200 because it includes recurring check-ins and rota support. These are full incremental support and expected expense-reimbursement budgets, not payments for volunteer labour.

The objective is to fulfil the same service commitment with supported participation and the lower total incremental cost. Both plans are affordable. An existing paid backup arrangement can fill all 24 helper places if necessary at HK$100 per place used, including every employment cost; its reservation cost is already funded and equal under both plans. A contingency budget covers this worst case. No employee loses paid work. Volunteers can decline or withdraw without penalty; no service promise depends on compelling them to attend.

**Student question:** Which plan would you provisionally support, and what time-by-task evidence could change that choice?

## Round two

Before either plan is committed, confidential availability conversations are completed. Under A, six different volunteers each week can attend only the distribution hour. That task needs two people; extra people cannot safely or usefully substitute for preparation or follow-up at another time. There is no additional task in the agreed service scope. Under B, the six recurring volunteers have freely chosen different hours: two preparation, two distribution and two follow-up, every Saturday. All are appropriately screened and trained for their chosen roles; the support budgets remain unchanged.

The availability map is a statement of current intentions, not a guarantee or evidence that recurring volunteers are inherently more reliable. The paid backup arrangement covers both known gaps and unexpected withdrawals. A coordinator checks availability before each service, keeps an accessible cancellation route and activates paid cover for any unfilled place. Supervisory capacity, insurance, actual expenses and necessary accessibility adjustments are funded under either route. If a volunteer declines a different role, their choice is respected; optional opportunities outside this service require separate planning.

**Student question:** With the availability map, which plan would you fund, what is its planned total cost, and how will you protect the service if someone withdraws?

## Debrief: count usable coverage

A supplies only two useful places per Saturday: 8 across the month. Its other 16 offered hours overlap an already filled task; goodwill is real but cannot be counted twice as service coverage. The paid team fills 24 − 8 = 16 places. Planned incremental cost is HK$600 + 16 × HK$100 = HK$2,200. B covers all 24 places on the current map, so its planned cost is HK$1,200. B is HK$1,000 lower for this service, despite the higher support budget and smaller headcount.

## A rota is not a guarantee

The comparison uses stated availability, not estimated attendance probabilities. One unexpected unfilled place adds HK$100 under either plan; no claim is made about expected cancellations. B still needs a named coordinator, confirmation routine, funded backup and respectful withdrawal route. The whole service remains covered even if every volunteer withdraws. A one-off group can be valuable for a genuinely suitable task; this calculation does not rank people or declare recurring volunteering universally better.

## Instructor note

Begin by separating people, offered person-hours and usable task-time places. Ask students to defend A’s lower initial support cost before revealing the map. Then require the 8/16/24 reconciliation and a backup plan, not just “choose B.” A redesign may be discussed, but changing the service or volunteers’ freely stated availability is a new proposal, not a hidden assumption. Do not equate volunteering with free compulsory labour or import UK employment rules as Hong Kong legal advice.

ENT-40 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Escalation of commitment · Sunk costs · Evidence updating

Spent six months. Fund one more?

Persistence needs a forward-looking reason

Past effort can make abandoning a project feel wasteful. Escalation of commitment occurs when defending an earlier choice displaces a fresh assessment of what another investment can achieve. Continuing can still be sensible: compare future incremental costs, benefits and alternatives rather than treating either persistence or quitting as automatically virtuous.

Your classroom decision

Two adult founders of fictional Hong Kong scheduling venture Harbour Roster have spent six months and HK$300,000 developing a new product. The cash is irrecoverable under every option, and the completed code has no separate sale or reuse value in this exercise. One founder says stopping now would make those six months pointless.

They must choose C: fund one final month-long development cycle, or S: stop this product’s development now. C requires HK$60,000 of new cash and the founders’ available project time. It attempts a specified technical milestone: reliable conflict checking on the agreed test suite. No payment or additional cycle has been authorised.

If C achieves the milestone, its subsequent product opportunity has a supplied present value of HK$200,000 after all later development, delivery, selling and support costs. If it fails, that value is zero. The HK$60,000 cycle cost is not deducted in those outcome values and is paid whether the milestone succeeds or fails.

Under S, the founders can instead complete an available one-month consulting assignment with a certain HK$20,000 net present-value surplus after all its cash costs. The assignment and C need the same founder time and cannot both be done. If C is chosen, the assignment expires; it cannot be postponed or subcontracted.

Separate protected funds and a paid support team cover all existing customer, worker, supplier and administrative obligations under both options. Those obligations do not depend on the new product succeeding. Both options are affordable without those funds. Stopping this development is not immediate dissolution or permission to cancel support, withhold pay or transfer customer data.

The founders agree to maximise expected incremental monetary value for this exercise. Their initial assessed milestone-success probability is 60%. All amounts are expressed at today’s value, with no additional discounting required. Apart from the stated outcome and consulting surplus, no differing salvage, learning, reputation, tax, closure or future-opportunity value is assumed.

Round 2 · More information

An independent engineer tests the unresolved integration and provides a new, accepted planning assessment: only a 30% chance of meeting the milestone within the final cycle. Treat this probability as the exercise’s revised input, not a measured certainty. The cycle cost, conditional outcome values, available consulting assignment and protected obligations are unchanged.

The first founder argues, “After HK$300,000 and six months, we cannot stop now.” The second asks whether the next HK$60,000 still earns its place. No additional evidence supports the old 60% assessment, and no cheaper test, extended cycle or alternative financing arrangement is supplied as a third option.

Your decision

Keep or change the development decision under the accepted 30% assessment? Reconcile the cost of continuing with the consulting opportunity forgone. Explain what the past investment can teach without treating it as a recoverable benefit of spending more.

Debrief · Start at today

At 60%, C has expected outcome value 0.60 × HK$200,000 + 0.40 × HK$0 = HK$120,000. Subtract the new HK$60,000 cycle cost to obtain HK$60,000. S yields HK$20,000 from consulting. C initially has an expected advantage of HK$40,000.

Equivalently, C’s expected benefit of HK$120,000 exceeds its HK$60,000 cash cost plus HK$20,000 opportunity cost by HK$40,000. Do not subtract the consulting surplus twice: either compare the two alternatives directly or include the forgone surplus once in an incremental calculation.

Debrief · Revise with the evidence

At 30%, C’s expected outcome value falls to HK$60,000. After its HK$60,000 new cost, its expected net value is zero. S still provides HK$20,000. Stopping development and taking the consulting assignment is now better by HK$20,000 under the agreed expected-value criterion.

The new cycle merely breaks even against doing nothing, not against the available consulting work. Its success outcome would still be valuable, but the expected benefit no longer covers both the new cash cost and the alternative surplus forgone. No protected customer obligation is sacrificed by choosing S.

Debrief · Find the threshold

Let p be the milestone-success probability. C gives HK$200,000p − HK$60,000; S gives HK$20,000. Equality requires HK$200,000p = HK$80,000, so p = 40%. C is strictly better above 40%, ties at 40% and is worse below 40%, holding every other supplied assumption fixed.

The 60% initial input clears the threshold; the revised 30% does not. The recommendation changes because future prospects changed, not because the earlier expenditure disappeared. A genuinely improved forecast or lower remaining cost could justify another assessment, but simply repeating the old probability is not new evidence.

Debrief · Separate sunk spending from current resources

The HK$300,000 is already gone under C and S. Deducting it from both would shift both totals equally without changing their ranking. Adding it as a benefit that continuation will “recover” invents a cash inflow. The same is true of time already spent: those six months cannot be reassigned today.

Past work can still provide useful technical information. Use that information to improve the forward-looking forecast and document lessons. Assets with resale value, enforceable commitments or reusable code would require separate treatment; this case explicitly supplies none of those differing values. Historical cost and current opportunity cost are not interchangeable.

Debrief · Do not diagnose from the outcome

A bad eventual result does not prove that the initial 60% decision was irrational. Likewise, a lucky success after a poor decision would not validate reasoning based only on sunk expenditure. Judge the decision against the information, alternatives and criterion available when it is made.

Record the accepted probability, remaining cost, conditional value and 40% boundary before allocating further money. An independent technical check helps challenge a founder’s attachment, but is not infallible. Real founders may have risk preferences or other legitimate objectives; surface them explicitly rather than hiding them inside a claim that past spending must be justified.

Instructor note

Keep the protected support budget outside the discretionary comparison: it remains available and required in either route. Ask students to write both alternative values, then reproduce the difference using one opportunity-cost calculation. Finally ask whether the decision changes if the sunk amount doubles while all future facts stay fixed.

ENT-20 begins after closure is chosen and maps responsible fulfilment. Here the unresolved choice is whether another development cycle is worthwhile. STR-11 allocates development across existing product lines without supplied future returns; this exercise supplies probabilities and conditional values to isolate evidence updating and escalation of commitment.

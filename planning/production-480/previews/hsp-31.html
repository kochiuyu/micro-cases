HSP-31 · Hong Kong · fictional hotel · 15–20 minutes · Booking pace · Aligned lead times · Demand-calendar uncertainty

Fewer bookings today. Behind the booking curve?

Compare booking stages before changing price

Hotel revenue management distinguishes bookings already held for a future stay from the rooms eventually occupied. Cornell researchers examine reservation build-up and forecast error; hotel-system documentation separates new reservations from cancellations.

Commercial guidance from Lighthouse and SiteMinder also discusses booking timing and demand context. These sources explain the questions to ask, not demand at the fictional Hong Kong hotel below. No actual bookings or forecasts are reported in this case.

Your classroom decision

Mei manages a fictional 100-room Hong Kong hotel. It is 28 days before a particular Saturday stay. All rooms are the same type, and every booking in this exercise is for that single night. Last year’s comparison is the corresponding Saturday in the same seasonal period, with the same available capacity. The hotel has 40 rooms on the books, compared with last year’s final occupancy of 82. A colleague says: “We are 42 rooms behind. Discount now.”

The table separates the booking snapshots from final occupancy. On the books means reservations held at that checkpoint, net of cancellations recorded up to then. There were no amendments, no-shows or extra walk-ins in last year’s reconciliation. The hotel honoured every booking and will not overbook.

Choose one policy for the next seven days. Hold keeps the standard net room revenue at HK$1,000 per occupied room. Promote offers a clearly advertised HK$800 rate to new bookings for this Saturday, capped at 20 rooms, with a one-off HK$1,000 campaign cost. The variable servicing cost is HK$200 per occupied room under either policy. These net-revenue figures already exclude taxes and distribution charges; there are no other differential costs. Existing bookings and guest commitments are not changed. The two policies use the same cancellation terms.

Both policies end with a review at 21 days before the stay. The first-round evidence supplies no response estimate for either policy. Make a provisional choice, not a claim of optimal price. All records, prices and outcomes here are invented.

The stay date is unchanged. Thus last year’s 28-day snapshot was already after ticket release, while this year’s is before it. This may shift booking timing; it does not establish how many ticket buyers will need this hotel.

Align the clock

The apparent 42-room gap compares a work-in-progress snapshot with a finished stay. At the same 28-day lead time, this year is two rooms ahead: 40 versus 38. Both years added a net eight rooms between days 35 and 28. Last year finished at 38 + 50 − 6 = 82, so its later net pickup was 44, not 50.

Adding last year’s 44 to this year’s 40 gives 84 as a mechanical illustration. It is not a supported forecast: one historical Saturday, a changed event calendar and no uncertainty estimate are insufficient to promise that outcome. Even the two-room lead does not prove stronger underlying demand.

Align the demand calendar as well

Equal days before arrival are necessary but not always enough. The ticket-release milestones differ. Check the release actually happens, the relevance of the event to this hotel and whether inquiries become reservations. An inquiry is not an occupied room.

The reveal provides a plausible delayed-demand explanation. It does not eliminate the weak-demand scenario. Neither “we are 42 rooms behind” nor “the event will fill us” is warranted.

Cost the two policies without inventing certainty

Standard-rate contribution is HK$1,000 − HK$200 = HK$800 per occupied room; promotional contribution is HK$800 − HK$200 = HK$600 before campaign cost. Promotion yields 20 × HK$600 − HK$1,000 = HK$11,000 in either supplied scenario.

Holding yields 24 × HK$800 = HK$19,200 if demand is delayed, or 10 × HK$800 = HK$8,000 if demand is weak. Promotion is HK$3,000 better in the weak scenario but HK$8,200 worse in the delayed scenario. Those comparisons are conditional calculations, not expected returns. No probability is supplied.

Make waiting an active policy

A defensible recommendation is to hold until day 21: the aligned snapshots do not show a pace deficit, and the ticket release occurs within the review window. A manager prioritising protection against weak demand may instead choose the capped promotion, accepting its conditional upside cost. The case does not identify a uniquely optimal policy.

At day 21, compare new bookings, cancellations and net pickup over the same seven-day interval; inspect event-related inquiries separately from converted bookings. Check the event milestone and compare other relevant Saturdays or segments rather than blindly demanding last year’s final occupancy. Set the next rate using the updated evidence, remaining inventory and contribution. Do not invent a guaranteed sellout or a precise response threshold from this single observation.

Instructor note

Allow three minutes for the first vote, three to reconcile snapshots, four for the reveal and contribution calculations, and five for recommendations. Keep the event calendar and scenario table hidden until the first vote. Ask students to identify both the reading date and the stay date for every number.

Reward a bounded decision with a review plan. Occupancy alone is not contribution; gross new reservations are not net pickup; a historical endpoint is not a current forecast. This case concerns booking-stage alignment, not HSP-03 length-of-stay allocation or HSP-11 overbooking. No reservation may be displaced and no overselling is permitted.

  2. Pace Help Center: Pick-up Topic. Operational documentation distinguishes new, cancelled and net room-night activity, stay dates and update dates; supports like-stage reporting.
  3. SiteMinder: Booking lead time: Guide for hotels. Defines lead time and discusses its role in planning. No global average, supplier performance claim or country benchmark is imported.
  4. Blent: Pickup Pace report. Product documentation describes bookings and cancellations across days before a stay, and segment-level filtering. Not evidence for demand at this hotel.
  5. Lighthouse: The importance of pickup and pace in hotel revenue management. Explains pickup versus pace, comparable booking windows, and local-event context. Commercial guidance, not a causal study of ticket releases.

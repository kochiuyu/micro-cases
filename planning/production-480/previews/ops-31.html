OPS-31 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Loading feasibility · Non-stackable cargo · Capacity constraints

The truck is half empty. Is there room?

A payload number is not a loading plan

On 25 September 2025, Hong Kong’s Transport and Logistics Bureau answered a Legislative Council question about vehicle loads. Its reply described load-security enforcement and guidance on selecting suitable vehicles and placing loads safely. The reply does not establish the capacity of any particular truck.

The wholesaler, exhibition, vehicles, prices and quantities below are original fiction. The exercise is a simplified procurement comparison, not instructions for physically loading a vehicle.

Your classroom decision

You coordinate an exhibition delivery for a fictional Hong Kong household-goods wholesaler. All eight identical palletised displays must reach the same venue tomorrow morning. Each loaded pallet weighs 180 kg, including its pallet and packaging. The carrier offers A, one large truck, or B, two small trucks. Each vehicle can make only one trip before the agreed receiving window closes. A second trip or another vehicle is not included.

The quotations below include drivers, handling, securing, fuel and all other delivery charges. Both packages are available and have the same scheduled arrival window. Venue access and qualified handling arrangements are available for either vehicle size. No purchase has been made, and you can obtain the missing loading details before confirming. Payload here means the cargo allowance remaining after the vehicle, driver, equipment and other non-cargo weights are accounted for; it is not gross vehicle weight.

Round 2 · More information

The carrier returns the complete loading assessment. Each upright pallet needs a rectangular planning footprint of 1.20 m × 1.00 m and is 1.40 m high. The footprint includes the agreed handling and restraint allowance in this simplified model. These display units cannot support another pallet: stacking is prohibited. They cannot be tipped, dismantled or repacked for this delivery. No double-deck equipment is offered.

A has a clear usable planning floor of 4.00 m × 2.00 m and internal height of 3.00 m. Each B truck has 2.50 m × 2.00 m and internal height of 2.00 m. Each vehicle has a clear rear-door opening 1.80 m wide × 1.60 m high, so an individual upright pallet can pass through. There are no other intrusions in the stated planning rectangles.

The carrier has verified safe restraint, axle distribution, floor strength and handling for six pallets in A, or four pallets in each B truck. These checks are supplied assumptions, not consequences of the area calculation. Neither package may bypass them. Pallets must remain wholly within the vehicle and on one floor level. All first-round prices, weights, deadlines and commitments stay fixed.

Your decision

Which quoted package can carry all eight pallets in one trip per vehicle? Show a floor-space and weight check, and explain why spare tonnes or cubic metres cannot rescue the other package.

Debrief · Weight is only one capacity measure

The eight pallets weigh 8 × 180 = 1,440 kg. That is 48% of A’s 3,000 kg allowance. Four pallets in each small truck weigh 720 kg, below each 900 kg allowance. The initial weight comparison therefore does not rule out either package. A is HK$400 cheaper, but that is not yet a reason to confirm a load that has not been checked for fit.

Debrief · Count the floor that cannot be shared

Each non-stackable pallet needs 1.20 × 1.00 = 1.20 m². Eight need 9.60 m², exceeding A’s 4.00 × 2.00 = 8.00 m². Rotation cannot overcome this area deficit. Even seven need 8.40 m², so A cannot carry more than six. Six have a simple three-by-two arrangement: 3.60 m along the truck and 2.00 m across it.

Each B truck can take four: two rows of two use 2.40 m × 2.00 m, within 2.50 m × 2.00 m. The same four weigh 720 kg and stand 1.40 m high. The supplied door and safety checks are satisfied. Two such trucks carry all eight. Merely adding the two floor areas would not prove this; the explicit four-pallet arrangement does.

Debrief · Buy a feasible delivery, not unused space

Choose B at HK$2,200 among the quoted packages. Its HK$400 premium buys a feasible delivery of all eight, not greater weight capacity. A cannot fulfil the consignment even though the cargo’s planning volume, 8 × 1.20 × 1.00 × 1.40 = 13.44 m³, is only 56% of its 24.00 m³ internal planning volume.

The empty space above the displays is unusable because the units are not stackable and no supporting deck is offered. Volume below total truck volume is a necessary check, not proof of a packable arrangement. Nor does being below payload establish axle compliance or secure loading. A different vehicle or packaging proposal would need a fresh quote and assessment; it is not a free alternative in this comparison.

Instructor note

Use three minutes for a provisional vote and identifying missing information, five for the floor plans and weight checks, and five for interpretation. An initial preference for cheaper A is reasonable if explicitly conditional; either confident booking is premature without dimensions and stackability. After the reveal, do not manufacture equal support for an infeasible A.

Have learners draw four rectangles in each B truck and explain the area upper bound for A. The problem is packing feasibility, not route optimisation, freight-mode choice or loading practice. Mandatory safety provisions are never traded for the HK$400 saving. No carrier dimensions, rates or legal permission are inferred from this fictional example.

## Proposed briefs

## Accounting (ACC): cases 31–40

### ACC-31 · Half finished. How many units of work?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional Hong Kong skincare filler must value its month-end work-in-progress estimate using physical bottle count or equivalent units of conversion work. Materials enter at the start; labour and overhead accrue evenly.
- **Reveal:** Inspection revises the unfinished bottles' conversion completion from 70% to 40%, without changing their material content or physical count.
- **Learning objective:** Separate equivalent units for materials and conversion; reconcile completed and unfinished production costs under a supplied weighted-average policy.
- **Difference:** ACC-22 concerns absorption profit from inventory growth; this concerns measuring partially completed work, not incentives to overproduce.
- **Research leads, unverified:** OpenStax process costing; ACCA equivalent-unit guidance; a dated Asian manufacturer production disclosure. No claim about a real company’s completion estimates.

### ACC-32 · One catch. Two saleable products?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional seafood processor must decide whether to process one separated output further or sell it at the split-off point. Management presents a loss after allocated joint costs.
- **Reveal:** The optional further-processing route consumes a separately saleable co-product as an input; its foregone sale value was omitted. Show the physical flow explicitly, rather than suggesting a by-product disappears without cause.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish joint-cost allocation from incremental processing revenue and opportunity cost.
- **Difference:** ACC-21 compares avoidable product costs; this makes the joint-production boundary and split-off valuation explicit. ACC-01 uses a finished tray, not inseparable upstream costs.
- **Research leads, unverified:** ACCA joint-product costing; OpenStax sell-or-process-further treatment; FAO seafood processing context.

### ACC-33 · Sales rise. The break-even point moves?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional Kowloon stationery kiosk considers a sales target expressed only in total revenue for two products with different contribution margins.
- **Reveal:** A school contract changes the product mix without changing total forecast revenue.
- **Learning objective:** Calculate a weighted contribution margin and explain why break-even depends on mix, holding prices and fixed costs constant.
- **Difference:** ACC-12 optimises a bottleneck; this concerns the revenue needed to cover fixed cost with no capacity constraint. MKT-05 concerns basket complementarity rather than cost-volume-profit arithmetic.
- **Research leads, unverified:** ACCA multi-product CVP; OpenStax break-even analysis; HKTDC stationery-sector context.

### ACC-34 · A useful prototype. An asset already?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional Shenzhen design team asks its controller to capitalise the entire cost of a new sensor project. The case supplies a simplified recognition policy and dated evidence milestones.
- **Reveal:** Technical feasibility is established later than the project manager initially claimed, while commercial viability evidence was available earlier.
- **Learning objective:** Apply all supplied development-cost recognition conditions from the qualifying date; distinguish research spending from development recognition.
- **Difference:** ACC-07 identifies costs of acquired equipment; this examines uncertain internally developed intangible assets and recognition timing.
- **Research leads, unverified:** IFRS IAS 38 overview; HKICPA educational material; public Asian technology-company accounting-policy notes.

### ACC-35 · New information. Or an old mistake?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional repair-equipment business must classify a revised useful-life assessment under a supplied policy for estimate changes versus errors.
- **Reveal:** A maintenance report available at the prior reporting date was omitted from the records, unlike the genuinely new information initially described.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish prospective estimate updates from correction of prior-period errors using information availability, not desired profit.
- **Difference:** ACC-08 compares depreciation methods; this focuses on the evidentiary date and correction treatment. Do not present either treatment as discretionary earnings management.
- **Research leads, unverified:** IAS 8 official materials; HKICPA standards guidance; reputable accounting-firm worked examples.

### ACC-36 · After year-end. Evidence about which year?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional wholesaler prepares accounts after a major customer enters insolvency. Students decide whether the event informs the year-end receivable estimate or belongs only in later disclosure under an explicitly supplied policy.
- **Reveal:** In an explicitly labelled counterfactual comparison, the cause is a wholly new post-year-end disaster rather than pre-existing financial distress; the insolvency announcement date stays fixed. This is not presented as a retroactive change to established facts.
- **Learning objective:** Separate event timing from the timing of the underlying condition in adjusting and non-adjusting events.
- **Difference:** ACC-14 estimates credit losses; this isolates the subsequent-event reporting boundary, not the loss-rate calculation.
- **Research leads, unverified:** IAS 10 official overview; HKICPA guidance; public accounting examples. Use fictional parties and avoid implying a real borrower’s distress.

### ACC-37 · Cash tax paid. Tax expense matched?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional manufacturer compares accounting and tax depreciation under a simplified single-rate model. Students reconcile current tax payable with total tax expense.
- **Reveal:** The timing difference begins to reverse in the next year while the enacted tax rate stays fixed.
- **Learning objective:** Explain a deferred-tax liability as a timing difference, not a new cash payment or tax evasion.
- **Difference:** ACC-15 reconciles profit and operating cash; this isolates tax base versus carrying amount and reversal. No Hong Kong tax-rate claim is needed in the fictional model.
- **Research leads, unverified:** IAS 12 introductory materials; ACCA deferred-tax worked examples; HKICPA education.

### ACC-38 · Rent paid. No obligation on the statement?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional warehouse tenant uses a supplied simplified lease-accounting schedule to decide whether presenting only annual rent expense adequately describes its committed use of space.
- **Reveal:** Evidence about an optional renewal changes the supplied reasonable-certainty assessment. State the applicable rule explicitly; optionality alone does not determine whether the renewal belongs in the lease term.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish right-of-use assets, lease liabilities and cash rent, with clearly supplied term and discount assumptions.
- **Difference:** FIN-12 chooses lease versus buy economically; this concerns reporting an already chosen lease. Avoid making students infer complex legal enforceability.
- **Research leads, unverified:** IFRS 16 official education; HKICPA lease guidance; Asian retailer lease-policy disclosures.

### ACC-39 · Same sales total. A different variance?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional bakery controller explains a revenue shortfall by separating selling-price and sales-volume effects using an explicitly stated decomposition order.
- **Reveal:** The product mix shifts, requiring students to separate mix from total volume rather than blame the sales team for one aggregate gap.
- **Learning objective:** Reconcile a multi-product sales variance without double counting and distinguish arithmetic attribution from managerial causation.
- **Difference:** ACC-05 flexes operating costs and ACC-13 examines material cost-quality tradeoffs; this analyses sales price, mix and quantity.
- **Research leads, unverified:** ACCA sales mix and quantity variance; CIMA performance-analysis education; local bakery-market context.

### ACC-40 · A reassuring reply. Evidence from whom?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional audit team compares a customer balance confirmation returned directly through its controlled channel with a response forwarded by management.
- **Reveal:** The customer disputes one invoice but supplies a subsequent payment covering a different invoice; the total payment alone initially looked reassuring.
- **Learning objective:** Assess evidence provenance, exceptions and invoice-level corroboration rather than treating any positive cash receipt as confirmation of all receivables.
- **Difference:** ACC-17 chooses a sample for inventory evidence; this evaluates reliability of external confirmation and alternative procedures. No allegation against a real entity.
- **Research leads, unverified:** IAASB ISA 505; HKICPA audit education; public audit inspection lessons on confirmations.

## Finance (FIN): cases 31–40

### FIN-31 · A higher percentage. More value created?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional Hong Kong workshop chooses between mutually exclusive investments with different scale. One has a higher internal rate of return; the other a higher net present value at the supplied hurdle rate.
- **Reveal:** Available funding is reduced, making the larger indivisible project infeasible rather than merely less attractive.
- **Learning objective:** Separate return-rate ranking, value creation and feasibility; do not claim IRR or NPV overrides a binding funding constraint.
- **Difference:** FIN-08 compares payback with present value; FIN-19 allocates across several projects. This isolates IRR scale conflict for one mutually exclusive choice.
- **Research leads, unverified:** OpenStax finance investment-appraisal chapter; ACCA IRR limitations; HKICPA project-appraisal education.

### FIN-32 · More return on equity. More risk to equity?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional owner compares funding the same operating project entirely with equity or partly with fixed-interest debt. Supplied operating outcomes, borrowing cost and initial asset investment are identical across the comparison; taxes and transaction costs are excluded.
- **Reveal:** In a supplied downturn branch, operating earnings fall below the fixed interest charge even though they remain positive before financing.
- **Learning objective:** Calculate how leverage amplifies both upside return on owner equity and downside loss; distinguish an unchanged operating project from a changed claim structure.
- **Difference:** FIN-10 examines dilution through sequential equity funding; FIN-11 compares floating versus fixed rates. This holds the borrowing rate fixed and isolates financial leverage rather than inflation, real/nominal forecasting or interest-rate movements.
- **Research leads, unverified:** OpenStax financial leverage; ACCA capital-structure education; CFA Institute operating versus financial risk. No actual borrowing recommendation.

### FIN-33 · A business price. A price for the shares?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional founder receives an enterprise-value offer and must estimate the equity proceeds after specified debt and surplus cash adjustments.
- **Reveal:** A portion of apparent cash is restricted and cannot be transferred freely; the offer supplies its treatment explicitly.
- **Learning objective:** Bridge enterprise value to equity value without double counting debt or cash.
- **Difference:** FIN-29 distributes proceeds among share classes; this establishes the amount available to equity before that distribution.
- **Research leads, unverified:** CFA Institute valuation education; ACCA enterprise/equity value; public acquisition-announcement definitions. Do not generalise one contract’s cash definition.

### FIN-34 · A guaranteed loan. Whose future cash?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional business considers guaranteeing a supplier’s borrowing without paying cash today. Students map contingent cash calls under supplied scenarios.
- **Reveal:** The firm’s own weak-sales scenario coincides with the supplier’s inability to repay, making the guarantee hardest to meet precisely when internal cash falls.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish no initial payment from no financial exposure; analyse correlated contingent liquidity demands.
- **Difference:** FIN-16 compares income-stream covariance; this adds a contingent contractual cash call, not a second revenue stream. Signing is not part of the classroom task.
- **Research leads, unverified:** HKMA guarantee-risk education; bank guarantee explanatory material; ACCA contingent financing discussion.

### FIN-35 · A cheaper forward. A more flexible protection?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional importer with an uncertain order compares a fixed currency commitment with a purchased option under supplied payoffs and fees.
- **Reveal:** The customer cancels the order, leaving no foreign-currency purchase need but different contractual consequences for the two protections.
- **Learning objective:** Value flexibility when the underlying exposure may disappear; compare total scenario cash flows rather than only quoted rates.
- **Difference:** FIN-04 nets currency receipts and bills, while FIN-28 addresses basis risk; this isolates commitment versus asymmetric optional protection.
- **Research leads, unverified:** HKMA foreign-exchange risk education; bank hedging explainers; CME option education. This is not a recommendation to trade derivatives.

### FIN-36 · A cheaper machine. Over how many service years?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional scanning bureau compares two machines providing identical annual service but lasting three and five years. Using supplied discounted life-cycle costs and a common discount rate, compare equivalent annual costs under an explicitly repeatable, continuing-service assumption.
- **Reveal:** The bureau’s contracted need ends after three years and renewal is not available. Supplied year-three resale values replace the indefinite replacement assumption.
- **Learning objective:** Match unequal-life investment comparisons to a common service horizon; explain when annualising costs is useful and when the repeatability assumption fails.
- **Difference:** FIN-12 compares leasing with ownership and FIN-26 examines terminal-value dependence in a growing cash-flow forecast. This compares two owned assets of unequal lives at identical output, then changes the service horizon, with no financing choice or perpetual growth.
- **Research leads, unverified:** ACCA equivalent annual cost and replacement decisions; OpenStax capital budgeting; university engineering-economics replacement-chain teaching. All schedules and resale assumptions fictional.

### FIN-37 · One large payment. A predictable monthly reserve?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional wholesaler sets a monthly sinking-fund contribution for a known equipment replacement date using supplied interest and timing assumptions.
- **Reveal:** Contributions begin three months later, although the purchase date is unchanged.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish accumulated value of periodic deposits from multiplying a monthly saving by the number of months; expose timing effects transparently.
- **Difference:** FIN-30 examines balloon debt repayment risk; this designs a funded replacement reserve with no borrowing or refinancing assumption.
- **Research leads, unverified:** OpenStax annuity future value; IFEC savings-calculator explanations; ACCA time-value education.

### FIN-38 · A sales forecast. How much working capital?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional distributor estimates extra funding for growth using inventory days, receivable days and supplier-credit days under a supplied steady-state model.
- **Reveal:** A new channel pays faster but requires higher safety stock, producing opposing changes in working-capital needs.
- **Learning objective:** Connect operating-cycle assumptions to incremental funding, distinguish sales from cost bases, and state steady-state limitations.
- **Difference:** FIN-03 traces one order’s dated cash shortage; this models continuing growth and cash-conversion-cycle components.
- **Research leads, unverified:** ACCA working-capital cycle; OpenStax short-term finance; Hong Kong SME trade-finance context.

### FIN-39 · A reward for early repayment. After which fee?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional business considers repaying a fixed-rate loan early using surplus cash; compare avoided future interest with a stated settlement fee and lost deposit interest.
- **Reveal:** The fee declines sharply after the next scheduled reset date, but waiting entails one more interest period.
- **Learning objective:** Compare dated incremental cash flows of early settlement and waiting; principal repayment is not itself a cost saving.
- **Difference:** FIN-07 evaluates supplier payment discounts and FIN-06 compares amortisation schedules; this concerns termination of an existing debt contract.
- **Research leads, unverified:** IFEC loan-cost education; HKMA responsible borrowing materials; public lender settlement-term examples, clearly separated from fictional terms.

### FIN-40 · A bigger dividend. A smaller share of the same value?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional company compares a cash dividend and a share repurchase under an explicitly idealised no-tax/no-cost model at fair value.
- **Reveal:** Repurchase is proposed above the supplied fair value, redistributing value between selling and continuing holders even with the same total cash outflow.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish payout form from value creation and analyse price-dependent transfers in a transparent classroom model.
- **Difference:** FIN-20 tests distributable surplus and cash affordability; this assumes both authorised payouts are feasible and examines the allocation among holders.
- **Research leads, unverified:** OpenStax corporate payouts; CFA Institute repurchase education; HKEX rules only for verified context, not assumed eligibility.

## Marketing (MKT): cases 31–40

### MKT-31 · The same sales decline. Fewer buyers or fewer purchases?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional Hong Kong pantry brand must choose whether its next brief prioritises reaching non-buyers or making another purchase occasion useful. The supplied market panel distinguishes category buyers, brand buyers and purchases per brand buyer.
- **Reveal:** A stable aggregate sales total conceals falling brand penetration and rising purchase frequency among remaining buyers; management had interpreted the total as broad-based strength.
- **Learning objective:** Decompose brand sales into buyer penetration, purchase frequency and quantity per purchase over consistent periods; distinguish a descriptive growth source from proof of an effective intervention.
- **Difference:** MKT-13 models cohort value and conditional retention; this measures market-level buyer reach and purchase intensity without valuing retained customer cash flows. MKT-09 measures advertising reach, not actual brand buying.
- **Research leads, unverified:** Ehrenberg-Bass published buyer-penetration/frequency research; consumer-panel measurement methodology; OpenStax market-share metrics. All panel numbers and market-size assumptions fictional.

### MKT-32 · A generous referral. A trustworthy recommendation?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional homeware retailer chooses between equal-budget referral rewards paid to the advocate or shared with the referred buyer, with mandatory clear disclosure in both designs.
- **Reveal:** Interviews reveal recipients interpret the one-sided reward as serving the advocate more than themselves; the evidence is qualitative, not a sales forecast.
- **Learning objective:** Examine incentive alignment and social interpretation in referral design without treating hidden sponsorship as acceptable.
- **Difference:** MKT-03 concerns borrowed character attention; this concerns interpersonal recommendations and who benefits from an incentive.
- **Research leads, unverified:** Journal research on referral reward allocation; Hong Kong Customs endorsement guidance; published referral-program terms as context only.

### MKT-33 · A free trial. Enough time to experience the benefit?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional language-learning service compares seven-day and usage-based trials at the same expected support cost; the target benefit requires several completed sessions.
- **Reveal:** New users often spend the first week setting up rather than attending, so expiry precedes meaningful product experience.
- **Learning objective:** Match trial design to the product’s benefit-realisation cycle; distinguish access granted from value experienced.
- **Difference:** MKT-28 addresses perceived capability before purchase; this studies sampling duration and actual opportunity to evaluate. Do not use automatic paid conversion as a surprise.
- **Research leads, unverified:** Scholarly free-trial design research; consumer-protection guidance on subscription clarity; service-onboarding research.

### MKT-34 · The same message. Who must agree?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional GBA packaging supplier pitches one customer account whose purchasing, operations and finance staff have different approval criteria.
- **Reveal:** A technically satisfied operations manager cannot authorise the order; the finance gate requires a measurable payback explanation.
- **Learning objective:** Map an organisational buying centre and sequence evidence to distinct decision roles without treating the loudest contact as the sole buyer.
- **Difference:** MKT-07 separates an individual buyer from an individual user; MKT-26 formulates customer value. This makes the multi-person approval process the central constraint.
- **Research leads, unverified:** Webster and Wind organisational buying research; B2B buying-centre literature; HKTDC supplier-sales resources.

### MKT-35 · A new product. Added sales or moved sales?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional Hong Kong snack brand considers a premium variant using projected variant sales, margins and launch cost.
- **Reveal:** A supplied substitution study estimates how many purchases shift from its existing product versus competitors or previously unserved occasions.
- **Learning objective:** Evaluate incremental portfolio contribution rather than standalone launch sales, with uncertainty shown explicitly.
- **Difference:** MKT-05 protects complementary basket items; MKT-16 tests packaging effects. This focuses on within-brand cannibalisation in line extension.
- **Research leads, unverified:** New-product cannibalisation research; OpenStax marketing product-line concepts; public Asian FMCG launch reports as context, not causal estimates.

### MKT-36 · Recognised here. Understood there?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional Hong Kong skincare brand selects a name for a new Southeast Asian market: retain its familiar phonetic name or use a locally meaningful adaptation. Product, price and distributor remain fixed.
- **Reveal:** Structured local-language testing reveals an unintended association in one candidate and weak pronunciation recall in the other; neither creative team's intuition predicted both results.
- **Learning objective:** Separate cross-market recognition, pronunciation and meaning when adapting brand language; compare evidence from the target audience rather than stereotypes.
- **Difference:** MKT-08 concerns visual continuity of a pack in its existing market; STR international cases concern entry choices. This holds market entry fixed and tests the linguistic communication task.
- **Research leads, unverified:** Scholarly phonetic versus semantic brand-name translation research; cross-cultural consumer-language studies; HKTDC brand-localisation resources. Use invented names reviewed for actual unintended meanings before production.

### MKT-37 · A vivid testimonial. Representative of whom?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional tutoring service chooses between a clearly disclosed exceptional success story and a representative distribution of outcomes, with no promises of guaranteed results.
- **Reveal:** Parent testing shows the exceptional story is interpreted as the typical outcome despite a small qualification.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish literal truth from the overall inference an advertisement invites; redesign accurate communication without fabricating representativeness.
- **Difference:** MKT-11 makes an invisible service process tangible; this concerns typicality and outcome claims. Legal compliance is a boundary, not a tradeable option.
- **Research leads, unverified:** Consumer Council education-advertising material; Customs misleading-claims guidance; scholarly testimonial and base-rate research.

### MKT-38 · The customer left. Which offer deserves a return?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional fitness studio designs one win-back message for lapsed members after examining recorded cancellation reasons.
- **Reveal:** A large group left because the timetable changed, not because of price; a blanket discount cannot restore their feasible attendance window.
- **Learning objective:** Segment reactivation by defection cause and match the remedy to a solvable barrier, while respecting contact preferences.
- **Difference:** MKT-18 studies ongoing communication cadence; MKT-30 distinguishes shopping occasions. This addresses heterogeneous reasons for relationship exit.
- **Research leads, unverified:** Customer win-back and service defection research; direct-marketing consent guidance; retention-management education.

### MKT-39 · More stockists. More of the category market?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional Hong Kong sauce brand chooses between gaining many small stockists or fewer high-category-volume stores, under a fixed listing budget and equal in-store availability assumptions.
- **Reveal:** The many-store route increases the percentage of outlets carrying the brand, but the fewer-store route covers a larger share of category spending. The exhibit supplies each outlet group’s category sales, not the brand’s future sales.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish numeric distribution from category-weighted distribution; recognise that access to category spending does not guarantee capture of it.
- **Difference:** MKT-09 measures unique advertising audiences and MKT-21 rewards channels for recorded purchases. This concerns distribution coverage before sales, using store-count versus category-sales weights; no shopper-wayfinding or display redesign.
- **Research leads, unverified:** NielsenIQ and Kantar distribution-measurement definitions; scholarly retail-distribution/brand-performance research; HKTDC food-retail channel context. Category sales and listing costs are fictional, not a forecast derived from coverage alone.

### MKT-40 · A large community. Whose contribution sustains it?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional craft-tool brand must choose between recruiting more community members and supporting existing members who answer practical questions, within a fixed moderator budget.
- **Reveal:** A small group supplies most helpful responses and is becoming overloaded; raw membership growth would add unanswered requests.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish audience size from reciprocal community participation and design sustainable support without turning members into unpaid employees by assumption.
- **Difference:** MKT-09 counts unique advertising reach; MKT-18 examines message cadence. This concerns member-to-member contribution and community value production.
- **Research leads, unverified:** Brand-community research; online participation inequality studies; published community-management standards. All participation and workload exhibits must be fictional and labelled.
## OPS · Operations, supply chains and logistics

These are fictional editorial briefs, not researched accounts. All named research leads remain unverified and must be replaced if substantive accessible evidence cannot support the finished case.

### OPS-31 · The truck is half empty. Is there room?
- **Opening:** A Hong Kong household-goods wholesaler must book one large truck or two smaller trucks for a paid exhibition delivery. Choose using quoted payloads and charges.
- **Reveal:** Pallet dimensions, stacking restrictions and door clearance show that weight capacity is not the binding constraint; one apparently cheaper loading plan cannot carry the complete consignment.
- **Learning:** Translate multidimensional loading capacity into a feasible shipment, then compare costs only among feasible plans.
- **Novelty:** Unlike OPS-02's transport-mode/missing-component decision and OPS-14's route windows, this is physical packing feasibility, not speed or distance.
- **Research leads (unverified):** Hong Kong Transport Department vehicle loading guidance; GS1 pallet guidance; carrier published dimensional-freight explanations; logistics research on loading constraints. No unsafe overloading option.

### OPS-32 · First in. First to expire?
- **Opening:** A fictional Singapore pantry distributor chooses between date-of-receipt rotation and expiry-date rotation for sealed stock with equal handling cost.
- **Reveal:** A later delivery has earlier expiry and one customer's minimum remaining-shelf-life requirement differs from another's. Allocate the eligible lots before calculating avoidable write-offs.
- **Learning:** Distinguish FIFO from FEFO and treat customer eligibility and safety limits as hard constraints.
- **Novelty:** OPS-15 sorts returned goods; OPS-30 traces recall exposure. This concerns normal forward stock rotation under remaining-life requirements.
- **Research leads (unverified):** Singapore Food Agency stock-rotation guidance; WHO inventory guidance; GS1 date identification resources; peer-reviewed perishable inventory research. Use fictional non-medical pantry stock and never imply expired food may be sold.

### OPS-33 · A sample passed. Did the shipment pass?
- **Opening:** A Shenzhen accessory buyer must accept a lot under one of two pre-agreed sampling plans, with explicit rejection and reinspection procedures.
- **Reveal:** The sample meets one plan's acceptance number but not the other; the supplier has proposed choosing the plan after seeing the sample.
- **Learning:** Understand a precommitted acceptance-sampling rule, consumer/producer risk and why sample acceptance is not proof of zero defects.
- **Novelty:** OPS-10 concerns process stability; OPS-26 follows yield through stages. This is a lot-disposition sampling decision, not a control chart or stage quality calculation.
- **Research leads (unverified):** NIST engineering statistics handbook; ASQ acceptance sampling overview; accessible university quality-control material; published supplier-quality guidance. No safety-critical goods or unsupported legal assurance.

### OPS-34 · Equal work. A balanced line?
- **Opening:** A Hong Kong gift-pack workshop allocates six tasks among three stations to meet a specified cycle time, retaining all task precedence and checks.
- **Reveal:** A task pair cannot share a station because the fixture occupies the full bench; the attractive equal-minutes assignment is infeasible.
- **Learning:** Balance task workloads subject to precedence and equipment constraints rather than simply divide total minutes by workers.
- **Novelty:** OPS-03 buys capacity at an existing bottleneck; here the fixed people and equipment are given and the task-to-station assignment is the decision.
- **Research leads (unverified):** Open operations-management texts on assembly-line balancing; ILO work-study material; accessible industrial engineering teaching resources; manufacturing case studies. Teaching data must be explicitly synthetic.

### OPS-35 · Mostly zero. Forecast nothing?
- **Opening:** A small Macau equipment-service depot chooses a forecasting approach for an infrequently requested replacement bracket. A compact monthly history includes many zeros.
- **Reveal:** Nonzero order sizes and intervals vary separately; a simple last-month rule forecasts zero immediately before a booked maintenance period.
- **Learning:** Separate intermittent demand frequency from order size, and distinguish a forecast from a stocked quantity or service promise.
- **Novelty:** OPS-01 concerns censored sales and OPS-18 amplified orders. Here zeros are real observations and demand intermittency is the specific modelling issue.
- **Research leads (unverified):** Forecasting: Principles and Practice intermittent-demand discussion; accessible Croston-method research; manufacturer spare-parts planning reports; maintenance-demand case studies. Do not claim a small series validates a forecasting winner.

### OPS-36 · The project has time. Does the specialist?
- **Opening:** A Singapore fit-out coordinator accepts a timetable that satisfies all task precedences and a committed finish date.
- **Reveal:** Two parallel tasks require the same qualified technician at the same time; a paid second technician or a later noncritical task is available.
- **Learning:** Distinguish precedence feasibility from resource feasibility and use slack for resource levelling before buying extra capacity.
- **Novelty:** OPS-22 crashes paths with explicit dependencies. This keeps durations unchanged and resolves resource conflicts among otherwise feasible parallel tasks.

- **Reveal:** Later people copied visible choices despite contrary private signals; the apparent volume of evidence contains fewer independent observations than it seems. Reassess a recommendation when one independently verified quality observation arrives.
- **Learning objective:** Explain an informational cascade without equating popularity with independent information or assuming people are irrational.
- **Difference:** ECO-29 concerns passive defaults; IS sampling cases concern who was measured. Here the data-generating process is sequential imitation that suppresses private information.
- **Unverified research leads:** Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch cascade research; Banerjee herding model; public university game-theory notes; experimental cascade studies; consumer-review evidence as context, not proof of the fictional mechanism.

### ECO-39 · A larger rebate. A smaller reason to save?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional building cooperative chooses whether its fixed electricity-efficiency rebate depends on residents' past consumption or is a uniform amount. Households choose effort before the eligibility baseline is set. Show a transparent two-period example.
- **Reveal:** Households know the rule in advance and a consumption-based baseline rewards avoidable early usage. Compare a genuinely pre-existing fixed baseline or an independent benchmark, with household circumstances and fairness considered explicitly.
- **Learning objective:** Recognise baseline manipulation and the difference between a counterfactual benchmark and behaviour induced by the policy itself.
- **Difference:** SUS-21 concerns rebound after lower effective energy costs; ECO-10 concerns prevention under insurance. Here the incentive arises before treatment from how the reward's baseline is constructed. Unlike IS-38, households strategically alter measured behaviour in anticipation of a rule; no recommender exposure, model retraining or statistical feedback loop is involved.
- **Unverified research leads:** energy demand-response baseline research; IEA demand-side management; public utility rebate programme terms; mechanism-design papers on additionality; regulator guidance on measurement and verification.

### ECO-40 · The same expected payoff. The same choice?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional adult workshop club offers a guaranteed allocation of credits or a fair lottery with the same expected credit value. Credits buy only optional club activities, cannot be cashed out, and are not gambling products sold to participants. A supplied preference table permits students to compare expected utility as well as expected credits without claiming everyone has those preferences.
- **Reveal:** A second equally informed member has a different utility schedule and prefers the lottery; calculate each person's certainty equivalent and separate disagreement about preferences from misunderstanding probabilities. Neither choice affects essential needs or rights.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish expected monetary or credit value from expected utility and explain risk aversion, neutrality and risk preference through explicit hypothetical assumptions.
- **Difference:** FIN-01 concerns event-insurance terms and ECO-10 prevention incentives under insurance. This is a pure risk-preference comparison with identical known probabilities and no effort response, default, hidden information or financial-product recommendation. It does not duplicate HSP-25's additionality analysis.
- **Unverified research leads:** OpenStax or CORE risk-choice material; public university expected-utility teaching; original experimental risk-preference research; accessible certainty-equivalent explanations; research on limits of expected-utility models. Report theoretical assumptions separately from empirical claims.

## HSP · Hospitality, tourism, property and service management

### HSP-31 · Fewer bookings today. Behind the booking curve?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional Hong Kong hotel must choose whether to offer a transparent early-booking promotion for a future weekend. Its rooms-on-the-books are lower than last year's final occupancy. Show current lead time, the prior year's same-lead-time bookings, subsequent pickup and cancellations so students compare like booking stages.
- **Reveal:** An important event's ticket-release date has moved later this year; a supplied scenario shifts the timing of enquiries without proving that total demand has fallen. Compare a costed promotion now against preserving price for the specified booking window, with a clear review date.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish booking pace from final occupancy and interpret pickup using comparable lead times and demand calendars rather than panic-discounting from an unmatched snapshot.
- **Difference:** HSP-03 concerns allocation across lengths of stay and HSP-11 concerns overbooking from cancellation dependence. Here all existing reservations are honoured, no overbooking is allowed, and the issue is the time alignment of revenue-management evidence before a price decision. The business question is a future inventory's pickup curve, not retention of customer cohorts or transition from early adopters to mainstream users.
- **Unverified research leads:** Cornell hotel booking-curve research; Hong Kong Polytechnic revenue-management studies; hotel forecasting papers; public convention calendars; operator revenue-management documentation. Do not treat an announced event as guaranteed hotel demand.

### HSP-32 · Sell the room tonight. Keep it ready next month?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional hotel chooses the week for planned, non-emergency room refurbishment. All rooms remain safe and compliant until the scheduled work; no essential repairs may be deferred. Compare displaced room contribution, contractor access and future usable room nights.
- **Reveal:** A proposed low-occupancy window coincides with bookings whose stated purpose requires the affected shared facilities; another window has more room demand but preserves those commitments through an already costed alternative. Trace affected stays and facility access through each work phase rather than choose from occupancy or contractor mobilisation alone.
- **Learning objective:** Plan asset downtime around guest-service disruption rather than treat each closed room night as an isolated lost sale.
- **Difference:** HSP-22 concerns housekeeping workload; HSP-17 concerns handover after works. Here the decision is lifecycle renovation of a functioning guest property and the interruption of its promised shared services. Unlike OPS-04 batch/setup economics, the central exhibit must track rooms rendered unavailable by adjacent works, usable facilities and guest obligations over the renovation phases; repeat contractor mobilisation is secondary, not the decisive reveal by itself.
- **Unverified research leads:** hotel renovation planning research; RICS lifecycle and property-maintenance guidance; Cornell hospitality asset management; public hotel operator refurbishment reports; building-work guest-communication guidance.

### HSP-33 · An upgrade accepted. The right room left unsold?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional hotel's arriving guest can be offered a paid upgrade using an otherwise available premium room. Display the upgrade contribution, remaining demand scenarios and a protected equivalent room for the guest's existing booking. Decide whether to offer it now.
- **Reveal:** A later party can only be accommodated by that premium configuration, and its contribution depends on the remaining room mix. Reassess the opportunity cost without treating existing guest commitments as expendable.
- **Learning objective:** Evaluate upgrade revenue against room-type opportunity cost rather than view every incremental payment as free revenue.
- **Difference:** HSP-03 studies length-of-stay displacement; HSP-11 studies cancellation-dependent booking limits. This case holds total room inventory and confirmed bookings fixed and studies heterogeneous room types.
- **Unverified research leads:** Cornell hotel upgrade and revenue-management research; hotel room-type inventory studies; public revenue-management explainers; consumer guidance on promised accommodation; operator room-category descriptions.

### HSP-34 · A minimum spend. A banquet worth accepting?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional venue receives a small wedding request with a food-and-beverage minimum. Display food contribution, setup and room-reset needs, and the other confirmed uses of the same event space. Accept under the current package or offer another date with a clear price.
- **Reveal:** The minimum covers food revenue but not an unusually long exclusive setup period that blocks a separate profitable event. Compare the full period of exclusive use, not a deceptive post-booking surcharge.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish minimum revenue from the opportunity cost of event-space exclusivity over a multi-stage booking.
- **Difference:** HSP-28 schedules shared appointment resources; HSP-03 concerns overnight room allocation. This case links a banquet contract's revenue floor to access rights before and after the guest-facing event.
- **Unverified research leads:** Cornell function-space revenue management; convention venue booking terms; public Hong Kong venue hire schedules; event production planning guidance; hospitality contribution analysis.

### HSP-35 · The guest does the work. Is the service easier?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional serviced apartment can use self-check-in for routine arrivals or retain assisted check-in for all, with the same secure identity checks and an accessible assisted alternative. Compare staff work and the steps the guest must complete.
- **Reveal:** Some work has shifted to guests before travel and to a remote support team after failures. Display the complete task time and successful completion rate, not only lobby waiting time.
- **Learning objective:** Analyse customer participation as redistributed service work, not the disappearance of work.
- **Difference:** SUS-16 concerns inclusion in a public digital service; HSP-26 concerns cross-provider handoffs. This case evaluates the total guest-and-provider workload of a hospitality self-service design.
- **Unverified research leads:** hospitality self-service technology studies; service co-production research; Nielsen Norman Group service design; Hong Kong guesthouse guidance; hotel self-check-in accessibility studies.

### HSP-36 · The building is busy. The lease is paying?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional co-working property offers a choice of membership packages for a floor with meeting rooms and desks. Compare billed revenue, committed service access and peak simultaneous demand, keeping fire and occupancy limits fixed.
- **Reveal:** Members mostly need meeting rooms at the same few times, while desks remain available. A lower-headcount package may meet promised access more reliably; a cheap unlimited entitlement is not costless merely because average occupancy is low.
- **Learning objective:** Match property service entitlements to peak shared-amenity capacity and truthful membership promises.
- **Difference:** HSP-18 concerns tenant compatibility; ECO-14 concerns usage pricing. This case centres on operationally deliverable property memberships and mismatched amenity bottlenecks.
- **Unverified research leads:** RICS flexible-workspace research; public co-working membership terms; workplace occupancy studies; facilities-management guidance; consumer contract transparency materials.

### HSP-37 · A convenient meeting. An exhausting programme?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional conference organiser chooses a compact one-day timetable or a longer programme with breaks and fewer simultaneous tracks. Exhibit session times, transitions and participant objectives; keep accessible movement and essential rest needs protected.
- **Reveal:** Attendance counts hide participants arriving late to the specific sessions they value because transitions and session overruns cascade. Rebuild the schedule around completion of key participant journeys rather than the nominal number of talks.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish programme density from usable learning and networking opportunities under real transition time.
- **Difference:** HSP-09 designs networking encounters; HSP-27 sequences interpretation content. This case studies timetable propagation across a multi-track event with fixed participant commitments.
- **Unverified research leads:** event-design research; professional conference accessibility guidance; meeting-design studies; Hong Kong convention organiser manuals; public conference timetable examples.

### HSP-38 · A cleaner-looking lobby. A visible service interruption?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional hotel chooses between doing ordinary lobby polishing during quiet daytime hours with a clearly segregated safe area, or overnight with a different staffing cost. Both meet worker, guest safety and access requirements. Compare costs and affected guest activities.
- **Reveal:** Daytime visibility reassures some guests about care but interrupts others' use of a space advertised for meetings. Provide observed fictional task needs, not a universal claim that hidden or visible work is better.
- **Learning objective:** Locate support work within the guest experience and make the service promise, timing and visibility consistent.
- **Difference:** HSP-13 concerns entering private guest rooms; HSP-16 concerns acoustic comfort. This case concerns frontstage maintenance work in a shared service space and how it changes that space's promised use. Keep staffing, skills, employee rights and team structure fixed: it is not a PPL work-design or transparency-of-performance case.
- **Unverified research leads:** service operations transparency research; hotel public-area maintenance guidance; hospitality servicescape studies; accessibility standards; workplace scheduling guidance.

### HSP-39 · A group booking. Who may change it?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional tour organiser receives conflicting room-list instructions from the group leader and individual adult participants. The contract specifies who can change the paid allocation, while guests retain privacy and personal choices. Choose a clear confirmation workflow before the deadline.
- **Reveal:** An individual request affects another traveller's room and cost, so acting on it independently would create conflicting promises. Separate permission to share information from authority to change the group booking, and obtain only the required confirmation.
- **Learning objective:** Map decision rights in group service delivery without assuming the payer controls every personal preference or the guest can alter every shared commitment.
- **Difference:** HSP-20 concerns diverse visitor motivations; HSP-26 concerns supplier handoffs. This case concerns authority boundaries among customer-side parties and avoiding incompatible booking instructions.
- **Unverified research leads:** package-travel contract guidance; public group-booking terms; privacy-regulator guidance on minimal sharing; travel-agent professional standards; group hospitality service research.

### HSP-40 · A great last moment. A better whole visit?
- **Opening decision:** A fictional attraction can spend its improvement budget reducing a long mid-visit inconvenience or adding a pleasant farewell experience. Show the actual guest burden, repeat-intent measures and financial limits. No safety or access failures can be traded away.
- **Reveal:** Immediate recollection favours the farewell option, but later measures of repeat behaviour and time lost point differently under supplied fictional data. Choose and explain which outcome the operator should improve, rather than optimise a single satisfaction score.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish remembered experience, experienced burden and subsequent behaviour in service evaluation.
- **Difference:** HSP-19 concerns waiting uncertainty; HSP-29 concerns recovery entitlements. This case compares competing outcome measures and the temporal distribution of an ordinary service experience.
- **Unverified research leads:** peak-end evaluation research; hospitality experience-memory studies; visitor satisfaction and revisit studies; service-quality measurement research; attraction management evaluation guides.

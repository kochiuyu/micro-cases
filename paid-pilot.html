ENT-02 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Customer discovery · Staged commitment

They like the prototype. Will they buy it?

Hong Kong in the news · July 2026

On 28 July 2026, the Hong Kong Housing Society and Cyberport reported that 14 startups had completed their second PropTech proof-of-concept programme, with more than 20 field trials. Some solutions had reached deployment or commercialization. Real operating sites let founders test whether a proposed solution works outside a demonstration.

The announcement did not disclose customer conversion, retention or profitability for every startup. A successful trial and a scalable business are related, but different, claims. This case applies that distinction to a fictional physical product, not to any participating startup.

Your classroom decision

You and a co-founder designed a compact wooden desk organizer for small offices. At a Hong Kong design demonstration, 80 office workers left their email addresses for updates; 64 said the prototype looked useful. Nobody at that demonstration was asked to pay. Your intended customer is a small-office operator buying organizers for staff, not necessarily the employee using one.

The co-founder wants to order 500 units while the lower unit-cost quote is available. You can instead make 20 units for a small paid trial. Both versions have the same usable design and quality. Either batch can be delivered in four weeks; waiting for a later production slot could delay a larger launch. Neither quote commits you to future batches.

They show the cash committed to production, not profit. The email list is not a list of orders; unsold units have no assured buyer or resale value.

Your decision

Would you authorize 500 units, make 20 first, or wait? What must be true about the buyer—not just the person who likes the prototype—for your choice to make sense?

Round 2 · One fact changes

A follow-up test now reports actual offers to 10 independent small-office operators with purchasing authority. Each received the same clear offer: five organizers for HK$1,500, delivery in four weeks, no subscription or hidden charges. Two operators paid; the other eight have not committed by the decision date. Some prefer their existing desk setup or have no budget for this purchase.

Those are two paid orders for 10 units in total, not 10 paying customers or 80 orders. The 10 operators were deliberately recruited for interviews, not randomly sampled from all offices. No buyer has received or used the product yet. The 20-unit batch can meet both paid orders and leave 10 units for further tests. You must deliver the promised product or arrange an agreed remedy, including a refund if you cannot fulfil it.

Your decision

Does this evidence justify the larger batch, a bounded trial, or stopping? Specify what you will learn from delivery and use before making the next production decision.

Debrief · Interest, payment and use

The demonstration showed interest among users, at no price commitment. The follow-up showed two buyers willing to pay the stated price for a small order. Both observations are useful, but answer different questions. A user may enjoy a product without controlling the office budget; a budget holder may buy for reasons users did not mention.

Two out of ten approached operators paid in this test. Do not multiply that 20% by the entire office market or the 80-person email list: the groups differ, the sample is small and deliberately selected, and eight non-buyers may have different reasons. Payment strengthens the evidence, but does not yet establish sustained usefulness, economical delivery or demand for 500 units.

Buy learning before scale

The small batch commits HK$4,000 rather than HK$50,000 to production: HK$46,000 less cash at risk in this decision. Its higher unit cost buys a chance to learn before a larger commitment; it is not proof that small batches will be economical forever. The two orders generate HK$3,000 of receipts for 10 units, while the full trial batch costs HK$4,000 before other expenses. Do not call those receipts profit or treat the unsold 10 units as sales.

A bounded trial is defensible: fulfil the two orders, observe whether staff actually use the organizers, record delivery and support effort, and test the same full-price offer with additional relevant buyers. A larger batch needs stronger demand evidence or another clear strategic reason. Stopping entirely may be premature if two genuine buyers can help identify a valuable segment.

Make the next test explicit

Write the learning plan before results arrive. For example: deliver the two orders on time, check use after four weeks, record problems and returns, and approach a defined set of additional budget holders with the same price and scope. Ask non-buyers about their current workaround, purchasing process and reason for declining. Do not change the offer silently or count a courtesy compliment as another order.

Decide what evidence would advance, change or stop the next batch. Any numerical threshold is a managerial choice, not a scientific validation rule. Track buyer payment, product use, any further paid orders and all fulfilment costs separately. A durable desk organizer need not be repurchased monthly; evidence of value can include continued use and a later decision to equip more desks. No automatic renewal is assumed.

Back to the evidence

HKHS’s programme demonstrates the value of real-world trials, but public cohort announcements do not prove unsubsidized demand for every solution. Its 2024 and 2026 releases describe different stages of the same programme. Customer-discovery guidance similarly asks founders to test their assumptions with real customers and stakeholders rather than rely on presentation applause.

For a physical-product example, GE Appliances reported in August 2015 that the Opal ice-maker crowdfunding campaign attracted commitments above its target. That is stronger evidence of commitment than free interest alone, but early-adopter backing before delivery is not proof of later profit, fulfilment reliability or a mass market. The fictional organizers here are not an Opal or PropTech project.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–3: choose a batch and explain what the 80 emails mean. Minutes 3–7: reveal the paid offers; separate users, buyers, orders and units. Minutes 7–12: design a four-week learning plan with an explicit next decision, while preserving the promises already made to the two buyers.

Challenge “20% will buy” by asking who entered that denominator. Challenge “the large batch is cheaper” by asking cheaper per unit or lower total commitment. Challenge “two buyers prove nothing” by asking what can be learned from delivering real value to them. This case is about staged demand learning, not fundraising mechanics or a universal minimum sample size.

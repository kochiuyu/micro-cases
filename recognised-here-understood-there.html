MKT-36 · Hong Kong / Bangkok · 15–20 minutes · Cross-market brand language · Pronunciation and meaning · Naming evidence

Recognised here. Understood there?

Sound, meaning and recognition can travel differently

A name can preserve a brand’s sound without preserving its meaning for a new audience. Naming research and practical guidance distinguish associations, pronunciation, memory and brand linkage. All candidates and test responses below are fictional. Candidate codes deliberately replace actual words; the exercise makes no claim about what any real Thai expression means.

Your classroom decision

You manage a fictional Hong Kong skincare brand preparing a first Bangkok retail launch. Entry, product formulation, price, distributor and shelf placement are already settled. The product is an ordinary moisturiser, with no therapeutic claim. The decision concerns its customer-facing name, not product efficacy or whether to enter Thailand.

Candidate A retains the familiar home-market sound in a proposed Thai-script rendering. Candidate B is a meaning-led adaptation intended to suggest everyday personal care, with a different sound. Both would retain the same small parent-brand identifier. A may preserve linkage; B may communicate more locally. Neither advantage has yet been demonstrated.

Candidates are called A and B only in this teaching text. Participants in the fictional study see full proposed names, not those codes. No actual wording is supplied for students to translate or pronounce. Judge the supplied audience evidence rather than guessing which sounds or meanings Thai customers should prefer.

Both test cards omit the parent identifier and use identical typography, layout and factual product description; only the candidate name changes. No packaging is printed or order promised. The team must first choose a naming direction. Linguistic screening and appropriate trademark review are separate prerequisites before any real launch; neither candidate has final clearance.

Your decision

Which direction would you take into testing, A or B? Separate the customer tasks of saying the name, remembering it, interpreting it and linking it to the parent brand. What evidence would make you reconsider?

Round 2 · More information

A fictional exploratory study recruits 80 adult Thai-speaking Bangkok moisturiser shoppers unfamiliar with the brand. They are volunteers, not a representative market sample. Random assignment gives 40 people to each candidate, with each seeing only that candidate. The same neutral description and instructions are used; nobody is told the designers’ intended meaning.

Each participant first describes spontaneous associations and reads the displayed name aloud without coaching. A predefined rubric accepts locally natural pronunciation variants that preserve the intended brand sound; it does not grade accents against a foreign ideal. Two trained local-language coders independently assess recordings and reconcile disagreements using that rubric.

Everyone then hears their assigned name once in a standard recording. After the same two-minute unrelated task, they try to recall it without seeing the card. Finally, everyone sees and hears the home-market parent name and answers whether the candidate belongs to that brand. That last task measures prompted linkage, not prior awareness or spontaneous recognition.

A has 14 of 40 acceptable first readings and 18 delayed recalls. B has 32 acceptable first readings and 28 delayed recalls. Before the audio, two A participants and twelve B participants spontaneously associate the name with a household-cleaning product rather than personal care. These are fictional coded responses, not dictionary definitions.

After the parent reference is shown and heard, 34 A participants and 16 B participants correctly link the candidate to it. Each task uses all 40 people in its assigned group. People may succeed on some tasks and fail others; no joint outcomes are supplied. No purchases, safety outcomes or sales effects were measured.

Neither creative team predicted both A’s pronunciation difficulty and B’s unintended association. One revision and a fresh target-audience test are funded at HK$12,000, whichever direction is chosen. Six weeks remain for this work and separate clearance. There is no tested hybrid or cost-free third name; printing stays on hold until review is complete.

Your decision

Choose which direction to revise within the funded round. Explain the observed trade-off without adding task counts into a popularity score. Specify one change to the brief and how you would test it, while distinguishing consumer evidence from linguistic and legal clearance.

Debrief · Four tasks, four different signals

A’s first-reading result is 14/40 = 35%, versus B’s 32/40 = 80%. Delayed recall after one audio exposure is 45% for A and 70% for B. Those are different tasks and exposure conditions: delayed recall does not show how easily someone can initially read an unfamiliar printed name.

The household-cleaner association appears in 5% of A’s group and 30% of B’s. Prompted parent linkage is 85% for A and 40% for B. A’s home-market continuity helps in that specific reference task, while B’s intended meaning does not prevent an unwanted association among some of these volunteers.

Debrief · Do not collapse the evidence into one score

The same 40 people contribute to each task within a candidate. Adding successful readings, recalls and links would count people repeatedly and impose unstated weights. Without joint responses, we cannot calculate how many both remembered the name and avoided the unwanted association. The case gives no defensible overall winner formula.

The study compares the complete candidate renderings, not sound alone against meaning alone. Differences may reflect several linguistic features together. Random assignment strengthens this particular comparison but does not make volunteers representative or establish that the observed gaps will recur throughout Bangkok or other Thai-speaking communities.

Debrief · Revise a direction rather than print a verdict

I would revise A’s local rendering to improve first-reading accessibility while trying to preserve its parent-brand link. The reason is not that foreign-sounding names are inherently better: A has fewer unwanted associations here, and the home-brand connection is a stated goal. Its poor reading and recall results still require a substantive response.

Revising B is also defensible if local readability and recall take priority, but the new brief must investigate and address the cleaning-product association. Do not assume a synonym automatically fixes it. Either revision is a new candidate whose earlier scores no longer apply; no supplied option is ready for final printing.

Debrief · Keep the next test tied to use

Ask local-language specialists to explore alternative renderings and associations with intended buyers, then test the selected revision against a retained reference under the same presentation conditions. Prespecify acceptable pronunciation variants, unaided association coding, delayed recall exposure and parent-linkage task. Include relevant language-use differences rather than treating nationality as one uniform response.

The HK$12,000 covers only one revision and test round, not unlimited exploration. Agree the practical decision criteria before seeing new results and report recruitment and uncertainty honestly. A larger count alone will not repair leading questions, coached pronunciation or a sample that misses the people expected to use the name.

Debrief · Evidence is not clearance

An unwanted association is a finding to investigate, not proof that the name literally means household cleaner in Thai. Conversely, an accurate translation or dictionary check does not establish how buyers will interpret, pronounce or remember a brand. Preserve the distinction between expert language assessment and observed audience responses.

Trademark availability, permissible product claims and final packaging compliance require their own appropriate checks. A favourable name test grants none of those approvals. Keep the product and market-entry decisions fixed while resolving the language brief; do not turn prototype counts into a claim of higher demand, better skincare or cultural superiority.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–4: choose a direction and name the four communication tasks. Minutes 4–9: interpret each result with its own exposure and denominator. Minutes 9–14: write a revision brief within one funded round. Minutes 14–18: distinguish target-audience learning, actual language review and legal clearance.

Do not ask students to invent Thai meanings or mimic an accent. All words are deliberately coded and all associations are fictional. Assess whether the proposed revision responds to the evidence and preserves relevant brand goals, not whether the student asserts that retaining or translating a name is always superior.

  1. Lowrey and Shrum · Phonetic Symbolism and Brand Name Preference · 2007. Original laboratory research shows that sound-related preferences can depend on product attributes. Native-English student experiments do not establish Thai meanings, skincare preferences or the fictional counts.
  2. Snodin, Higgins and Yoovathaworn · How Thai businesses utilize English in their product names · 2017. Original descriptive analysis of Thai OTOP names documents language mixing and contextual naming patterns. It is not a consumer experiment or evidence that all Thai shoppers interpret names alike.
  3. WIPO · IP Panorama · Module 2: Trademarks and Industrial Designs. Educational guidance distinguishes language, connotations and mark searches when selecting a name. No actual trademark search, linguistic clearance or local legal ruling is supplied.
  4. SurveyMonkey · Name Testing: How to Validate a Brand or Product Name. Original commercial guidance distinguishes associations, recall, pronounceability and stimulus conditions. No vendor performance guarantee, sample-size rule or simplified significance shortcut is adopted.
  5. UK Government Digital Service · Using moderated usability testing. Primary service-research guidance recommends relevant participants, clear neutral tasks and consistent delivery. Applied conceptually to this name exercise, not claimed to validate a retail test or sample size.

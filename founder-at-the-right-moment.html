ENT-25 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Service productisation · Founder dependence · Scalable delivery

Every customer gets the founder. Can the business grow?

A repeatable service need not be an impersonal one

Research on service productisation examines how variable services become defined offerings through clearer specifications and repeatable components. It does not require every client to receive an identical diagnosis. Service-blueprint practice also distinguishes customer-facing interactions from the supporting work behind them.

This fictional case asks where a founder’s distinctive contribution belongs within a service, rather than whether a founder should simply work harder or disappear.

Your classroom decision

Mei founded Counterwise, a small Hong Kong advisory studio helping independent cafés improve their customer flow. Each engagement covers one café, one observation visit and one practical action report. It excludes architectural drawings, regulatory advice and implementation. Mei currently does everything herself: diagnosis, report preparation, quality review and the client discussion.

Eight cafés want this service next month at the usual fixed fee. Mei has not promised delivery dates. She normally reserves 40 hours a month for this service after sales, administration and other commitments. For this month only, she can choose up to eight additional paid delivery hours without displacing those commitments. She wants to grow beyond another month of longer hours.

Her colleague Sam has 32 paid hours available for this service next month. Sam already knows the studio’s tools but has not delivered this package independently. Mei could keep doing each engagement herself or spend time training Sam and documenting a bounded handoff. Customer acceptance and the amount of review needed remain uncertain.

Colleague hours cannot yet replace founder hours automatically. All figures and customer details are invented for discussion.

Your decision

For next month, would you keep delivery founder-only, pilot a divided service, or accept fewer cafés? Identify one task to retain, one task to test transferring, and the evidence you need before committing.

Round 2 · More information

Mei speaks to all eight café owners. Four will pay the usual fee if Mei still diagnoses their café and personally reviews and explains the recommendations. They are comfortable with Sam preparing the report from her brief. The other four want Mei to do every stage at that fee; they would rather wait than accept the divided service. This is feedback from these eight prospects, not a general market finding.

A rehearsal using a completed project suggests a divided engagement needs two founder hours for diagnosis, three Sam hours for report preparation, and one founder hour for review and explanation. Before live delivery, a checklist, annotated example and practice session will require eight additional founder hours and six Sam hours once, within next month’s capacity. These are planning estimates, not proven live-delivery times.

The proposed handoff lets Sam organise evidence and draft only the actions specified in Mei’s brief. Conflicting observations, new recommendations or work outside the package return to Mei. Review is already included in the three founder hours per divided engagement; unexpected rework is not. The fee, scope and promised turnaround are unchanged. No client has yet booked the revised service.

Your decision

Four cafés accept the divided service and four want Mei throughout. Would you pilot four divided engagements and deliver four personally, keep all eight founder-only, or reduce the batch? State the hours, the customer promise and a condition for expanding the pilot.

Teaching focus

Locate founder dependence at the activity level. Separate the judgment customers buy from routine work that carries that judgment into a usable deliverable. A teachable service needs a scope, an input brief, an output standard and an exception route, not merely a new person assigned to the same vague promise.

Working through the choice

All eight founder-only engagements take 8 × 6 = 48 founder hours and no Sam hours. The four-and-four pilot takes 8 setup + 4 × 3 divided + 4 × 6 founder-led hours = 44 founder hours, and 6 setup + 4 × 3 = 18 Sam hours. It fits the temporary 48-hour ceiling but still exceeds Mei’s normal budget by four hours. It saves only four founder hours this month, not twelve, because setup consumes eight.

An all-divided batch would take 32 founder hours and 30 Sam hours including setup. It is arithmetically feasible but not currently acceptable to four customers. Do not count it as the available solution or quietly change their service.

If future customers accept the package and the estimates hold, recurring divided delivery uses three hours from each person. At normal capacity, the ceiling is the smaller of 40 ÷ 3 and 32 ÷ 3: ten whole engagements, limited by Sam. Ten is a capacity ceiling, not forecast demand. At eight engagements, founder time would be 24 hours before unexpected rework. Founder dependence is reduced, not eliminated.

A defensible recommendation

Pilot four with explicit customer agreement, preserve founder diagnosis and final discussion, and deliver the other four personally if Mei accepts the four extra hours. The pilot purchases learning and a reusable method, rather than an immediate saving in total labour: 44 + 18 = 62 hours across both people, versus 48 founder hours alone. Paid colleague availability does not make that work economically free.

Track actual founder and colleague time, corrections, scope exceptions and customer acceptance of the finished work. Require every pilot report to pass Mei’s review and each client to receive the promised founder contact. Before expanding, compare actual times with the three-hour estimates and reserve capacity for observed rework. If diagnosis routinely leaks into report preparation, revise the brief or narrow the package rather than hide the extra work.

Founder-only delivery remains defensible for a temporary demand spike or if customers are paying specifically for sole-authored work. A smaller batch protects normal hours but delays willing customers. Neither choice establishes a repeatable growth model. A student may prefer either if they state what evidence would justify reopening the scaling decision.

Instructor note

Allow 3 minutes to read, 4 for the first choice, 3 for the reveal and arithmetic, and 5–10 for debate. Hold back Round 2 until students identify what customer promise and capability evidence are missing. Ask teams to draw three service stages and assign a named owner and an exception rule to each.

After the reveal, ask why the 32-hour all-divided answer is not yet a commercial option. Then ask why the 44-hour pilot is not a normal-hours solution or a labour-saving claim. Strong answers preserve consent and valued founder contact, count setup and both people’s capacity, and make expansion conditional on live quality and timing. Accept founder-only or reduced-volume choices when their time horizon and learning plan are explicit. Do not award credit simply for saying “delegate” or “automate”.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

Counterwise, Mei, Sam, the cafés, feedback, capacity, rehearsal and every numerical assumption are fictional teaching devices. Public sources supply concepts and managerial guidance, not evidence for these timings, customer preferences or commercial outcomes. Australian guidance is used for general management ideas, not Hong Kong legal requirements. This is a service-design and founder-dependence decision, not a profit forecast.

  1. Wirtz et al. (2021), Service Products and Productization, accepted manuscript. The sections on service specification and modularisation explain defining a service and identifying repeatable components. They do not show that all professional judgment can be standardised.
  2. Nielsen Norman Group, Service Blueprints: Definition. Explains how a service blueprint connects visible customer interactions with supporting activities. Used to distinguish stages, not to assume backstage work is automatically transferable.
  3. Australian Government, Improving productivity in your business. Recommends examining task time, errors, rework and customer satisfaction, as well as documenting routine tasks. Supports the pilot measures rather than the fictional estimates.
  4. Business Queensland, Business policies, processes, procedures and codes of practice. Explains the role of procedures in training, consistency and greater staff independence as a business grows. Documentation alone is not proof of competence.
  5. Business Queensland, Managing and leading in a small business. The delegation exercise asks which tasks others can do and what training or support they need. Supports a bounded handoff with continuing founder responsibility.

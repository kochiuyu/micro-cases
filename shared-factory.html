Two suppliers. One factory?

In the news · Diversifying supply chains

On 9 January 2025, HKTDC and UOB Hong Kong released joint research on Greater Bay Area supply-chain diversification and resilience. It drew on a survey of more than 600 GBA-based companies conducted in the third quarter of 2024.

The research documents surveyed businesses’ plans and experiences, not proof that every diversification choice prevents disruption. Our separate gift shop and stress test below are fictional.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A Hong Kong gift shop needs 1,000 identical presentation boxes each month. Distributor A charges HK$10 per box; distributor B charges HK$11. Both meet the shop’s quality specification, and either can normally supply the required volume.

The owner proposes buying 500 boxes from each distributor rather than all 1,000 from A. The split would cost HK$500 more each month. “Then one supplier’s trouble cannot stop all our packaging,” she says.

These prices include delivery and all costs that differ between the two purchasing arrangements in normal operations. The team has not yet checked where the distributors obtain the boxes or what they could deliver during a disruption.

Monthly planCalculationCost
All from A1,000 × HK$10HK$10,000
Half from each500 × HK$10 + 500 × HK$11HK$10,500

The HK$500 difference is known. The disruption risk and value of protection have not been quantified.

Your decision

Would you pay the HK$500 monthly premium for the split, or keep buying from A? What would you verify before calling the second distributor a backup?

Round 2 · One fact changes

Both distributors obtain these boxes from the same manufacturing plant. For this stress test, assume the plant cannot supply either distributor during the next required delivery window.

Neither distributor holds finished stock available for this shop’s next delivery, and neither has a qualified alternative able to supply within that window. Assume the shop also has no usable buffer covering that delivery.

This is a planning scenario, not a predicted or reported real shutdown. No disruption probability, loss estimate, storage cost or alternative-source quotation is supplied.

Your decision

Does this split keep the shop supplied during the stated plant shutdown? What would you change or investigate before paying for continuity?

Count independent paths, not invoices

Under this specific scenario, neither distributor can deliver the required boxes in time. The 50–50 split therefore does not provide continuity against the common-plant failure.

The two purchasing paths share an upstream dependency: the manufacturing plant behind both distributors. Separate names and invoices do not establish independent production.

That does not make B useless. The split could help with a problem affecting A alone, such as a distributor-specific processing failure, provided B has available capacity and can respond in time. That ability still needs evidence.

Ask what the backup can actually supply

Before paying for continuity, verify the production site, relevant upstream dependencies, stock reserved or available to the shop, alternative capacity, quality qualification and replenishment time.

Ask for a concrete stress-test answer: if this plant stops, how many acceptable boxes could reach us by the required date, from where, and under what commitment?

A supplier in another location may still depend on the same plant, material or transport route. Map the risk you intend to reduce rather than assuming geography alone solves it.

Compare workable protection

Options include qualifying a genuinely separate source, arranging an appropriate stock buffer, or staying with A while explicitly accepting the exposure. Each has costs and limits, including storage, ageing, coordination and response time.

The available figures do not establish an optimal premium or purchasing split. To compare options, the shop needs credible disruption scenarios, likely consequences and the cost and effectiveness of protection.

Do not claim diversification always pays or that single sourcing is always reckless. The decision is whether a particular arrangement addresses the relevant risk at an acceptable cost.

Instructor note

Vote before revealing the common plant. Draw the two paths in words: Plant → A → Shop and Plant → B → Shop. Ask students to identify the shared point before discussing alternatives.

The stress test deliberately removes stock and timely alternative supply so its conclusion is clear. Do not turn the scenario into a forecast. Ask students to propose evidence that could make B a useful backup for a different risk.

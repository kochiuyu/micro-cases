HSP-40 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Remembered experience · Visitor outcomes · Service evaluation

A great last moment. A better whole visit?

The visit and its memory are not one measure

Research distinguishes feelings during an experience from retrospective evaluations. Salient moments and endings can matter, but a remembered score is not a complete account of the visit or proof of later behaviour. Visitor surveys also ask about satisfaction and intended return separately. The fictional attraction below compares those measures with observed revisits.

Your classroom decision

Fictional Harbour Discovery Gallery in Hong Kong has HK$18,000 for one further four-week service trial. Its stated aim is to reduce avoidable visitor inconvenience and support actual repeat visits. Both proposals fit that budget, including staff time and materials. Neither changes admission price, exhibits, opening hours or required safety and accessibility provisions.

M improves a midvisit handoff so visitors collect optional activity materials without an unnecessary second trip across the gallery. F keeps the original handoff and adds a warm, optional farewell with a small illustrated keepsake. Basic courtesy remains under M; the farewell never delays exit, asks for a favourable rating or offers a return discount.

Two earlier small pilots have finished using a separate budget. Each involved 100 consenting adult local visitors in different weeks. At exit, all answered the same overall-visit question on a one-to-ten scale. The director initially sees only the mean scores. The groups were not randomly assigned; fuller records are available.

Round 2 · More information

The completed records report average minutes spent on the avoidable materials-collection detour: six under M and fourteen under F. These are observed elapsed detour times, not subjective ratings or total visit duration. The same observation boundary was used. No safety route or access assistance was removed to shorten the detour.

At exit, 60 of M’s 100 visitors and 72 of F’s 100 said they intended to return within ninety days. Complete consented ticket records then found 36 M visitors and 24 F visitors actually returned within the same ninety-day window after their own visit. Each person counts once, even if they returned several times.

All 200 had the full follow-up window and the same ordinary prices and opportunity to visit. These are complete records for these participants, not all gallery visitors. Different weeks could still mean different weather, visitor motivations or events. No randomisation or adjustment establishes that either intervention caused the differences.

The gallery can now run a better controlled comparison within the new budget, or select one design for a monitored limited trial. Management must decide its measures before seeing new results. It cannot afford both enhancements in the limited trial, and should not cut baseline services to manufacture a combined option.

Your decision

Debrief · Read each measure on its own terms

F leads the exit score by 0.7 points and intended-return rate by twelve percentage points: 72% versus 60%. M has the shorter observed detour by eight minutes and the higher observed ninety-day return rate: 36% versus 24%, also a twelve-percentage-point difference.

Neither intention percentage is an actual return rate. The complete follow-up avoids missing-record ambiguity within these groups, but it does not make the groups comparable. Nor does a lower average detour prove every visitor had a better experience; inspect the distribution and visitors’ own accounts.

Debrief · Use the objective, not the most flattering score

Prioritise M for a monitored limited trial because its observed pattern aligns with both reducing inconvenience and supporting actual repeats. Alternatively, fund a better controlled comparison if choosing between designs is the primary uncertainty. Do not declare M causally superior or promise twelve additional returns per hundred future visitors.

F’s higher remembered evaluation is a real observation worth understanding. A farewell can be valuable without winning every measure. If the objective were only immediate retrospective satisfaction, F would look stronger in these data; changing the objective after seeing scores, however, would conceal the original purpose.

Debrief · Do not turn a heuristic into permission

A peak–end account is one possible explanation for favourable exit recollection. The pilots did not measure peak feelings or isolate ending effects, so they do not prove that mechanism. A different visitor mix could also contribute. A psychological label cannot establish why this particular score changed.

Improving an ending does not erase minutes spent on an inconvenient detour. Do not deliberately worsen earlier moments, conceal the burden or pressure guests to rate the farewell instead of the visit. Preserve honest feedback, basic courtesy and an easy exit whichever design is tested.

Debrief · Design the next evidence

Set outcomes and follow-up windows in advance: detour minutes, overall recalled evaluation, intended return and actual ninety-day return. Track costs, participation, missing responses and accessibility or workload problems. Use minimal consented records, report aggregates and let visitors decline research without losing service.

A feasible randomised comparison with protected baseline service can improve causal inference; account for group visits and spillovers when assigning treatments. Otherwise document differences between periods and remain cautious. Test across ordinary demand conditions, and do not replace observed behaviour with a more convenient survey score.

Instructor note

Ask students to label each figure as experienced burden, retrospective evaluation, intention or behaviour before voting. The exercise does not average these incompatible units into one quality score. No contribution margin or intervention-attributable revenue is given, so a return-on-investment calculation is unsupported.

HSP-19 concerns uncertainty and choice during a wait; HSP-29 concerns equal recovery entitlements with different remedies. Here the hinge is disagreement among outcome measures across a whole visit and follow-up. All pilots, costs, visitor counts and scores are invented; research does not validate these attraction results.

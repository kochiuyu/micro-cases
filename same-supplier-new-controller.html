GVR-44 · Hong Kong · 15 minutes · Supplier oversight · Common control · Concentration risk

Same supplier. Who now controls it?

Control cannot always be read from the direct owner’s name

Hong Kong’s Companies Registry distinguishes direct and indirect forms of significant control. Separately, the Hong Kong Monetary Authority’s guidance for authorized institutions identifies changes in beneficial ownership or control as possible due-diligence triggers. The procurement committee and supplier policy below are fictional; the banking guidance does not govern this contract.

Your classroom decision

Ming oversees a fictional Hong Kong service organisation’s facility support contracts. Harbour Support, approved last year, keeps the same legal entity, direct owner and teams. It seeks a HK$240,000 renewal. The committee also has an independent bidder willing to deliver part of this renewal on the same terms.

The organisation caps annual committed value under one controlling owner at HK$500,000. For this exercise only, trace control through any chain where each link has strictly more than 50% voting rights. There are no shareholder agreements, indirect holdings outside the shown chain or special voting rights. Include the full committed value of each controlled supplier, not just the controller’s voting percentage.

Harbour Support is held by Harbour Holdings, which has 100% of its votes. Vendor Delta has another contract, and Atlas Group controls Delta. Initially independent records show Atlas has no stake in Harbour Holdings, whose owners are unrelated to Atlas and Delta. An acquisition rumour is unverified. No extension or split is signed.

On verified information, would the renewal exceed the common-control cap? What should Ming check before making a recommendation while the acquisition rumour remains unverified?

Round 2 · More information

Updated evidence confirms Atlas bought 60% of the votes of Harbour Holdings before the renewal decision. The direct owner of Harbour Support remains Harbour Holdings. Delta’s contract cannot be reduced. The renewal work can be split in HK$10,000 increments with the verified independent bidder at the same prices and service standards. There are no other contracts under either bidder’s control.

Your decision

If the committee wants to retain as much work with Harbour Support as the cap allows, what is the largest award it can give Harbour Support and the smallest award it should give the independent bidder? What results should Ming document before recommending the split?

Debrief · Separate suppliers at first

On the first verified records, Harbour Support’s proposed HK$240,000 and Delta’s HK$320,000 are not in the same controlled group; both are under HK$500,000. Treat the acquisition as a verification trigger, not proof that the cap has failed. Obtain dated ownership and voting evidence before recommending the commitment.

Debrief · Follow both links

Atlas controls Harbour Holdings with 60% of its votes, and the holding company controls Harbour Support with 100%. Under the stated policy, both Harbour Support and Delta are therefore controlled by Atlas. Renewing in full would commit HK$320,000 + HK$240,000 = HK$560,000, overshooting by HK$60,000.

Debrief · Do not weight the contract by voting share

Atlas has HK$500,000 − HK$320,000 = HK$180,000 of headroom. Recommend HK$180,000 for Harbour Support and HK$60,000 for the independently controlled bidder, subject to fresh approvals. Record the ownership chain and effective date, aggregate the full amounts, confirm separation of the other provider and reset the review date.

Instructor note

Use 2 minutes to read, 4 for the initial screen, 4 to recalculate, and 5 to ask why past approval and legal-entity continuity do not protect the exposure limit. Poll whether to check common ownership again before each large commitment. This voting chain is not the Companies Registry’s legal test.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

The amounts, corporate relationships and policy are fictional. Nothing in the exercise is a judgement about legal control, a regulatory requirement for vendors, or any party’s actual suitability.

  1. Hong Kong Companies Registry, Guideline on Keeping Significant Controllers Registers. Sections 10 and 11 distinguish direct or indirect shares and votes, appointment rights, and other means of control.
  2. Hong Kong Companies Registry, Significant Controllers Register FAQ. Explains that applicable companies identify significant controllers and keep a register accessible to authorized enforcement; it is not an unrestricted public supplier database.
  3. Hong Kong Companies Registry, Keeping of Significant Controllers Register leaflet. Summarizes the purpose and required steps for the separate statutory regime.
  4. HKMA, AML/CFT Guideline for Authorized Institutions. Section 4.12.2 gives control or beneficial-ownership change as a possible trigger in an ongoing banking relationship.
  5. FATF, Guidance on Beneficial Ownership of Legal Persons. Describes why ownership information needs to be adequate, accurate and up to date, and the wider international framework.

STR-35 · Hong Kong and a fictional Asian market · 15–20 minutes · Institutional gaps · Market entry · Credible verification

Global brand. Local proof.

Visibility and verifiability are different

Institutions can help buyers evaluate unfamiliar suppliers. Research distinguishes being prominent from being perceived as capable, while quality-assurance bodies distinguish specific verified claims from blanket approval. This fictional case asks which missing information function a market entrant should address. It does not describe the institutional quality of any real country.

Your classroom decision

You lead fictional Harbour Process, a Hong Kong workflow consultancy entering an unnamed Asian market. It has already completed two relevant local projects successfully. The clients permit narrowly scoped reference checks and approved, sanitised evidence. The firm is not waiting to acquire experience or qualify for an incumbent-only supplier tier.

For the next four weeks, choose one HK$80,000 programme: A, additional brand advertising to the target business audience; or B, independent verification of specified project claims for prospective buyers. Each quotation includes all programme costs and fits the same staff capacity. The budget cannot fund both now; nothing has been purchased.

The offered service, price and delivery capacity stay unchanged. All required permissions, registrations and professional authorisations are already satisfied in this fictional setting. Existing contracts, confidentiality and client release controls remain protected. Neither programme supplies missing legal permission or makes a service safe merely by improving its image.

Before choosing, distinguish three possible obstacles: buyers may not know the firm; they may understand it but reject the service; or they may need it yet lack a trusted way to check claims. The current briefing does not establish which obstacle is binding. There are no sales probabilities or profit forecasts.

Round 2 · More information

Three current prospects accurately describe the service and recall the brand. Each has an identified workflow need but cannot verify the project claims through its usual purchasing channels. These are diagnostic conversations, not a representative survey or proof that advertising never works. No prospect promises an order.

For this narrow service segment, neither local nor foreign suppliers have a shared, buyer-recognised project-verification directory. That missing information channel is the supplied market condition, not a legal barrier, exclusive supplier list or claim about an entire country. Harbour Process already has the relevant experience; buyers lack a trusted checking route.

All three prospects agree that a proposed independent reviewer and written checking protocol would help their evaluation. The reviewer has verified competence for this limited task, no ownership or referral interest, and a fixed fee unrelated to findings or sales. Harbour Process pays; that relationship will be disclosed prominently.

B checks approved project scope and claimed outcomes directly with authorised client contacts, examines released supporting records, and observes a synthetic workflow demonstration. The report identifies what was checked, exceptions and limits. Unfavourable findings must remain visible. It is not accreditation, certification, a warranty or a guarantee of future performance.

The four-week quotation covers these checks. No confidential operational data is transferred beyond existing specific permissions; the synthetic demonstration contains no client data. Prospects retain their normal evaluation and contracting controls. The reviewer cannot approve regulated work, replace required licences or promise buyer acceptance beyond the stated willingness to consider the report.

Your decision

Choose the next programme using the new evidence. Explain which missing market function it addresses, what the report may legitimately claim, and what result would make you stop or revise the approach rather than buy more reassurance.

Debrief · Diagnose the missing function

B is the better-supported next experiment for these prospects. They already recognise the firm, understand its service and report a relevant need. The supplied friction is checking performance claims through credible channels. Additional exposure addresses a different problem. This is a conditional resource-allocation judgment, not a calculated profit maximum.

An institutional gap means a useful transaction-support function is missing or difficult to access in the specified setting. Here it is verification, not an assumption that all institutions are weak. A bounded, buyer-accepted checking process can partially supply that function without claiming to replace the broader institutional system.

Debrief · Reputation is not just advertising

Recognition can coexist with doubt. A brand may carry useful reputation, but repeating a claim does not independently verify it. Conversely, a technically sound report that buyers cannot interpret or do not trust may accomplish little. B is plausible because the actual prospects have accepted the proposed reviewer and protocol for consideration.

The case does not show that brand building is generally inferior. If later evidence identifies awareness as the binding obstacle, A could become appropriate. If buyers reject the service itself, neither louder advertising nor another report necessarily repairs product fit. Recheck the diagnosis rather than treating verification as a universal prescription.

Debrief · Make independence inspectable

Disclose who pays, the reviewer’s relevant competence, the method and the limits. A fixed fee helps avoid a favourable-result incentive but does not eliminate every conflict. Preserve the reviewer’s ability to report exceptions and let buyers contact authorised references. Do not buy a positive endorsement or hide contrary evidence.

Claim only that the specified evidence was checked by the named reviewer using the stated protocol. Do not advertise blanket approval, official accreditation or assured future results. Standards and accreditation sources illustrate why scope matters; they do not confer any status on this fictional consulting review.

Debrief · Define the next observation

After delivery, ask whether prospects can now evaluate the previously uncheckable claims, which exceptions remain, and whether their next decision concerns fit, price or another constraint. An order is not the only learning outcome. No supplied conversion probability permits an expected-return calculation, and three conversations cannot establish market-wide demand.

Pause or redesign B if permission is unavailable, reviewer competence or independence fails, buyers withdraw acceptance of the protocol, or evidence contradicts the claims. Correct the claim or service when warranted. An adverse finding is information, not a reason to suppress the report or purchase a more flattering reviewer.

Instructor note

An entrant lacking required experience faces a different problem from an experienced firm whose evidence cannot be checked. This case supplies completed projects and equal access rules. The strategic task is adapting how claims become credible across markets, not buying access to a protected tier or assuming local partners automatically confer legitimacy.

All market conditions and commercial figures are fictional. The sources supply conceptual and assurance boundaries, not evidence about this destination or legal clearance. Real professional rules, privacy duties and truthful-marketing requirements need their own applicable checks. No country-specific legal conclusion follows from the classroom choice.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

Ask participants to separate awareness, fit and verification before revealing prospect conversations. Recommend B conditionally for the stated narrow sample, not as a universal anti-advertising result. Both programmes cost HK$80,000 over four weeks; no conversion or margin data support ROI or expected-value arithmetic. Existing relevant experience distinguishes this from STR28 supplier-tier entry. Require disclosure of payment, scoped methods, adverse findings and continuing permission controls. A report is not accreditation or legal permission. Three buyer conversations are not representative or causal evidence. Discussion should identify a stopping condition and what to learn after the report.

ECO-22 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Double marginalisation · Vertical contracts · Sharing coordination gains

Two markups. One final customer?

Economic connection

When independent businesses at successive stages each add a markup, their choices can reduce sales and combined contribution. Economics calls this double marginalisation. A fixed fee plus a unit charge can change that incentive in a simple model. Real contracts and mergers require separate competition assessment; a classroom calculation does not establish that an arrangement is lawful or beneficial in every market.

Your classroom decision

A fictional maker supplies a distinctive notebook through one independent Hong Kong retailer for a one-month trial. For this deliberately simplified exercise there is one supplier and one retail outlet for this product; neither has another trading partner during the month. They remain separate businesses. The manufacturer’s complete incremental resource cost is HK$20 per notebook. The retailer has no additional per-unit resource cost; unchanged fixed costs are outside this contribution comparison.

The manufacturer can offer a wholesale price of either HK$40 or HK$60 per notebook, with no fixed fee. The retailer then chooses its own retail price to maximise its contribution. The table lists the only six retail-price candidates considered by this classroom model, not a restriction imposed by the supplier. At each price, exactly the listed quantity sells. Demand is known and unchanged across contracts.

Each firm maximises its own monthly contribution. Capacity covers all listed volumes, units are supplied only as sold, and there is no unsold inventory, default, financing constraint or extra tax, delivery, contracting or transaction cost. All comparisons concern the same month and product quality. The retailer can refuse trade and earn zero. No competitor prices are discussed, no resale price is imposed, and lawful contract availability is stipulated for this fictional exercise, not inferred from the numbers.

Round 2 · More information

Both firms have the option to retain a HK$60 wholesale contract with no fixed fee for the month. The manufacturer now offers an alternative: a HK$20 unit charge plus one HK$1,300 fixed fee paid by the retailer to the manufacturer for that month, independent of units sold. No additional service or resource cost is created by the fee. It is not paid twice, per unit or by customers.

If both accept, the retailer still chooses its own retail price from the same model candidates. If either rejects the new arrangement, the HK$60 wholesale contract remains available; HK$40 is not a current fallback offer. Also test a counterproposal that changes only the fixed fee to HK$1,700. These are voluntarily agreed alternatives, not a unilateral change to an existing obligation.

Appropriate review is stipulated to have cleared these fictional alternatives. There is no imposed minimum or fixed resale price, territorial restriction, exclusivity requirement, sharing with rivals or penalty for the retailer’s chosen price. Do not generalise that a unit charge equal to cost makes any real contract automatically lawful.

Your decision

Would both businesses accept HK$20 per unit plus the proposed HK$1,300 fixed fee instead of the HK$60 wholesale contract? Calculate their choices and contributions, then assess a HK$1,700 fee.

Retailer contribution is (retail price − wholesale price) × quantity. At wholesale HK$40, the retailer chooses HK$80 and sells 40: HK$1,600. The manufacturer receives (40 − 20) × 40 = HK$800. At wholesale HK$60, the retailer chooses HK$90 and sells 30: HK$900. The manufacturer receives (60 − 20) × 30 = HK$1,200. The manufacturer prefers the higher wholesale offer among the two; the retailer prefers the lower one, but still earns more than zero at HK$60.

Find the two margins

Under wholesale 60, the maker adds HK$40 above its resource cost and the retailer adds HK$30 above its purchase price. The retailer treats the whole HK$60 as its marginal cost even though only HK$20 is a resource cost for the channel. Combined contribution is (90 − 20) × 30 = HK$2,100. Wholesale payments transfer value between the firms; subtracting them again from combined contribution would double-count a cost.

Separate a unit incentive from a fixed transfer

At a HK$20 unit charge, retailer contribution before the fee at the six candidate retail prices is respectively HK$2,100, HK$2,400, HK$2,500, HK$2,400, HK$2,100 and HK$1,600. HK$70 therefore maximises it, with 50 sales. A fixed fee subtracts the same amount from every candidate, so it does not change that price ranking after acceptance. It can still change whether the retailer accepts the contract.

With fee 1,300, the maker earns the fee because its unit margin is zero. Retailer contribution is 2,500 − 1,300 = HK$1,200. Both exceed their wholesale 60 fallback: gains of HK$100 and HK$300. Total contribution rises from HK$2,100 to HK$2,500. At fee 1,700, the retailer would earn only HK$800, below its HK$900 fallback, so it rejects despite the larger potential channel total. No party is required to accept merely because someone else benefits.

The fee range is a negotiation range

Let the fee be F. The manufacturer requires F at least HK$1,200; the retailer requires 2,500 − F at least HK$900. Thus HK$1,200–1,600 makes neither worse off than wholesale 60; strictly inside gives both a gain. Boundary indifference does not guarantee acceptance. This range divides HK$400 of added contribution; it does not prove one uniquely fair fee. Customers face HK$70 rather than HK$90 and sales rise from 30 to 50 within this model. This is not marginal-cost retail pricing or a claim of complete social efficiency.

Know the model’s boundary

Actual demand uncertainty, additional outlets, service incentives, bargaining power, information limits or competitors can change the result. Research on vertical relationships also examines possible harms to rivals; removing a markup is not a universal welfare or merger defence. Here the retailer chooses its own price. Do not replace that choice with a supplier-imposed retail price or assume a legal conclusion from a favourable contribution table.

Instructor note

Give pairs three minutes to compute the retailer’s best response under each wholesale offer. Reveal the fee contract only after they identify the manufacturer’s preferred wholesale 60 offer. Ask them to track the HK$1,300 once to each side, then explain rejection at HK$1,700. ACC-20 concerns transfers inside one company and opportunity cost; ECO-14 charges final users an entry fee. Here independent successive businesses bargain over their own participation while the final retail price responds. All numeric results are original hypothetical calculations, not findings about a real notebook market.

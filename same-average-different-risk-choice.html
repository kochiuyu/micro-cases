ECO-40 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Expected utility · Risk preferences · Certainty equivalents

The same expected payoff. The same choice?

An average is not a preference

Two options can have identical expected quantities but different appeal. Expected utility applies probabilities to how a person values each possible outcome, rather than assuming every extra unit is equally valuable. This theoretical comparison uses explicit fictional preferences; it does not diagnose anyone’s personality or recommend a financial product.

Your classroom decision

A fictional Hong Kong adult workshop club is discussing a one-off, free allocation of activity credits. Credits pay only for optional club craft sessions. They cannot be bought, sold, transferred or converted into money. Basic membership, existing bookings, accessibility and essential services are unaffected. Nobody stakes money or previously owned credits.

Each member may choose G: receive 50 credits for certain, or L: receive 100 credits with probability 50% and zero credits with probability 50%. This is a hypothetical classroom allocation, not a sold gambling product or an actual draw. All outcomes are funded; neither choice changes any other member’s allocation.

Members fully understand the known probabilities and choose before the outcome is revealed. They begin with zero credits for this allocation, so outcomes are final credit balances, not additions to an unspecified balance. Credits arrive together, have identical use conditions and face no hidden charges or shortages of available activities.

For the exercise, Ada chooses the option with higher expected utility. The table supplies her expected-utility function over final credits, not merely an ordinal ranking. Utility units are preference-model numbers, not dollars or measured happiness. Between listed balances, use straight-line interpolation; the function is strictly increasing throughout the zero-to-100 range.

Expected credits equal the probability-weighted average of credit outcomes. Expected utility instead averages their utility values. A certainty equivalent is the guaranteed credit balance whose utility equals the lottery’s expected utility. It is a hypothetical indifference amount, not an additional offer or a payment to participate.

Debrief · Expected credits do not settle preference

G gives 50 credits with certainty. L has expected credits 0.50 × 100 + 0.50 × 0 = 50. But L actually delivers either zero or 100, never 50. Equality of expected quantities does not turn an uncertain allocation into a guaranteed one.

Ada’s utility from G is 7. Her expected utility from L is 0.50 × 10 + 0.50 × 0 = 5. Since 7 exceeds 5, she prefers G. Looking up the utility of the expected 50 credits would answer the wrong question about L.

Debrief · Ada’s certainty equivalent

Ada assigns utility 5 to 25 credits. Therefore, her certainty equivalent for L is 25 credits: she is indifferent between that hypothetical guaranteed balance and L. The actual G offer provides 50, which is above her certainty equivalent and gives higher utility.

Ada’s schedule is concave over the stated range: the utility increments for each extra 25 credits are 5, 2, 2 and 1. She prefers this lottery’s expected balance for sure, illustrating risk aversion. The model does not imply that she dislikes every kind of uncertainty in life.

Debrief · Ben’s different choice

Ben’s utility from G is 3, while his expected utility from L is 0.50 × 10 + 0.50 × 0 = 5. He therefore prefers L. His utility is 5 at 75 credits, making his certainty equivalent 75, above the lottery’s expected 50 credits.

Ben’s utility increments for each extra 25 credits are 1, 2, 2 and 5. His schedule is convex over the stated range, and he prefers this uncertainty to the guaranteed expected balance. That is a risk-preferring choice in this model, not a recommendation to purchase a lottery or take financial risks.

Debrief · A neutral benchmark

A third hypothetical member with utility u(c) = c has utility 50 from G and expected utility 50 from L. That member is indifferent and has certainty equivalent 50. Ada’s 25, the neutral member’s 50 and Ben’s 75 are all credit quantities, unlike their utility scores.

The definition uses each person’s own preference function. Ada and Ben both have expected utility 5 from L in our chosen normalisations, but this does not mean they experience the same welfare. Utility scales are not interpersonal measuring instruments, and adding their scores would not establish a socially best allocation.

Debrief · Preferences are not probability errors

Both members know that L can produce zero and that each outcome has probability 50%. Their disagreement comes from the supplied utility schedules, not optimism, hidden information, different initial balances or an effort choice that changes the probabilities. Respecting that distinction prevents calling every non-average-maximising choice a calculation mistake.

Expected utility is the exercise’s modelling assumption. Real choices may depend on context, framing, stakes and features not captured by these tables. The two schedules are invented, not estimated from a survey or an experiment. Do not infer either member’s preferences about insurance, investment, health or essential needs.

Instructor note

Ask students to label every result as credits or utility units. Compute expected utility from the outcome rows first; only then look back to find a guaranteed balance with the same utility. Both certainty equivalents land exactly on listed rows, so no interpolation is needed for the answers.

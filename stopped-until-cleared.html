Stop the work. Who may restart it?

Stopping and restarting answer different questions

OSHA’s worker-participation guidance encourages workers to raise concerns and initiate or request suspension of unsafe work without retaliation. Berkeley Lab’s public stop-work policy separately identifies assessment, verified correction, resolution of disagreements and briefing before resumption. These are organisational examples, not a universal approval hierarchy.

HSE guidance addresses inspection after changes that may affect equipment safety and traceable review of modifications. The US Department of Energy’s work-control handbook distinguishes corrective action from documented resumption. This classroom case uses those governance distinctions without prescribing machinery repairs, tests or operating procedures.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A small workshop is revising its formal safety-stop protocol for enclosed production equipment. It is choosing between two fully funded verification arrangements, not choosing whether to permit unsafe work. All workers can report concerns and trained workers have protected authority to stop affected work immediately without waiting for managerial permission. No worker is penalised for a good-faith stop. Existing legal duties, emergency procedures and manufacturer requirements remain mandatory.

Under both arrangements, qualified personnel control technical assessment and corrective work using the workshop’s established safe procedures. The person who performs a repair cannot verify their own work. The same documented acceptance criteria, worker consultation, change screening and briefing requirements apply. A named restart owner, the operations manager, or a formally appointed qualified deputy, alone records the operational release after all required clearances. Neither may override missing technical evidence.

Local verification assigns an on-site competent verifier, independent of the repair and the production target, for ordinary cases within their documented competence. An identified changed safeguard or unresolved concern must escalate to the central safety engineer before release. Central verification sends every formal stop to that engineer for review, including ordinary like-for-like cases. Both routes permit any required physical inspection by qualified personnel; a desk signature never substitutes for necessary evidence.

The on-site verifier knows the equipment and is available throughout the shift. The central engineer covers several workshops, is available for escalation today, and reviews routine cases at scheduled intervals. Central review offers wider consistency and cross-site experience but can create waiting and handoffs. Local review provides proximity but requires disciplined recognition of its competence and escalation boundaries. Both have funded backup coverage, a common record and the same safety standard. The decision is about the normal verification design.

Round 2 · More information

A trained worker stops one equipment cell after observing a fault. The cell remains unavailable under the established safety controls; no injury is reported. A qualified technician corrects the original fault and documents the repair. During restart verification, the verifier finds that a replacement protective component differs from the approved specification. The change’s safety implications and acceptability have not been assessed. This is a changed safeguard, not a completed like-for-like repair.

The repair record establishes only that the original fault was corrected. It provides no technical clearance for the changed safeguard and no assurance that the full safety basis remains valid. The central safety engineer is available to direct the required assessment; its completion time is not yet known. The technician, verifier, reporting worker and restart owner are all available for the relevant review and communication.

An urgent order is waiting. The production lead asks whether the repair signature is enough to restart now while the remaining review follows. No restart has occurred. Under either proposed protocol, a changed safeguard requires the engineer’s clearance before the restart owner may release the cell. Disagreements about safety must be resolved through the established technical escalation route, not by majority vote or commercial pressure.

Separate administrative work can continue only where a qualified assessment confirms it is unaffected and cannot interfere with or circumvent the stop. No substitute production route has been assessed. The team must now state the status, responsible roles and evidence needed to close the stop. This case asks for a governance decision, not an engineering remedy.

Your decision

May the cell restart now? State who must resolve the changed-safeguard issue, what the restart owner needs before release, and how workers and the production lead should be kept informed without inventing a completion time.

Answer the protocol choice

Prefer Local with mandatory escalation for this workshop’s stipulated competence, independence and coverage: routine evidence can be assessed close to the work while changed safeguards go to the central engineer. Central verification is also defensible if consistency or the distribution of specialist skills warrants its additional handoffs. The case supplies no failure-rate or cost data proving one universally superior.

Local does not mean the repairer or production lead certifies their own deadline. Central does not mean an engineer’s signature automatically releases the equipment. Choose the design with explicit boundaries, paid capacity, a qualified deputy and a record visible to affected workers. Evaluate whether concerns are resolved properly, not merely whether stops are short.

Three distinct authorities

Stopping authority lets a worker interrupt unsafe work promptly; it does not require the worker to diagnose or fix the hazard first. Technical clearance answers whether relevant hazards, changes and required evidence have been competently assessed and resolved. Operational release is the named owner’s documented confirmation that all prerequisites are complete and affected people understand the authorised scope.

The restart owner remains accountable for the release decision, but cannot manufacture technical competence or waive a missing clearance. The reporting worker contributes observations and must receive a response; they are not made personally liable for proving the equipment safe. Preserve the right to raise a fresh concern after any release.

Answer the live decision: keep the stop in place

No restart now. The changed protective component is outside the original repair clearance. Under either design, the central safety engineer must lead or obtain the necessary competent assessment and required technical acceptance of the changed safeguard, or a compliant resolution, through existing procedures. The case does not specify which technical remedy will be acceptable.

The stop remains active until required evidence covers the original fault, the change and relevant connected conditions; required checks are complete; unresolved technical concerns are settled; and affected workers are briefed. Only then may the named restart owner document the authorised release. A deadline, a repair invoice or the absence of an injury is not evidence that these gates have been passed.

Make status and ownership visible

Record the affected cell and scope, original concern, completed repair, newly identified change, outstanding assessment, accountable engineer, restart owner and decision status. Communicate “stopped, repair completed, changed safeguard not cleared” rather than a vague “almost ready”. Set the next update point without promising a restart time before evidence supports one.

Notify the reporting worker and affected team when findings and decisions change. If responsibility moves to a deputy or another shift, hand over the unresolved issue and current restriction explicitly. Unaffected administrative work is allowed only within its verified boundary. Do not quietly move the same blocked production elsewhere or treat elapsed waiting as approval.

Instructor note

Invite a genuine choice between Local and Central before the reveal. Both preserve the same controls; students should not invent a reckless local option or an omniscient central reviewer. The reveal does not necessarily reverse the design choice: it activates an escalation already built into Local and broadens the clearance question under either design.

Unlike GVR-20’s incident coordination, this is a normal-work control separating immediate stop, competent technical evidence and documented restart responsibility. No repair, isolation, bypass or testing method is provided. All workshop facts and roles are invented. Overseas regulator and laboratory documents are conceptual sources, not claims about local legal compliance; real protocols require competent site-specific design.

PPL-20 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Social learning · Informal influence · Change implementation

The instruction is clear. Whose example changes the habit?

Service habits need more than a slogan

Hong Kong launched its Hospitality Campaign on 3 June 2024, involving public bodies, tourism organisations and service sectors. The announcement describes an effort to promote hospitality, not a measured effect of a particular staff-training method. Our visitor desk, people and observations below are fictional.

Your classroom decision

A fictional visitor-information desk has ten experienced advisers. An already-approved closing routine asks the adviser to summarise the requested route and offer a printed or accessible digital route card, without pressuring the visitor to accept. Staff retain all essential directions, accessibility help and safety advice regardless. The extra routine is a non-safety-critical service enhancement, not a substitute for those duties. Its written guide is accurate, materials are available and scheduled service time permits it.

The manager has a funded four-week support block. A uses paid supervisor-led refresher briefings and short practice with a common script. B uses paid small-group demonstrations by a willing, experienced colleague, with feedback on ordinary examples. Each costs HK$4,000 all-in, including preparation and staff cover. Both reach every adviser, retain the written guide and supervisor checks, and preserve breaks, workload limits and customer commitments. The peer has no disciplinary authority. Participation in the extra peer role is voluntary and fairly compensated.

Both options can include questions and practical examples. A offers consistent explanation from the accountable supervisor; B makes the agreed routine visible in everyday colleague-to-colleague work. There is no measured comparison of their effects. The manager must choose the primary format for this block, not purchase both full packages. No covert monitoring, public rankings or punishment based on these learning observations is permitted.

Your decision

Would you use the formal refresher or peer demonstrations as the primary support format? Explain the behaviour you want to change and how written standards and supervisor accountability would remain visible.

Round 2 · More information

Before the block begins, all ten advisers correctly explain and demonstrate the routine in an announced rehearsal. In separately announced, consented observations of 20 ordinary eligible encounters, the routine is used in eight and omitted in twelve. These are encounters, not ten independent employee scores. No essential direction, safety or accessibility service was omitted. The record does not establish why the difference occurred.

In private voluntary discussions, several advisers say they copy a respected colleague’s longstanding habit of ending after verbal directions, even though they know the new routine. That colleague says the extra step seemed to slow the queue. A joint walkthrough confirms that the available materials and protected time make the routine workable without reducing required service. The colleague is now willing to model it consistently in the paid role, raise practical difficulties openly and share the role with another trained colleague. These statements and the walkthrough support a possible mechanism, not proof of a causal effect or a guarantee of future behaviour.

Both formats remain available for HK$4,000. A can now address the difference between the written routine and daily examples explicitly; B can use the willing colleague’s influence, but needs supervisor support and coverage when that person is absent. Existing supervision, rights and agreed workloads remain. The manager can review actual practice during the block and change support if it does not work, without blaming staff for raising concerns.

Your decision

Would the evidence change your choice of support format? Distinguish knowing the routine from following a local example, and specify the peer’s role, the supervisor’s responsibility and a fair check of whether the habit changes.

Knowledge and enactment are different observations

Ten successful rehearsals show capability under those conditions. Eight of twenty encounters used the routine, or 40% of the observed encounters; they do not show that only four staff knew it or that 60% of staff resisted. Repeated encounters, context and observation effects limit interpretation. The discussion suggests a social example worth addressing, not an established causal diagnosis.

Make a useful example credible

B has a stronger rationale when respected daily behaviour conflicts with an understood instruction. Give the willing colleague time, an accurate demonstration and a route for reporting practical barriers. A title alone does not make an effective role model, and a popular employee should not become an unpaid enforcer. Shared coverage avoids making the routine dependent on one person’s presence.

Keep management in the process

A remains credible if supervisor practice explicitly tackles the old example rather than simply repeating rules. Under B the manager still models the routine, confirms resources, answers unresolved questions and maintains fair accountability. Neither approach can compensate for impossible workloads by asking employees to smile more or skip breaks. Listen when staff identify an actual obstacle.

Instructor note

PPL-04 concerns novices who cannot yet execute a handoff; PPL-10 concerns learning an unfamiliar method. These experienced advisers already demonstrate the method. The decision concerns informal influence on daily enactment. Research on healthcare teams informs a bounded analogy, not clinical guidance or a prediction of tourism-service results. Evaluate observed eligible encounters, context and staff feedback with consent; do not import reported research effect sizes or covert observation methods.

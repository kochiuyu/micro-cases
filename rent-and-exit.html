FIN-12 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Lease versus buy · Residual value · Contract flexibility

Rent the equipment. Keep the exit?

In the news

On 17 September 2025, Hong Kong announced an extension of the application period for the 80 per cent Guarantee Product under the SME Financing Guarantee Scheme. The announcement concerned access to business financing, not which assets a firm should acquire.

Our fictional firm already has funds for either equipment route. No scheme eligibility, subsidy or loan approval is assumed.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A small Hong Kong surveying firm needs a desktop scanner to digitise archive documents. It is not used for safety-critical measurements. The owner initially plans three years of use and is comparing buying outright with a three-year lease of the same model.

Capacity, reliability, installation, training and maintenance service levels are equivalent under the two written quotes. Both routes are affordable without borrowing or using the agreed operating reserve. Neither has been accepted. Common staff time and operating costs are identical.

Use an undiscounted cash comparison for this teaching exercise: ignore interest on retained cash, income-tax effects and inflation. These simplifications isolate ownership, service costs and exit commitments; the result is not a present-value appraisal or a recommendation for a real firm.

Your decision

For the initial three-year plan, which route would you provisionally choose? Compare total net cash cost and explain what risk or cash-timing consideration your preferred route still carries.

Round 2 · More information

Before either quote is accepted, the client confirms that the archive project ends after 12 months, not 36. No other work needs the scanner afterward in this revised comparison. You must therefore compare purchase-and-sale at month 12 with lease-and-return at month 12, not with three years of productive use.

The lease’s written early-exit option permits return after the twelfth monthly payment, with the required notice given on time and an additional HK$12,000 exit charge. That charge includes collection and secure removal and releases all remaining monthly payments. The supplied condition and return standard can be met; no extra damage or usage charge is assumed. Merely stopping use would not activate this option.

Buying requires the HK$90,000 payment and one HK$6,000 service payment. A dealer supplies two plausible month-12 net resale scenarios: HK$45,000 and HK$65,000. Neither is a firm offer, neither has an assigned probability, and they are not guaranteed lower and upper bounds. Assume a sale can be completed at month 12 at the scenario price; no later income or holding cost remains in these scenarios.

The quotes, service quality and available funds otherwise stay unchanged. This is a revised planning horizon before commitment, not an instruction to break an existing lease.

Your decision

For the revised 12-month need, would you buy or use the lease’s agreed exit option? Compare both resale scenarios, find the net resale amount at which the cash costs match, and identify what remains uncertain.

Compare a complete period

For three years, buying has forecast net cash cost HK$90,000 + 3 × HK$6,000 − HK$30,000 = HK$78,000. Leasing costs 36 × HK$3,000 = HK$108,000. Buying is HK$30,000 lower under that resale forecast and the supplied undiscounted model.

The initial cheque is not the whole cost, and a monthly payment is not the total price. The lease spreads payments and removes the firm’s resale task; that does not automatically make it cheaper or cancellable at will.

An exit option has a price and conditions

At 12 months, the agreed lease exit costs 12 × HK$3,000 + HK$12,000 = HK$48,000. Do not omit the exit charge or also add the remaining 24 payments: the supplied option explicitly releases them when exercised correctly.

A different lease might not offer this route. Check the actual notice, return and release terms rather than assuming that unused equipment means no further payments.

Ownership keeps the residual-value exposure

Buying for the revised year costs HK$96,000 before resale. With HK$45,000 net proceeds, cost is HK$51,000: HK$3,000 above leasing. With HK$65,000, cost is HK$31,000: HK$17,000 below leasing.

Costs match when 96,000 − resale = 48,000, so net resale is HK$48,000. Above that amount buying is cheaper in this model; below it the specified lease exit is cheaper. The threshold is not a valuation or a forecast.

Recommend without inventing certainty

Leasing can be justified for a known HK$48,000 contractual exit cost and less initial cash use. Buying can be justified if the firm accepts resale uncertainty and has credible evidence supporting proceeds above HK$48,000. Both choices remain affordable; neither is forced by a cash shortage.

Do not average the two resale scenarios into an expected price without probabilities. A real appraisal would also examine timing and financing costs, taxes, maintenance exclusions, sale delays and alternative future uses. These are deliberately held out of this model, not declared irrelevant everywhere.

Instructor note

Allow three minutes for the first comparison, three for the changed horizon, four for scenario costs and the threshold, then two for recommendations. Ask each group to identify an exit obligation as well as a number.

The central issue is lease-versus-ownership commitments and residual risk for the same asset. It is not FIN-11’s floating-rate exposure or FIN-08’s choice between projects with different cash-flow timing. Treat HK$48,000 as a conditional cash-cost match, not an investment recommendation or an optimal resale price.

SUS-18 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Participatory governance · Agenda setting · Access to participation

Residents will vote. Who can get onto the ballot?

Participation begins before the vote

On 26 October 2024, Taipei’s city government described its participatory-budgeting process, including resident meetings and proposal-review workshops before voting. This official account shows several stages of participation; it does not prove every resident had equal influence. The neighbourhood fund and process choices below are original fiction, not Taipei’s rules.

Your classroom decision

A fictional neighbourhood trust in Hong Kong has HK$80,000 for one optional courtyard improvement. Three proposals are ready: a small planting area at HK$60,000, a reading corner at HK$70,000 and movable games furniture at HK$80,000. Each has passed the same permissions, safety, access, maintenance and cost checks. Existing required maintenance and accessibility remain funded separately. Unspent project money stays with the trust; it is not redistributed as a prize.

The trust has not yet finalised the process. Its published draft rules allow either of two routes before a fixed vote four weeks from today. A confirms the three-project shortlist now and uses four weeks to explain the alternatives. B spends the first two weeks offering supported proposal sessions and technical help, then two weeks explaining the final eligible choices. Two weeks of accessible information before voting is the stated minimum under either route. Existing proposals remain eligible; no winner has been promised.

B costs an additional HK$10,000 from a separate, approved process allowance, leaving the full project fund intact. A retains that allowance for later engagement work. In both routes, every adult resident has one vote, paper and supported in-person voting are available, and participation is voluntary. The project with the most votes wins; a tied top vote is resolved by a public draw. No vote has been cast. Newly supported ideas under B must meet the same published eligibility checks, cost ceiling and maintenance requirements; support does not guarantee a ballot place or funding.

Would you choose A or B for this cycle? Explain the tradeoff between preparation time, process resources and residents’ ability to shape the choices before they vote.

Round 2 · More information

A process review finds that all three initial proposals came from established clubs with volunteers who could prepare drawings and supplier estimates. The original invitation was open to all adults, but a technical submission was required and no drafting help was available. This does not mean the clubs acted improperly or their projects lack value.

At a voluntary listening session, several residents describe wanting extra shaded seating for short social visits. They had not submitted it because they could not turn the idea into a costed drawing. An independent technical check now confirms a safe, permitted HK$75,000 version with funded maintenance would fit the published project criteria. It is an optional comfort improvement, not an unmet mandatory access or safety duty. The listening group is not a representative survey; no one knows which project would win.

B can open the same supported route to all residents, not reserve it for the seating group, while preserving the existing three proposals. A can retain its longer explanation period but would leave this newly identified option outside the current ballot. Under A, the trust would document the gap and offer proposal help in a later process; no later fund or future project award is guaranteed. Both routes still meet the stated minimum voting access and notice conditions. The trust cannot simply add its favourite project and call that resident choice.

Your decision

Would you retain or change your process choice after learning how the shortlist formed? Specify who can propose, what support is offered and how eligibility decisions remain transparent without promising every idea a place or an award.

Separate two opportunities to participate

One person, one vote gives equal formal voting weight among eligible residents. It does not establish equal opportunity to generate the options. Requiring a drawing and estimate can favour people with technical help before anyone reaches the ballot. Review access to proposal development as well as access to voting.

Support the process, not a preferred winner

B addresses a specific preparation barrier, but should offer neutral assistance to everyone under the same rules. Publish what was submitted, the eligibility reasoning and how questions or corrections can be raised, without revealing unnecessary personal information. A club proposal should not be punished for being well prepared; a new idea should not bypass safety, cost or maintenance checks because its supporters lacked help.

Keep the tradeoff visible

B uses HK$10,000 and compresses explanation from four weeks to two while retaining the minimum. A preserves resources and more time to understand the ready choices, but knowingly narrows the current agenda. Students can defend a route only by acknowledging what it leaves unresolved. Neither a larger ballot nor a high turnout proves substantive inclusion, and the seating idea’s feasibility is not evidence that residents prefer it.

Instructor note

Ask students to draw the sequence: idea, supported proposal, eligibility review, informed vote, implementation. Mark where a barrier enters. SUS-03 allocates a scarce existing court slot; this case examines who shapes the eligible choice set. HSP-10 protects hosts’ control over their contribution; this is neutral support for public proposal access. Do not collect real resident information, run a vote or promise a grant as part of the exercise.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All trust, residents, projects, sums and rules are original fiction. Project envelope HK$80,000 is separate from the additional HK$10,000 process allowance. Only one project is chosen; remaining project money stays with the trust. A gives four weeks of explanation, B gives two weeks of supported development plus two weeks of explanation. Both meet the stipulated two-week minimum; the process is not retroactively changed after votes. Existing duties and maintenance are separately funded. The listening session reveals a preparation barrier, not representative demand. Seating is technically eligible at HK$75,000 but not an entitlement to win. Five source bodies are institutional examples/guidance/evaluation; the two New York programmes have different scopes and are not conflated. No real civic action or data collection is authorised.

  1. Taipei City Government: participatory-budgeting appreciation event, 26 October 2024. Chinese-language official account describes proposal and review stages before voting. No claim that its process eliminates participation barriers.
  2. OECD Guidelines for Citizen Participation Processes: participatory-budgeting stages (2022). Relevant section distinguishes proposal, eligibility and voting stages and clear rules. Guidance, not a universal optimal timetable.
  3. Scottish Government: evaluation findings on participatory budgeting, 2016–2018. Actual evaluation discusses screening transparency, participation barriers and resource tradeoffs. Context-specific qualitative evidence, not a measured effect for this trust.
  4. New York City Council: participatory-budgeting process. Actual process separates idea collection, supported proposal development and voting. Its capital-project rules are not imported into this fictional fund.
  5. NYC Civic Engagement Commission: Borough Assemblies. Official process description distinguishes eligibility screening and ballot formation. Separate programme from Council capital budgeting; not a guarantee of inclusion or a rule for Hong Kong.

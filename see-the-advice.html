A promise of expertise. What can buyers see?

In the news

On 21 February 2024, Hong Kong’s Consumer Council released a study of home-renovation services. It highlighted difficulties consumers faced in understanding and comparing offers and recommended clearer quotations.

For a buyer unable to judge technical work in advance, a polished promise may still leave a basic question: what will I receive and understand after paying? The assessment service and campaign below are fictional, not the Council’s recommendations or a reported business.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. You run Plainview Advice, an independent Hong Kong home-repair assessment consultancy. Its paid visit records visible, non-urgent maintenance observations and explains next steps in plain language. It is not a structural survey, concealed-defect investigation, statutory inspection or repair contract. Matters outside its competence go to appropriate specialists.

Most new enquiries come from first-time users of an assessment-only service. You have HK$12,000 for one principal promotional asset on the same website and booking page for four weeks. Both production offers are feasible and fully funded. There is no additional asset budget or change in advertising placements; neither supplier promises more views, bookings or profit.

The same competent, lawful service, fee, booking capacity and required safety arrangements remain under either choice. Both make the same clear scope, exclusions, fee, complaints route and verified team credentials available before booking. The choice is the lead asset, not whether customers receive basic disclosures.

No real customer image or confidential report is used. The consultancy receives no contractor referral commission. No external certification or guaranteed diagnosis is claimed.

Your decision

Which lead asset would you commission, A or B? Name the uncertainty it addresses for a first-time customer and one important uncertainty it leaves unresolved.

Round 2 · More information

Exploratory interviews with eight adults who recently enquired about the service are completed. The interviewer does not show either asset or suggest a preferred answer. Six independently describe a similar worry: they might receive technical terms without understanding why a recommendation follows or what further work would be needed. The other two emphasise wanting to know who will enter their home.

These fictional interviews are qualitative clues, not a representative survey or a comparison of campaign effectiveness.

The checked B sample separates four things: what was observed; what it may mean; what this visit cannot establish; and what happens next. An ordinary visible mark is recorded without claiming its hidden cause is proven. It demonstrates the explanation, not a diagnostic technique for students to copy.

A remains accurate and approachable. Neither budget changes. B does not diagnose the viewer’s property, certify safety or promise a definite cause after every visit.

Your decision

Keep or change your choice? Explain how the interviews affect your priority without treating six of eight as a conversion forecast. Name one check that would tell you whether readers understand what the real service will and will not deliver.

Make the service imaginable

A service may be hard to judge before buying, but its outputs can be made visible. The worked example lets buyers examine how Plainview records observations, explains uncertainty and sets out next steps.

This is evidence of a communication process, not proof of universal competence or guaranteed repair. A polished sample must correspond to what actual customers receive.

Use the interviews without overclaiming

The expressed concern about explanations aligns directly with B’s preview. It gives a reason to prioritise B, not proof it will sell more. Six of eight interviews is not a population estimate, and nobody was assigned to compare the two campaigns.

A still addresses personal familiarity. Choosing it remains defensible if that is the manager’s priority, but the concern about understanding advice should not simply be ignored. There is no supplied demand or revenue forecast with which to calculate a winning financial return.

Evidence is not decoration

A smart office, uniform or elaborate tool may suggest professionalism without answering what the buyer needs to know. Equally, a technically impressive sample can confuse a beginner. Ask whether the evidence addresses the customer’s actual uncertainty.

Here B’s value is the distinction between observation, interpretation, limits and next action. It is not the visual complexity of the report. A’s value is approachable people, not credentials that B withholds: those remain available under both offers.

Check understanding and delivery

After viewing an asset, ask a prospective customer to explain the paid deliverable, identify an exclusion and distinguish a possible cause from a confirmed one. Use neutral questions rather than coaching the answer.

After real visits, check whether the delivered explanation matches what the campaign led customers to expect. Track those expectation gaps separately from clicks or bookings. More enquiries alone would not show that customers understood the offer.

Keep the promise bounded

The same fee, service and disclosure duties apply under both assets. A sample is neither a guarantee nor an external accreditation. It cannot quietly expand a limited assessment into a structural survey, statutory inspection or completed repair.

The real sources illustrate service uncertainty and scope communication. They do not endorse this fictional consultancy or establish the effectiveness of either campaign.

Instructor note

Allow two minutes for the hook and offer, three for the first choice, three for the reveal and revote, and four for limits and a comprehension check. Require a choice rather than an indefinite demand for more research.

Ask A supporters how an approachable team helps before purchase. Ask B supporters what a sample cannot prove and how to keep it understandable. Do not turn the exercise into selecting a larger-reach campaign, qualifying leads or providing technical inspection advice.

Both cost HK$12,000 for four weeks; no combined asset is funded. The eight interviews indicate concerns, not a conversion experiment. Both choices preserve truthful scope, credentials and required disclosures. Every classroom entity, amount and interview result is invented.

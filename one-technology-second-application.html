ENT-35 · Taiwan · 15–20 minutes · Technological redeployability · Modular design · Focused entry and future options

One technology. Keep a second application possible?

Focus the launch, assess the design flexibility

A venture can concentrate its launch on one market while preserving a limited technical route into another. Research on modular design asks whether the later choices enabled are worth the architecture’s extra cost. Flexibility does not create customers or remove qualification work. The venture, tooling, tests and cash amounts below are fictional.

Your classroom decision

You advise FormWeave, a fictional Taiwanese materials startup preparing cushioning inserts for reusable shipping packaging. Packaging is its fixed first market under both choices. The team must install prototype tooling now to meet its development calendar; delaying this initial investment or launching two markets at once is not an available route.

S costs NT$300,000 and fully specialises the prototype tooling for packaging. M costs NT$330,000 and adds a modular interface. Both produce the same packaging samples, on the same date, with the same quality, safety and operating cost. The NT$30,000 difference buys compatibility, not faster packaging sales.

The interface may support a later materials-development programme with protective-equipment makers. That programme would investigate a different cushioning application; it is not permission to sell finished protective equipment. No safety-performance claim, user trial or regulatory approval follows from packaging success. Technical feasibility of the second application is unresolved.

The current commercial team and funded launch effort remain entirely with packaging. Either tooling choice is affordable without using protected operating funds. Future packaging receipts and costs are identical between designs and excluded from this incremental comparison. M does not create a second sales team or require a second launch.

Before the test details arrive, the founders know M costs NT$30,000 more but cannot quantify the later opportunity or conversion bill. Which design would you provisionally support, and what evidence would justify the premium? “More applications” is not, by itself, a calculation of added value.

Round 2 · More information

Still before the initial tooling choice, an independent laboratory confirms a separately funded test will finish in four weeks. The test occurs under either design, uses existing specimens and does not divert the packaging team. It perfectly resolves the one specified compatibility uncertainty for this classroom model, not every commercial or safety uncertainty.

For the exercise only, assign a 40% probability to a pass and 60% to a fail. These are supplied planning assumptions, not laboratory evidence or an empirical success rate. A fail rules out the follow-on programme in this model; the firm then continues packaging without conversion.

After a pass, M can be converted for NT$60,000; S would require NT$180,000 of rework. Both conversions would enable the same bounded follow-on development programme. The quoted amounts include all technical adaptation, validation and required qualification work for that programme; neither includes a consumer-product launch.

If pursued after a pass, the follow-on programme produces NT$160,000 of net cash receipts before conversion cost, with all its other operating costs and taxes deducted. Treat this amount as certain conditional on a pass for arithmetic only. It is an assumed development-programme value, not proof that feasibility creates market demand.

The programme has separately available staff and funding after the test, with no reduction in packaging capacity, service or cash flows. It can be declined under either design. The conversion opportunity remains available until the result; there is no expiry problem, additional maintenance premium or salvage value in this comparison.

Management ranks the designs by expected incremental net cash relative to S, including the initial premium and whichever conversion it would actually choose after the result. Ignore interest and discounting over the bounded comparison. These assumptions isolate technical redeployability, not a full venture valuation or an instruction to fund a real project.

Your decision

Choose the initial design under the supplied objective and state what to do after a pass or fail. Include conversion costs for both designs. What pass probability justifies M’s premium, and why is this not a recommendation to launch both markets now?

Debrief · Separate focus from architecture

Packaging remains the first market under S and M. M changes the cost of a possible later technical adaptation, not the initial launch focus. Before the reveal, paying NT$30,000 on a vague possibility is not demonstrably worthwhile; nor does focused selling logically require making all future adaptation expensive.

The decision needs a defined compatible application, a useful test arriving before conversion, a realistic conversion cost, available later resources and value net of those costs. A modular label without these facts supplies no economic case. Here the reveal supplies them as explicit classroom assumptions.

Debrief · Solve the later choice first

After a pass, converting M gives NT$160,000 − NT$60,000 = NT$100,000 of incremental net cash. Declining gives zero, so convert under the stated objective. Under S, the same programme would give NT$160,000 − NT$180,000 = −NT$20,000; decline instead. Do not force an uneconomic conversion just to compare two active programmes.

After a fail, neither design converts and neither earns follow-on cash. The packaging programme continues unchanged. The initial premium is already spent by the test date and is not charged again in the conversion decision. It must nevertheless be included once when choosing the initial design.

Debrief · Compare complete design policies

Relative to S, M gains NT$100,000 after a pass before its initial premium, and zero after a fail. Subtract NT$30,000 in both branches: M’s incremental outcomes are NT$70,000 for a pass and −NT$30,000 for a fail. Their expected value is 0.40 × 70,000 + 0.60 × (−30,000) = NT$10,000.

Choose M under the supplied expected-net-cash criterion. Its gross expected later benefit is NT$40,000; the NT$30,000 architecture premium leaves NT$10,000 net advantage. That is not guaranteed cash or whole-company profit. There is a 60% stipulated chance that the extra design expenditure produces no follow-on benefit.

Debrief · Find the boundary

Let p be the pass probability, holding all other terms fixed. M’s incremental expected value is p × NT$100,000 − NT$30,000. The designs tie at p = 30%. M strictly leads above 30%; S strictly leads below 30%. At equality, neither design has an expected-cash advantage.

At the supplied 40%, the largest initial premium that would tie S is NT$40,000. This is an internal conditional threshold, not a market price or proof the current quotation is fair. If conversion costs, programme value, resource availability or compatibility change, recompute the exercise decision and comparison.

Debrief · Preserve an option without pretending to own a market

The interface buys a less costly path that management may decline. It does not buy customers, approvals or a guarantee that every protective-equipment application works. The exercise explicitly assumes a conditional programme value and funded qualification work; real teams must verify these rather than treat a laboratory pass as commercial validation.

Keep packaging’s sales plan intact and assign a review of the test result and programme conditions before any conversion. There is no second launch today. Preserving one bounded technical route is different from funding many speculative product teams or promising an unqualified safety function to customers.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–4: separate first-market focus from tooling design and list missing facts. Minutes 4–9: solve conversion after each result under both designs. Minutes 9–14: calculate the NT$10,000 incremental expectation and 30% threshold. Minutes 14–18: identify which supplied assumptions would require evidence in a real venture.

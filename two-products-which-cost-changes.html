ACC-21 · Fictional Hong Kong processor · 10–15 minutes · Joint-process costs · Further processing · Opportunity cost

Two products. Which cost changes?

One fruit, more than one saleable output

Citrus extraction produces more than juice. FAO’s fruit-processing guide discusses saleable peel-based outputs, while a 2003 Institute of Food Technologists article describes peel being separated from juice and undergoing additional operations. Those sources establish a real industrial setting, not today’s prices or the economics of a particular factory.

The classroom processor, offers and accounts below are original fiction. The question is modest: after one common process has finished, which use of its peel leaves the business better off?

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. You manage a small citrus processor. Today’s extraction run is complete: the juice has its own buyer, and 100 kg of separated peel is ready for sale. This point, where the outputs can be considered separately, is the split-off point. You must choose what happens to the entire peel lot before the collection deadline.

The production manager proposes drying the peel for an ingredient buyer. The controller objects: the internal product report assigns HK$2,000 of the common extraction cost to the peel, so the dried option appears to lose HK$300. The fresh-peel buyer offers less money but requires no additional processing.

The common run has already cost HK$12,000: the report assigns HK$10,000 to juice and HK$2,000 to peel. None can be recovered by changing the peel decision. Juice receipts and work are unaffected. No offer has been accepted and no drying cost committed. Both alternatives are feasible, meet all required quality and safety conditions, and finish within the same planning period. Processing capacity has no other paid use today; staffing and other fixed costs do not change. Choose either whole-lot offer, not a partial mix.

The HK$2,600 is for the complete dried output from the same 100 kg fresh lot, not for 100 kg of dried peel. Its lower dry weight is already reflected in the agreed whole-lot price. The HK$900 covers every additional payment for processing, energy, packing, required checks and handling. Both buyers collect; no extra selling charge applies.

Payment timing is identical under either choice; there are no other fees or taxes in this comparison.

Your decision

Would you sell the peel now or process the whole lot further? Show which receipts and costs change, and explain whether the allocated loss should determine your choice.

Round 2 · More information

Before you accept either offer, a second qualified buyer makes a firm HK$1,900 offer for the entire fresh-peel lot, collected on the same terms. The original offer can be declined without penalty. The dried-output buyer still offers HK$2,600; the full additional processing cost remains HK$900.

Nothing else changes: no processing has begun, the common extraction cost is still incurred, and the juice sale remains unaffected. This is new information available before commitment, not permission to abandon an agreed order.

Your decision

Before committing, would you keep or change your decision after the new fresh-peel offer? Calculate the difference and the fresh-peel offer at which the two options are equally attractive.

Draw a line at split-off

Before the line: the HK$12,000 common extraction cost. After the line: the peel buyer’s payment and any additional processing payment. An allocation divides a common amount between product reports; it does not create a new payment when you choose to dry this lot.

This is a decision about an already-produced lot, not whether to buy more oranges or operate the plant next month. Those earlier decisions would require their own whole-process analysis.

Process further initially: HK$2,600 − HK$900 − HK$1,200 = HK$500 more for the company. The HK$1,200 is the sale you give up, an opportunity cost. Count it once: either compare the two net amounts or subtract it in the incremental calculation, not both.

A loss label can hide the better choice

The controller’s arithmetic is correct on the report’s basis: HK$2,600 − HK$900 − HK$2,000 = a HK$300 assigned loss. But fresh sale gives HK$1,200 − HK$2,000 = an HK$800 assigned loss. Processing improves the result by HK$500 even though its assigned result remains negative.

Allocation is not inherently useless. Inventory reporting and product reporting can require a systematic allocation or another appropriate treatment of by-products. That reporting purpose is different from choosing between today’s offers. This simplified internal allocation is not presented as a required financial-reporting method.

Round two: the forgone sale has changed

Sell fresh at HK$1,900: drying still leaves HK$1,700, now HK$200 less. The incremental test becomes HK$2,600 − HK$900 − HK$1,900 = −HK$200. The recommendation changes because the alternative sale improved, not because the common cost changed.

The indifference offer is HK$2,600 − HK$900 = HK$1,700 for the whole fresh lot. Below that, drying earns more; above it, fresh sale earns more; at exactly HK$1,700 the cash comparison is tied under the stated assumptions.

Check the decision’s boundary

Before using this logic in a real business, confirm the sellable yield, buyer acceptance, extra payments, capacity alternatives and required controls. A higher finished selling price does not by itself establish extra profit. Nor does this one-lot calculation establish whether the whole juice operation is viable.

Both case choices use the peel. No environmental ranking or claim that drying is automatically greener follows from the cash figures. Real process design and regulatory requirements need separate assessment; checks are not optional cost savings.

Instructor note

Allow two minutes to read and vote, three to compare the cash consequences, two for the new offer and a second vote, and four for the debrief. Keep Round 2 and the worked sections hidden until their turn. Ask a student to draw extraction, then two branches: juice unchanged, peel sold or further processed.

Accept an initial choice only with its reasoning; the supplied cash assumptions favour processing by HK$500, then fresh sale by HK$200. Ask why “the report shows a loss” is insufficient, and why “the peel is free” is also wrong: its sale has value. Check that students do not subtract the forgone sale twice.

ACC-01 considers an earlier purchase and salvage; ACC-11 allocates a support-cost pool. This case instead places the decision at a common-process split-off and changes the outside offer while the allocation stays fixed. Do not turn the exercise into a debate over a fabricated optimal allocation. If discussing a whole new run, explicitly change the decision boundary and supply the missing juice and other cost evidence.

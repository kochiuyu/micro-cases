SUS-38 · Hong Kong buyer; fictional fishing areas · 15–20 minutes · Conservation leakage · Counterfactual boundaries · Seafood sourcing claims

A local success. A displaced pressure?

A purchase change is not the whole market response

A buyer can change its own sourcing without controlling what other buyers and fishers do next. Conservation analysis therefore examines responses beyond the immediate purchasing decision. The fictional scenario below distinguishes procurement, fishing activity and ecological outcomes; research on protected areas does not supply its invented market-response numbers.

Your classroom decision

Fictional Harbour Table, a Hong Kong restaurant group, stops buying one lawful, non-protected fish species from ecologically sensitive Area A next year. It previously bought forty tonnes annually there. It will buy zero from A and will not replace that volume with fish from elsewhere. Its funded menu change and existing obligations remain feasible.

For the teaching model, compare the same next year with and without the sourcing restriction. Without it, total catch of this species would be 100 tonnes in A and 100 tonnes in neighbouring Area B. Harbour Table accounts for forty of A’s tonnes; other buyers take the remaining sixty. All B catch goes to other buyers.

Neither area is legally closed and all assumed fishing remains within mandatory protections, lawful quotas and agreed worker safeguards. The buyer is setting a stricter voluntary sourcing policy, not licensing fishing or changing anyone’s legal rights. A is sensitive, but no numerical ecological-damage comparison between the two areas has been supplied.

Use fishing hours by the same standardised vessel-and-gear type as the effort measure. For this one-year illustration only, every tonne caught requires exactly ten fishing hours in either area. All catch is landed and sold; there are no discards. This fixed conversion is a simplifying assumption, not a biological law or a forecast.

The communications team proposes: “Our restriction removes forty tonnes of fishing and the corresponding pressure from the sea.” The sourcing lead prefers reporting only the forty-tonne procurement reduction until other buyers’ purchases and fishers’ responses are understood. Choose what can be claimed, not whether to abandon the already adopted sourcing restriction.

Round 2 · More information

A complete fictional response scenario is now supplied. Other purchasers buy twenty-five additional tonnes from A, over and above their original sixty. Harbour Table still buys zero there. A’s catch therefore does not fall by the full forty tonnes. No fish is merely relabelled as coming from B.

Of the effort no longer used in A, one hundred fishing hours move to B. Under the stipulated conversion, this adds ten tonnes to B’s original 100. The rest of the released A effort ceases for the year. These responses occur because of the restriction in this scenario; there are no other changes, areas or species affected within the model.

The two areas contain the entire fishing response for this classroom comparison. The buyer does not increase fishing demand elsewhere, and the non-seafood menu’s environmental effects are outside this fishery-only exercise. No wider life-cycle, habitat, bycatch or population-recovery assessment is available. The numbers are stipulated counterfactual scenarios, not measured causal effects or verified supplier forecasts.

The buyer can retain its restriction and propose a funded discussion with fishers, other buyers and responsible managers about monitoring and avoiding displaced pressure. No participant has agreed to change behaviour, and the buyer cannot impose a fishery-wide limit. Any additional programme needs lawful, informed participation and evaluation rather than an assumed environmental credit.

Your decision

Debrief · Trace the replacement demand

Other buyers now take 60 + 25 = 85 tonnes from A. Harbour Table buys zero, so A’s total catch is 85 tonnes, fifteen below its 100-tonne baseline. At ten hours per tonne, A effort is 850 hours, a reduction of 150. The buyer’s own forty-tonne reduction remains true.

The extra twenty-five tonnes are replacement demand by other purchasers. They are not twenty-five tonnes of additional catch on top of the original 100, nor proof that the buyer secretly kept purchasing. The intervention’s direct procurement change and the market’s response must be recorded separately.

Debrief · Follow the displaced effort

B receives one hundred additional fishing hours, raising effort from 1,000 to 1,100 and catch from 100 to 110 tonnes. Of the 150 hours released from A, one hundred move to B and fifty cease. Do not subtract the relocated hours twice or describe them as globally eliminated.

Across both areas, catch is 85 + 110 = 195 tonnes and effort is 850 + 1,100 = 1,950 hours. Relative to the without-restriction baseline, that is five fewer tonnes and fifty fewer hours. It is not forty fewer tonnes or four hundred fewer fishing hours.

Debrief · Quantify the limited offset

The direct procurement reduction is forty tonnes. Replacement demand offsets twenty-five; increased B catch offsets another ten. The remaining net reduction is 40 − 25 − 10 = 5 tonnes, or 12.5% of the direct reduction. The other 87.5% is offset in this invented scenario, not an estimated universal leakage rate.

The regional catch reduction is 5/200 = 2.5% of the two-area baseline. That denominator differs from five divided by forty. Keep the procurement, regional catch and fishing-hour comparisons labelled. None of these percentages measures biodiversity gained, fish saved from eventual capture or the probability of ecosystem recovery.

Debrief · A defensible statement

Use a conditional statement: “Our plan removes forty tonnes from our purchases in A. Under the specified one-year response scenario, catch across A and B is five tonnes lower and fishing effort fifty hours lower than without the restriction.” State that these are modelled comparisons, not verified outcomes.

Do not say the oceans are 2.5% healthier. Gear hours can be added as an activity measure under the supplied standardisation, but their ecological effects need not be equivalent across habitats. Moving activity away from A may matter differently from adding it in B; no damage weights or ecological response functions are provided.

Debrief · Incomplete effectiveness is not futility

The scenario retains a net reduction and a larger reduction inside A. It does not establish the full ecological benefit, but it also does not show that action is useless. Retain the restriction while investigating how complementary, lawful cooperation could reduce replacement demand or effort shifts. Do not promise that coordination will eliminate leakage.

A real evaluation needs traceable species and area records, other buyers’ volumes, vessel activity, effort displaced or retired, and a defensible without-intervention comparison. It also needs stock, habitat and non-target-species evidence. Account for changing markets, weather and management rather than attributing every before-and-after difference to one buyer.

Instructor note

Minutes 0–5: separate procurement from the proposed environmental claim. Minutes 5–10: reveal other buyers and effort relocation; reconcile both areas. Minutes 10–15: write a bounded statement and identify the missing ecological evidence. Ask whether fifty fewer hours necessarily means fifty equal units of ecological benefit.

SUS-24 relocates the same company activity outside a reporting boundary; SUS-27 tests ecological reversibility. Here independent actors respond to a procurement intervention, changing its net effect. All species status, permissions, baseline quantities, responses and conversion rates are fictional. No real fishery is certified, condemned, recommended or traded.

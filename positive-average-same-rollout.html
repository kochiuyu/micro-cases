IS-32 · Singapore · 15–20 minutes · Heterogeneous treatment effects · Prespecified groups · Rollout economics

A positive average. The same rollout for everyone?

An average is not a rollout policy

Randomised-evaluation guidance distinguishes an overall average effect from effects within groups defined before treatment. A rollout also depends on delivery costs and uncertainty, not just whether one interval excludes zero. The platform, experiment and costs below are original fiction.

Your classroom decision

You manage fictional Singapore venue-booking platform Room Lane. A reminder panel prompts consenting adult account holders to finish one pending booking. The control retains the usual page without that panel. Prices, access, cancellation rights and accessibility are unchanged. Neither version makes bookings automatically.

The completed trial randomly assigned 4,000 accounts, half to each version, within two usage groups defined before assignment. Each group contributed 1,000 reminder and 1,000 control accounts. Occasional means zero to two completed bookings in the preceding 90 days; frequent means three or more. The thresholds were fixed before outcomes existed.

Each account had one eligible draft and contributes one yes/no outcome: completed that booking within 14 days. Assignment and delivery are verified; outcomes are complete. No shared accounts, cross-version exposure, competing venue-capacity effects or other spillovers occur in this simplified trial. All assigned reminder accounts received the panel; booking remained voluntary.

The headline is 870 completions among 2,000 reminder accounts versus 800 among 2,000 controls: 43.5% versus 40.0%, a positive 3.5-percentage-point difference. An approximate 95% interval is +0.65 to +6.35 points. The two groups have the same 50/50 weight in both arms.

Before committing, you can open the prespecified group report and complete delivery quotes. Choose broad rollout, occasional-only delivery using the fixed baseline rule, or no rollout yet. All retain usual service, consent, privacy safeguards and a funded stop switch. A positive average does not by itself price these alternatives.

Round 2 · More information

The analysis plan named this single usage split and the reminder-by-group difference before assignment, anticipating that occasional users might need more prompting. No other cutoffs, segments, outcomes or interim stopping rules were searched. Group membership is baseline usage, not activity after seeing the reminder.

Within each group, each arm contains 1,000 accounts. Occasional users completed 280 bookings with reminders versus 200 without: 28% versus 20%. Frequent organisers completed 590 versus 600: 59% versus 60%. These four counts sum to the headline; no composition reversal or reassignment is involved.

The occasional effect is +8.0 percentage points, with approximate 95% interval +4.27 to +11.73. The frequent effect is −1.0 point, interval −5.30 to +3.30. The direct difference between effects, occasional minus frequent, is +9.0 points, interval +3.31 to +14.69. These are individual intervals, not simultaneous 95% coverage of all claims.

For the next 14-day cohort, 10,000 eligible accounts are planned: 5,000 in each fixed baseline group. Broad delivery costs S$1,000; occasional-only delivery costs S$1,400 because it needs extra rule validation. These all-in incremental quotes cover setup, delivery, monitoring and reversal for this cohort. No further fee or recurring commitment is included.

Each additional completed booking contributes S$10 after all booking-related variable costs, before these quoted feature costs. No-rollout incremental cost is zero. Both rollout paths are affordable. For a planning calculation only, apply the trial’s point estimates to this same-mix cohort; stable effects and unchanged capacity are assumptions, not established future outcomes.

Unwanted prompting could annoy frequent organisers, but the trial supplies no quantified nuisance measure or monetary loss. Do not invent one or label the −1-point estimate proof of annoyance. Safety, privacy and accessibility checks remain passed; future customer-experience monitoring must not be replaced by the booking metric.

Your decision

Which rollout would you recommend now? Reconcile the overall and group effects, compare the two point-estimate net contributions, and explain what the frequent-group interval and the cost difference leave uncertain.

Debrief · Keep the average, open the groups

Inspect the planned report and quotes before approval. The positive average supports an estimated benefit for this trial’s equal mix, not a universal gain. Occasional users show an estimated +8 points; frequent users show −1. Their equally weighted average is 0.5 × 8 + 0.5 × (−1) = +3.5 points.

Unlike a composition reversal, both arms have identical group weights. Random assignment within each group supports estimating reminder effects there. It does not show that making someone a frequent organiser changes their response, or that every occasional user benefits. Group averages do not reveal individual causal effects.

Debrief · Compare effects directly

The interaction is 8 − (−1) = 9 percentage points. Its supplied interval is above zero, supporting a difference between group-average effects under the model. Do not infer that difference merely because one group’s interval excludes zero and the other’s does not: those are separate questions.

The frequent interval spans harm, zero and benefit. Failure to demonstrate a positive effect is not proof of no effect, equivalence or nuisance. Prespecification makes the comparison more credible but does not remove sampling uncertainty, model assumptions or the need to consider whether effects transfer to another cohort.

A 95% confidence interval describes a procedure’s long-run coverage under its assumptions, not a 95% probability assigned to this fixed effect or a range containing 95% of users’ responses. These approximate intervals are supplied for interpretation; they are not a joint guarantee or a forecast interval for next month’s bookings.

Debrief · Price the two policies

Broad rollout projects 5,000 × 0.08 = 400 additional occasional bookings and 5,000 × (−0.01) = −50 frequent bookings relative to usual service: net 350. The negative term means fewer completions than the counterfactual, not negative actual bookings. Projected incremental contribution is 350 × S$10 − S$1,000 = S$2,500.

Occasional-only delivery projects 400 additional bookings, none induced among untreated frequent users, and S$4,000 − S$1,400 = S$2,600 net. This is S$100 above broad delivery on the point estimates, despite its S$400 extra implementation cost. No rollout defines the zero incremental baseline; these are uncertain projections, not realised receipts.

Debrief · Find the decision boundary

The policies treat occasional users identically. If the true frequent-group effect is d as a proportion, broad minus targeted net contribution is 5,000 × d × S$10 + S$400. They tie at d = −0.008, or −0.8 percentage points. Broad wins above that value; targeted wins below it, under these cost assumptions.

The frequent estimate of −1 point slightly favours targeting, but its interval crosses the −0.8-point decision boundary widely. Evidence that group effects differ is not proof that targeting is economically superior. Even zero frequent effect would favour broad delivery by S$400 because targeting costs more.

Debrief · Recommend without overstating

A reversible occasional-only rollout is a defensible cautious preference: it focuses on the clearer estimated benefit, with a small point-estimate net advantage. Broad delivery is defensible if simplicity and the uncertain frequent benefit justify exposure. Neither policy is established as best; no rollout is defensible if management will not accept this uncertainty.

Record the chosen policy and tradeoff. Preserve the baseline rule, consent and stop switch; monitor booking completion and unwanted prompting. Seek a prospectively designed follow-up focused on frequent users and the economic boundary before permanent expansion. Its sample size, cost and outcome are not supplied, so do not promise a free or conclusive retest.

Instructor note

Minutes 14–18: debate a reversible policy without calling nonsignificance zero or the S$100 point-estimate edge certain.

Stratified random assignment, complete outcomes, verified delivery, no interference or capacity displacement. Group definition, outcome and interaction specified before assignment. Effects use risk differences; approximate individual 95% normal intervals use unpooled binomial variances, with equal fixed group weights for the overall effect and summed independent variances for the interaction. No simultaneous coverage claimed. Next-cohort effects and contribution projections assume transportability; costs are complete for one cohort, with no recurring commitment. Group membership is not itself randomised here; the case does not identify its causal effect.

Used only for general statistical cautions, not medicine or a regulatory requirement for this platform.

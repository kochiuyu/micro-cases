STR-05 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Strategy · Acquisition rationale · Synergies

Two hundred clients. Two hundred new clients?

In the news · Combining capabilities

On 28 July 2023, Vistra announced completion of its merger with Tricor. The announcement described a Hong Kong-headquartered combined business and emphasised complementary capabilities, broader services and geographic reach.

Those were the companies’ stated strategic reasons, not independent proof that all expected benefits were achieved. The fictional consultancy below raises a related question: what makes a combination more valuable than simply owning two existing businesses? Its customer figures and proposal are invented; they imply no error by Vistra or Tricor.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. You advise a Hong Kong design consultancy with 300 active business clients. It could acquire a translation studio with 200 active business clients. Both counts use the same completed twelve-month period and define an active client as a distinct business that bought at least one service; duplicates within each list have been removed.

The proposal highlights complementary skills and a ready-made customer base: “Buying the studio could give us a much bigger platform for growth.” A colleague favours developing a service partnership first, while the consultancy grows its own client relationships.

Acquisition could bring the teams under one management, but would require a purchase price and integration work. A partnership might preserve flexibility but provide less control over shared delivery. Today you are choosing which growth route deserves further investigation, not approving a purchase. Price, client retention and integration costs are not yet supplied.

Design consultancy300

Your decision

Would you prioritise investigating the acquisition or a service partnership? What benefit would justify owning the studio rather than working alongside it?

Round 2 · One fact changes

An authorised review reports that 150 of the studio’s 200 clients are already clients of the design consultancy. Students receive only this aggregate result; no real client identities or records are involved.

The service capabilities and total clients of each business are unchanged. These shared clients already bought something from both businesses during the same period. The review does not establish what they will buy next year, whether they will stay after a deal, or whether they want a combined service.

Your decision

How many unique clients are in the combined current base, and how many are new to the buyer’s base? Would you change your preferred route or the benefit you would investigate?

Count reach without erasing the business

The combined current base contains 300 + 200 − 150 = 350 unique clients. The studio brings 200 − 150 = 50 clients new to the consultancy’s current base, not 200.

That does not mean the other 150 clients or their translation purchases have no value. The studio still has its own services, skills and existing business. Customer overlap is not revenue duplication: a shared client can pay separately for design and translation. Nor does a new-to-buyer client automatically become a buyer of design services.

Separate existing value from additional value

Synergy is additional value made possible by combining the businesses, beyond what they would generate separately. Simply adding their existing sales is not evidence of that extra value.

Shared clients might benefit from coordinated multilingual design, fewer handoffs or a genuinely improved offer. But clients already buying both services cannot be counted again as new cross-sales merely because ownership changes. Ask which new purchases or cost savings would occur because of the combination, and what delivering them would cost.

Overlap could help coordination or reduce the claimed expansion in reach. It is evidence to refine the rationale, not an automatic verdict for or against acquisition.

Ask what requires ownership

If the main benefit is referrals, investigate whether an appropriately designed partnership could deliver it. If the benefit needs common scheduling, shared investment and accountable delivery, explain why ownership would enable those better than a contract. Neither route is costless or guaranteed to work.

Check customer needs and likely retention, staff willingness to stay, delivery responsibilities and integration capacity. Estimate additional benefits separately from the studio’s existing business value. Then compare the purchase price and integration burden with the value the buyer can realistically retain.

A sound business can still be an unattractive acquisition at the wrong price. A modest increase in customer count can still accompany a valuable combination. No price or final investment recommendation follows from these counts.

Instructor note

Allow two minutes for the first choice, one for the overlap calculation, then six for revising the acquisition rationale. Ask an acquisition supporter what common ownership changes. Ask a partnership supporter which coordination problem a contract may fail to solve.

The objective is corporate-strategy value creation, not merely a set calculation or a recommendation that acquisitions fail. Distinguish unique clients, existing revenue, additional sales and value retained after price and integration. A strong answer can keep its initial route while replacing a weak justification.

ECO-25 · Hong Kong · 15–20 minutes · Durable goods · Pricing commitment · Strategic waiting

Buy today. Wait for tomorrow’s discount?

Tomorrow’s price can change today’s demand

Durable-goods pricing research studies a seller whose future offers compete with its current offer. Buyers may postpone a purchase when expected savings outweigh the value of earlier use. A plan to keep prices high is not necessarily credible when the seller later wants to reach the buyers still waiting.

This two-period equipment launch is original fiction, not a report about a real company. It compares two specified policies, not every possible price path. Research supplies the mechanism and its limits; all quantities, values and implementation assumptions below are invented.

Your classroom decision

You manage a fictional Hong Kong seller launching one durable desktop binding machine to a closed group of 120 small businesses. Each business wants at most one machine. Period 1 is this month; period 2 is next month, the final purchase opportunity in the exercise. Buying earlier provides extra use, but the physical product is identical. No new buyers arrive; there are no competing sellers, resale, rental, repeat purchases, stockouts or later model upgrades in this simplified market.

The launch price is already fixed at HK$1,000 for period 1. You must now choose A: a credible upfront commitment to keep HK$1,000 in period 2, or B: discretion to choose either HK$1,000 or HK$600 in period 2 after observing sales. These are the only authorised prices. Under A, an independently administered sales system locks both periods’ price and prevents side discounts; this restriction is effective, visible to buyers and has no incremental cost in the exercise. Under B, buyers know that management will choose the more profitable of the two later prices. A mere announcement is not the same as the lock.

Each unit sold incurs HK$200 in avoidable production and fulfilment cost, regardless of purchase month; unsold units are not produced. Fixed costs are unchanged and excluded. There are no additional fees, financing, tax or commitment-cost differences. Compare total contribution, revenue minus these variable costs. Buyers maximise their monetary-equivalent benefit, may decline for a payoff of zero, and have no budget constraints. The table gives total value if bought now, not a recurring monthly value. Waiting costs and buyer expectations have not yet been validated.

Your decision

Would you lock the two-period price or preserve markdown discretion? Give a provisional choice and identify the buyer information needed before counting 80 full-price sales plus 40 discounted sales.

Round 2 · More information

Buyer interviews now establish the following exact behaviour for the classroom model. The higher-value businesses lose HK$300 of early-use value if they wait one month; the lower-value businesses lose HK$50. Their remaining total values from a period-2 purchase are therefore HK$900 and HK$650 respectively. These losses capture all waiting inconvenience and foregone use; do not subtract them again from the period-2 values. There is no additional monetary discounting. A business that never buys receives zero, not a negative waiting charge.

All buyers know these values, the two price policies, the guaranteed availability and the seller’s contribution objective. They anticipate the seller’s actual period-2 choice, rather than treating the markdown as a surprise. The facts do not change the launch price or expand the permitted price menu. You have not yet selected A or B, so you can revise the provisional choice without breaking a promise.

Your decision

Under each policy, when will each group buy and what total contribution will the seller earn? Check that the discretionary period-2 price is sensible for the buyers still waiting, then explain whether the seller’s preferred policy also gives buyers the largest surplus.

Start with the missing comparison

At the opening, the table alone does not establish the timing of purchases. The tempting calculation is 80 × (1,000 − 200) + 40 × (600 − 200) = HK$80,000. That assumes higher-value buyers purchase early even though they can wait. It is a hypothetical non-strategic benchmark, not a feasible forecast until their timing incentive is checked. A defensible first answer can favour flexibility or commitment provisionally, but must ask about lost early use, credible expectations and availability.

Work backwards from the last month

Under discretion, any remaining higher-value buyer values a period-2 machine at HK$900 and any remaining lower-value buyer at HK$650. At HK$1,000 neither type buys. At HK$600 either type remaining buys with positive surplus, contributing HK$400 per unit. Therefore, whenever any buyers remain, the seller selects HK$600 rather than earning zero at HK$1,000. This remains true if one higher-value buyer changes their own timing. The markdown is a best response within the stated two-price menu, not an unexplained promise.

Let buyers anticipate that choice

A higher-value buyer gets HK$1,200 − HK$1,000 = HK$200 from buying now. Waiting for HK$600 gives HK$900 − HK$600 = HK$300. All 80 wait. A lower-value buyer would get −HK$300 now, but HK$650 − HK$600 = HK$50 later, so all 40 also wait and then buy. Policy B sells zero in period 1 and 120 in period 2. Revenue is 120 × HK$600 = HK$72,000; variable cost is 120 × HK$200 = HK$24,000; total contribution is HK$48,000.

What the price lock changes

With policy A, higher-value buyers compare HK$200 now with −HK$100 from a later HK$1,000 purchase, or zero from declining. All 80 buy now. Lower-value buyers decline in both periods because either purchase gives negative surplus. Revenue is HK$80,000 and variable cost is HK$16,000, giving HK$64,000 contribution. Commitment beats discretion by HK$16,000 for this seller, despite selling 40 fewer units. It makes the high future price credible and changes current purchases; it does not create extra product quality.

More contribution is not more buyer surplus

Under A, buyer surplus totals 80 × HK$200 = HK$16,000. Under B, it is 80 × HK$300 + 40 × HK$50 = HK$26,000, including the lost early-use value already reflected in later valuations. Buyers collectively prefer B in this model. Seller contribution plus buyer surplus is HK$80,000 under A and HK$74,000 under B. These narrow total-surplus figures differ because waiting loses use value while allowing 40 extra purchases. They are not a complete social-welfare assessment or a reason to equate the seller’s objective with every stakeholder’s interest.

The boundary of the recommendation

Choose A for the stated contribution objective and these two policies. Do not conclude that sellers should never discount, that all durable-goods markets collapse to cost, or that HK$1,000 is the globally optimal launch price. New price choices, uncertain demand, stockout risk, resale, entrants, different waiting costs and costly or weak commitment can change the result. The case concerns the same product over time, not complementary consumables or later running charges. Before applying the idea, validate willingness to pay and the credibility and cost of the actual commitment mechanism.

Instructor note

Allow 3 minutes for the opening choice, 6 minutes for the reveal calculations, and 6 minutes for comparison and challenge. Ask students to show each group’s buy-now, buy-later and no-purchase payoffs before computing seller contribution. Keep the hypothetical HK$80,000 mixed-sales benchmark separate from the HK$80,000 revenue under A and HK$80,000 total surplus under A: equal numbers do not mean equal concepts.

Common errors are treating today’s willingness to pay as later remaining value, subtracting waiting loss twice, counting a repeat purchase, treating revenue as contribution, or assuming a future announcement binds management. The restricted price menu is essential. If students propose HK$900 or another price, welcome it as a new model, not a permitted move in the base exercise. No optimisation over all price paths or general Coase-conjecture proof is required. Optional extension: a real incremental commitment cost would reduce A’s contribution advantage; HK$16,000 is the break-even extra cost holding every other assumption fixed.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

Original fictional exercise: all businesses, machine, counts, values, prices, costs, timing, buyer knowledge and commitment technology are invented. No empirical demand estimate or real seller is represented. Unit cost is avoidable and incurred only on sale; fixed costs unchanged. The price menu is explicitly restricted and period-1 price fixed, so results compare policies, not globally optimal prices. Waiting loss is foregone early use already embedded in remaining value, not a cash charge. No-buy payoff zero. Five substantive public primary-source texts were read on 16 November 2026 using targeted public access, without library proxy or systematic downloading. Independent review, art, integration and live QA are separate gates.

  1. Glenn Ellison / MIT OpenCourseWare · Monopoly Pricing and Durable Goods, Part I · Fall 2022. Slides 8–15 explain two-period commitment, buyer anticipation and limits of the Coase conjecture. Conceptual foundation; this case uses original discrete groups and a restricted price menu, not the lecture’s continuous-demand calibration.
  2. Elmar Wolfstetter / Humboldt University · Monopoly · sections on time inconsistency and durable-goods monopoly. The substantive chapter explains why a seller may want to depart from an earlier plan and why forward-looking buyers alter purchase timing. Public repository text has imperfect numeric extraction; no extracted formulas or publication date are relied upon.
  3. Joel Sobel · Durable Goods Monopoly with Entry of New Consumers · Econometrica 59(5), 1991, 1455–1485. Introduction and model show the importance of new-buyer entry, resale restrictions and equilibrium assumptions. Used to bound the lesson, not to import its infinite-horizon results into the two-period classroom market.
  4. Gérard P. Cachon and Robert Swinney · The Value of Fast Fashion · Management Science 57(4), 2011, 778–795. Section 3.1 models waiting as lost consumption value and includes stockout risk and uncertain demand. A contrasting seasonal-goods model, not evidence about Hong Kong equipment buyers or a universal case for price commitment.
  5. Gerardo Berbeglia, Peter Sloan and Adrian Vetta · The Effect of a Finite Time Horizon in the Durable Good Monopoly Problem with Atomic Consumers · public preprint. Introduction, model and two-period example show why finite horizons and discrete buyers matter, and reason backwards from remaining demand. The PDF carries an arXiv v3 stamp dated 2016 and a title-page date of 2018; cited as the accessed preprint, not as a verified journal version.

IS-13 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Aggregation reversal · Standardised comparisons · Decision dashboards

Better in both groups. Worse overall?

Industry context

On 27 February 2026, Hong Kong’s Census and Statistics Department released its report on business use of information technology in 2025. The release reported overall figures and differences by company size and industry.

Those published differences are not evidence of an aggregation reversal. They illustrate why a headline and its underlying groups answer different questions. The print-service teams, records and scheduling decision below are original fiction, not findings about any surveyed business.

Your classroom decision

A fictional Hong Kong print-service business prepares routine customer files for production. Two teams, Harbour and Hill, each handled 100 jobs last month. Harbour completed 66 by the agreed file-ready deadline; Hill completed 74. Every assigned job appears once, all deadlines have passed, and the same definition of on-time completion is used. There are no missing records or data-entry errors. The measure concerns file preparation, not print quality or customer safety.

The scheduling manager is considering changing how next month’s work is distributed. The analyst must choose the main comparison in the briefing: A, the actual overall rates for the work each team received, or B, a comparison giving both teams the same proportions of simple and complex jobs. Both versions retain the original totals and disclose their purpose. No staffing, pay or customer commitment changes automatically follow from the report.

Simple and complex categories were defined consistently from job specifications before team assignment and before outcomes were known. The teams did not choose their categories. Dispatchers assigned work operationally rather than randomly; other job and shift differences may remain within a category. The detail table can be opened before the final briefing. The upcoming booked portfolio contains equal numbers of simple and complex jobs, so the proposed reference mix is 50% of each. This is a reporting comparison, not proof of what either team would achieve if its work changed.

Which comparison would you lead with for the scheduling discussion: actual overall rates or a common 50/50 work mix? Explain what 66% and 74% establish, and what they do not establish about moving work.

Your decision

Keep or change your headline comparison? Calculate both rates at the common 50/50 mix, explain the reversal without calling either observed rate wrong, and state one limitation before recommending a scheduling change.

Rebuild the headline

Harbour’s overall rate is 0.20 × 90% + 0.80 × 60% = 66%. Hill’s is 0.80 × 80% + 0.20 × 50% = 74%. These are accurate summaries of what happened to each team’s actual portfolio. Hill received a much larger share of the category with higher observed completion rates.

Give the groups the same weight

At a common 50/50 mix, Harbour’s descriptive rate is 0.50 × 90% + 0.50 × 60% = 75%; Hill’s is 0.50 × 80% + 0.50 × 50% = 65%. The 10-percentage-point difference compares the recorded category rates using identical weights. These are standardised summaries, not new observed counts or guaranteed forecasts of 75 and 65 completed jobs.

Two answers to two questions

The raw rates answer: what share of each actual portfolio was on time? The standardised rates answer: how do the recorded category rates compare when given the same mix? The ranking reverses because the original weights differ. This is an example of Simpson-type aggregation reversal. Equal total job counts do not mean equal composition. Neither summary should erase the other.

Do not turn adjustment into causation

For the proposed common-mix comparison, lead with 75% and 65%, clearly labelled, and retain 66% and 74% beside the group counts. That is more informative than shifting work to Hill simply because its overall rate was higher. But it does not establish that moving any job to Harbour would improve its outcome. Operational assignment, within-category difficulty, shifts and workload may differ. Changing the workload could itself change performance. Capacity and costs are also absent, so the table cannot optimise a schedule.

Instructor note

Ask students to vote on the briefing headline, then reconstruct the four cell rates and the two weighted averages. Let a student defend the raw rate for describing the actual service portfolio; that answer is valid for that question. The common mix is appropriate for the stated descriptive comparison, not universally the right adjustment in every causal problem. The categories here precede assignment and are not outcomes of the teams’ work, but that alone does not remove every confounder. A responsible next step is a limited operational review of comparable job specifications, shifts, capacity and assignment rules before reallocating work. Do not invent significance levels, a causal effect or an optimal staffing plan. No external reading is needed.

Sources and case assumptions · optional reference

All information needed for classroom decisions is provided above. Sources are optional reading for attribution and background.

All teams, jobs, records and decisions are original classroom fiction. Harbour has 18 of 20 simple jobs and 48 of 80 complex jobs on time, giving 66 of 100 overall. Hill has 64 of 80 simple jobs and 10 of 20 complex jobs on time, giving 74 of 100 overall. The recorded category rates are 90% and 60% versus 80% and 50%. A common reference mix of 50% simple and 50% complex gives descriptive standardised rates of 75% and 65%, a 10-percentage-point difference. The original overall difference is 8 percentage points in Hill’s favour. The reference mix comes from the stipulated upcoming booked portfolio, not optimisation or a selected favourable denominator. These calculations are exact for the fictional records; no sampling or forecasting precision is asserted. Categories are consistently defined from specifications before assignment and outcome. Assignment was not random, and categories may omit important differences. Every job appears once, with no missingness, selection from unreturned surveys, duplicated records or stale data. No employee-level selection, pay or access decisions are made. Distinct from IS-10 experimental interference, IS-11 survey nonresponse, IS-12 missing durations and ACC flexible-budget comparisons: this is a reversal in complete observed rates caused by differing category weights. Five substantive source bodies were read on 15 November 2026. The Hong Kong business survey release is context, not a demonstration of reversal. The Hong Kong health table and UK ONS guidance demonstrate explicitly defined standardisation outside business; no health conclusions or shared standard population are assumed. The two original-author research papers were read in relevant conceptual sections, not cover to cover. No source tables, figures or example numbers were reproduced.

The landlord pays. Who saves?

In the news · A landlord–tenant partnership

On 29 July 2024, Swire Properties reported on its Green Performance Pledge, a landlord–tenant collaboration covering office fit-out and operation in Hong Kong.

This company account illustrates collaboration, not independent proof of savings or the terms of our fictional small-building example.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. A small Hong Kong commercial landlord owns the fixed lighting in an office. The tenant pays its separately metered electricity bill. Three years remain on the lease.

The current lights are safe and serviceable. This is a voluntary efficiency upgrade, not an overdue repair. A qualified assessment confirms that the proposed equipment can meet the same lighting and safety requirements. It would remain the landlord’s property.

Both parties can afford the upgrade. But the current arrangement gives the landlord no share of the direct bill savings, and neither party can change the lease or charge the other unilaterally. They want to reduce electricity use without ignoring who pays.

Current proposalAmount or responsibility
Complete three-year quote: purchase, qualified installation, agreed monitoring and incremental servicingHK$24,000, paid upfront by landlord
Assessor’s forecast annual electricity-bill savingHK$12,000, received by tenant
Remaining lease3 years
Existing cost-recovery agreementNone

The agreed installation schedule causes no extra closure cost. For the arithmetic, use three equal years with no lease break or default; ignore discounting, tax, financing and any residual, sale-value or rent increase benefit. The savings figure is a forecast at stated hours and tariff, not a measured result or guarantee.

Your decision

Would you fund the upgrade on the current terms, seek a voluntary sharing agreement before commissioning, or defer? Propose an arrangement the tenant might accept and say what would change your recommendation.

Round 2 · One fact changes

The tenant is willing in principle to pay the landlord 75% of verified positive bill savings for the remaining three years, keeping 25%. The landlord would fund the HK$24,000 upfront. No verified saving means no savings payment, not a fixed charge based on the forecast.

They propose an agreed pre-upgrade baseline, meter records and adjustments for changed operating hours and tariff, with both parties able to inspect the calculations. The assessor confirms that the meter and records can support this method. Monitoring is already included in the quote; the annual saving has not yet been achieved.

They would seek an appropriately reviewed written arrangement with both parties’ consent. Nothing is signed or installed. Consider both the HK$12,000 annual forecast and a lower scenario of HK$8,000 annual savings. The lower figure is a classroom sensitivity case, not a prediction or assigned probability.

Your decision

Would you proceed to final agreement on the 75/25 basis, change the split, or defer? Compare both annual savings scenarios and name one condition that belongs in the agreement.

A benefit for the pair is not a return to each party

Under the original terms, three years of forecast bill savings total HK$36,000. After the HK$24,000 upgrade, the pair’s combined forecast net benefit is HK$12,000.

But the landlord pays HK$24,000 and receives none of those direct savings, while the tenant receives HK$36,000. Calling the combined HK$12,000 the landlord’s return hides the problem. Funding alone could still be an environmental or relationship choice, but the unrecovered outlay must be acknowledged.

Make the proposed allocation visible

If HK$12,000 is saved each year, the three-year total is HK$36,000. The landlord receives 75%, or HK$27,000, leaving HK$3,000 after the upgrade cost. The tenant keeps HK$9,000. Their combined net benefit remains HK$12,000.

If only HK$8,000 is saved each year, the total is HK$24,000. The landlord receives HK$18,000 and is HK$6,000 short of its outlay; the tenant keeps HK$6,000. Combined net benefit is zero.

The payment rule reallocates the savings; it does not create more electricity savings. Do not add the sharing payments to the total bill saving a second time.

A possible bargain still carries performance risk

The proposal gives the landlord a route to recovery and leaves the tenant some benefit. It can support moving toward agreement if both accept the allocation and risk. The lower scenario shows why the landlord may instead seek different terms or defer.

Payment only from verified positive savings limits the tenant’s payment exposure but does not guarantee the landlord recovers its investment. A different split or voluntary upfront contribution can be considered; neither can be imposed, and a longer term cannot be assumed without agreement.

Agree how the result will be checked

Specify the baseline, adjustments, access to records and payment calculation. Clarify responsibility for servicing and monitoring, disputed figures, changed use and an early exit. Those practical terms matter even when both want the upgrade.

Track electricity use and lighting quality alongside the bill. A tariff change can alter money without an equivalent change in energy use. No emission factor is supplied, so the case does not establish a carbon saving or carbon-neutral outcome.

Instructor note

Draw an arrow from landlord to installer for the outlay and one to the tenant for the bill savings. Add the voluntary sharing arrow only after the reveal. The issue is the distribution of costs and benefits, not another payback calculation or a price assigned to environmental harm.

Keep forecast, agreed measurement method and future verified result separate. Suitable records do not establish that the forecast will occur. The two scenarios have no supplied probabilities, so no expected-value calculation is warranted.

Accept a clearly explained alternative bargain or a decision not to proceed. The excluded rent, residual and other benefits isolate the classroom problem; they are not claims that real landlords never benefit in other ways. Actual lease rights, finance, equipment and protections need appropriate professional review. No contract is being signed through this exercise.

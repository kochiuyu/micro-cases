PPL-11 · Hong Kong · 10–15 minutes · Performance appraisal · Recency bias · Attribution

A strong finish. A strong year?

In the news

In its 2024 Annual Report, Hong Kong’s Public Service Commission discussed over-generous appraisal reports and called for fair, candid assessments justified by facts and observations.

The issue is not to make every rating lower, but to support the judgment. The private service company, employee and review rules below are fictional; civil-service procedures do not govern this exercise.

Your classroom decision

Fictional classroom situation. You manage a Hong Kong business-services helpdesk. Tomorrow you will discuss Kai’s January–December development review with Kai. The job stayed the same throughout the year: resolve assigned enquiries accurately, communicate next steps and support colleagues.

The agreed rubric considers the whole year and assigned work. Meets expectations means dependable delivery of those responsibilities. Exceeds expectations means documented contributions above the normal role standard across more than one part of the year, while core responsibilities remain dependable. One visible success alone is insufficient. The agreed rubric requires judgment about the significance, scope and continuity of above-standard contributions across the year. Contributions in two periods are necessary but do not automatically qualify for the higher rating. There is no quota limiting the higher rating.

You must prepare a provisional rating and reasons, then hear Kai’s account before finalising it. This classroom review is for development: no pay, promotion or dismissal decision is attached. Relevant facts may be corrected; the rating is not a promise already made.

The summary covers all four quarters. A detailed dated work log exists, but its context has not yet been included in this summary. Kai has no known quality or conduct failure. Raw completion counts are not an agreed standalone target.

Your decision

Would you provisionally rate Kai Meets or Exceeds expectations for this year? Cite evidence from more than the final quarter and name the context you would check before finalising.

Round 2 · More information

The dated log shows Kai was assigned the team’s multi-party enquiries in January–June. These needed coordination across several organisations. Kai met the agreed milestones and quality standards; the lower count was not evidence of neglected work. Handling these assignments was part of the normal role, not automatically above it.

In March and June, Kai also created two practical handover guides, beyond the standard case notes. Colleagues used them and recorded that the next responsible person and outstanding action became clearer. The log documents adoption, but no measured time saving. Whether these contributions merit the higher rubric is a judgment, not a numerical threshold invented afterward.

The December project log credits a team of four. Kai coordinated client updates reliably; two colleagues developed the sorting method and another checked difficult cases. The client named Kai because Kai communicated with them. Kai genuinely contributed, but did not deliver the project alone.

The year’s criteria and available ratings are unchanged. These are contextual facts about existing work, not new achievements after the review period.

Your decision

Keep or change your provisional rating? Explain the weight you give the earlier guides, difficult assignments and shared December success. Write one specific recognition and one development discussion point for Kai.

Review the year, not the most memorable month

December is easy to recall because the project was visible and a client named Kai. It deserves attention, but cannot replace January–September. Recency bias is giving recent events more weight simply because they are easier to remember.

Reading a full-year table is only a start. Relevant context may be absent even when all four quarters appear.

Separate contribution from visibility

Kai’s client coordination was real work. The thank-you message does not establish sole responsibility for the project outcome. Credit the specific contribution without taking colleagues’ work away.

Similarly, fewer completed enquiries do not prove weak effort when assignments differ. The log supports dependable delivery of more complex work, not a precise numerical adjustment or automatic superior rating.

Two defensible judgments, with different reasons

Exceeds can be defended using the March and June guides as documented beyond-standard contributions in separate parts of the year, alongside dependable core work. Do not base it solely on the December praise.

Meets can be defended if the manager judges the two guide contributions too limited in scope or continuity to demonstrate sustained above-standard contribution. Explain that interpretation against the agreed rubric and apply it consistently; do not erase the contribution or invent a new numerical cutoff.

The evidence changes the reasons for a rating even if it does not change its label. A lower rating is not automatically less biased.

Turn the judgment into useful feedback

Recognition might say: “Your March and June guides made the next owner and action clearer for colleagues.” It describes an observed contribution without claiming measured savings.

A development discussion could ask how Kai would test whether those guides work across other enquiry types, with agreed paid time and support before setting any new goal. This is not proof Kai failed this year or a demand for unpaid additional work.

Invite Kai to correct the factual account and explain the work. Listening is compatible with managerial judgment; agreement is not guaranteed.

Keep a better record next year

Agree expectations at the start, record concrete observations across the year and discuss them during regular reviews. Record work difficulty, available resources and shared responsibilities rather than relying on the latest headline.

Do not turn every helpful action into surveillance or pretend a richer log eliminates all judgment. Evidence should be proportionate, relevant and available for correction.

Instructor note

Allow three minutes for the rubric and first vote, four for the reveal and revote, and five for a reasoned appraisal conversation. Both ratings must reference the same whole-year standard. Ask students who retain their label whether their reasoning changed.

This is appraisal in the same job, not PPL-03’s selection for a different supervisory role. It is not a forced ranking, a reward for a single team outcome or a formula using case counts. No statistical adjustment, causal effect or real personnel decision is claimed.

Watch for replacing one bias with another: overcrediting a public success, ignoring invisible earlier work, or assuming every hard assignment exceeds expectations. Keep legitimate recognition separate from an unsupported higher rating.
